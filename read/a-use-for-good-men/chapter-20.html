Chapter Twenty

The Visit

Sam filled the travel mug until coffee touched the underside of the lid. He tipped a little into the sink and tried again.

At the dining table, Laura put Sarah on speaker.

“I gave the campus officer the photograph,” Sarah said. “The one of me, and the one Mom took of the car. He said to call if I see the man or if anyone asks to meet me because they know you.”

Sam brought the mug to the table. “Did he give you his name?”

“Yes, Dad. And the number.”

“Put it in your phone.”

“It is in my phone.”

Laura moved the phone nearer him. “Sarah, tell him what you and Tessa agreed.”

“We’re walking back from the afternoon lab together. She knows what happened. I told the residence adviser too. I’m not doing this on my own.”

“Good,” Laura said.

Sam rested both hands around the mug. They had shown Sarah the photograph the previous afternoon. She remembered the Wednesday it was taken, the class she had left and the friend holding the bicycle. She did not remember anyone taking her picture.

“I could come and talk to the officer,” he offered.

“He’s already spoken to me. I live here.”

“I know that.”

“I want you to ask before you come up. Not call when you’re outside. Ask me, and wait until I’ve answered.”

Laura watched him across the phone.

“All right,” Sam conceded.

“Dad?”

“I’ll ask you first.”

“Thank you.” Sarah’s voice changed as someone spoke near her. “I have to go. I’ll call tonight.”

“Love you,” Laura said.

“Love you both.”

“Love you, Sare,” Sam put in, before the call ended.

He checked that the mug’s lid had caught on both sides. Laura picked up the phone.

“Are you going to Arlington?”

“I need to speak to Dambrosky.”

“About the photographs?”

“Yes.”

“James asked you to let him give his account to the lawyer.”

“I won’t speak for James.”

She sat and did not answer. Sam lifted his bag from the chair and checked for his keys.

“Tell me when you’re coming home,” she said.

“I will.”

Outside, the morning was cold enough to show his breath. He left a message for Dambrosky’s assistant, asking to see him that afternoon. In the map on his phone, the route to northern Virginia waited for him to start.

He could hear Sarah saying she did not remember the photographer. She had been standing in public, drinking from her water bottle, looking at a friend. Someone could stand that close and leave with her face on a memory card.

He backed down the drive. At the end of the street he lingered longer than he needed to.

Cumberland was in the other direction.

He turned toward it.

West of Hagerstown the highway began to climb, and the trees on the ridges had turned further than the maple at home. Twice he thought of what he had promised Sarah an hour before, and both times he kept driving. The towns came up in the valleys and dropped behind. Sarah had chosen the college at the end of this road. In August he had carried boxes into it and driven home saying it was not so far.

❦

A grounds worker was clearing leaves from the college’s front walk when Sam found the visitors’ lot. Each pass of the blower pushed a bright line of yellow toward the grass. Two students crossed through it, and the worker let them pass before starting again.

Sam parked, took his phone and left the bag on the passenger-side floor. He sent Sarah a message.

I’m here. Outside the main library. Can we talk?

He had seen the building in the photograph. Now its windows reflected the pale sky and branches of a tree he had mistaken for part of the brickwork. There was a bench at the foot of it.

Sarah called.

“You said you would ask.”

“I wanted to see you.”

“I’m in a class.”

“I can wait.”

“You said you’d ask first.”

The line stayed open. He heard a door close at her end.

“Where are you?”

“On the bench beside the library walk.”

“Stay there.”

He watched people pass until he saw her coming between two buildings, her backpack on one shoulder and a phone in her hand. Her hair was tied up on top of her head. She walked fast with her eyes on the path, and it was Laura’s walk, the one Laura used on a site when a delivery had come wrong. Another young woman walked with her. Sarah spoke to her at the corner, and the woman stayed there while Sarah came on alone.

Sam stood.

“You didn’t need to come out of class,” he said.

“I wasn’t going to sit there wondering what you would do.”

He reached for the loose strap of her backpack. She moved it higher on her shoulder before he touched it.

“Come home for a few days,” he said.

“No.”

“Bring what you need. We can call the school from home.”

“I have a lab this afternoon. I have an exam Wednesday. I’m not leaving.”

“You can explain the circumstances.”

“I did. To the people here who can help me.” She looked at the bench. “Would you sit down?”

He did not sit. The young woman at the corner was watching them now.

“I have the car,” he said. “You don’t have to arrange anything.”

“Dad, please sit down.”

He sat. Sarah stayed on her feet, leaving more room between them than she would have at home.

“I know you’re scared,” she began. “I am too. I keep looking at that picture and trying to remember who was on the path. I don’t remember. It’s horrible. But coming here after you promised not to makes it worse.”

“I promised to ask first.”

“You didn’t.”

“I sent you a message.”

“When you were here.”

A student approached with a stack of books against his chest. Sarah stepped aside to let him pass, then watched until he was inside the library.

“I don’t know what these people might do,” Sam said.

“I don’t either. I’m trying to do what the campus officer told me.”

He looked at her hands. One had the phone. The other gripped the backpack strap near her collarbone. She used to reach for his hand before crossing a parking lot, even when she was old enough to complain about doing it.

“If anything happened to you while I sat at home—”

“You wouldn’t be able to stop it by staying here all day. Or by making me sit in the house. They found Mom at work. I’m trying to have people around me who know what’s happening.”

“I know what’s happening.”

“You know about the company.” She stopped. Her hand tightened on the strap. “You don’t know Tessa. You don’t know which building I—” She shook her head. “I have a lab at two. I haven’t looked at it since you called.”

The grounds worker switched off the blower. In the sudden quiet, Sam could hear the catch in her breath.

“Who is that?” he asked, looking toward the corner.

“Tessa. The friend I told you about this morning.”

Tessa lifted one hand when she saw them looking. Sam raised his own, then let it fall.

“I could speak to her.”

“She doesn’t need you to tell her what to do.” Sarah wiped under one eye with the heel of her hand. “Please go home, Dad. I need to get back to class.”

He stood slowly. This time she took a step back.

He paused with one hand on the bench. She looked at the hand, then at his face.

“I’m going,” he said.

“All right.”

He wanted to hug her. He could not find a way to ask that did not make it another thing she would have to refuse.

“Call your mother,” he said.

“I will.”

She walked back to Tessa. The two young women stood together before turning toward the building. Sarah did not look back.

Sam returned to the car. His hands were cold, although he had worn the heavier coat. He sat with the door open until a driver wanting the neighboring space lowered her window to ask whether he could close it.

He apologized and pulled it shut.

There was a message from Dambrosky’s assistant. Three o’clock in Reston, if Sam wanted to come.

He confirmed the time. Before leaving, he sent Laura a message saying he had visited Sarah and was driving to Reston. He watched the screen for a reply, then started the car. No reply had come when the mountains were behind him. He drove east in silence with the phone in the cup holder. Every few miles he looked down at it. The screen gave him the time and nothing else.

❦

The receptionist gave him another visitor sticker. Sam put it flat against his shirt, above the place where it would fold.

Dambrosky came out to meet him carrying nothing. He glanced at the sticker and led Sam to the same office. The blind was half lowered against the afternoon sun, and a closed laptop was the only thing on the desk.

“Graham says you want to speak to the directors,” he said.

“I do.”

“They’ll make time.” Dambrosky gestured toward the chair. “What did you want to tell me first?”

Sam sat, leaving his coat on. The photograph of the crane remained on the cabinet. Someone had put a new box of tissues near it.

“Your people photographed my daughter at her college.”

Dambrosky closed the door. “I’ve seen the report.”

“She was drinking coffee with a friend.”

“She was in a public place.”

“That is your answer?”

“The observation was suspended Friday. I told you that myself.” Dambrosky took the other chair. “I’m not going to pretend the photograph wasn’t taken.”

“You wrote that I wouldn’t leave if I thought veterans would lose their services. You had that assessment before you approached Civic Passage.”

“Before we met with you, yes.”

Sam had expected a pause. He wanted Dambrosky to ask where he had found it, or deny the words and have to be shown them. Instead the man settled his hand on the arm of the chair.

“Did you expect me to hear about that photograph and decide to help you?” Sam asked.

“I wanted you to consider whom you were bringing into the disagreement.”

“My daughter wasn’t in it until you sent someone to her college.”

“She hasn’t been approached on my instructions.”

“On whose, then?”

“Nobody was told to approach her,” Dambrosky repeated.

“You had her photographed. You had my wife followed. You stood behind Rusk when he said Len knew all along who the shipment was for. Now you admit that was wrong, and you want me to stop opposing the installation before you’ll put it right.”

“I have offered to correct the account of Len’s work.”

Sam’s coat felt too heavy across his shoulders. He drew one arm out of it, then the other, and let it lie behind him on the chair.

“The men you supply are standing at the checkpoints so nobody on that coast goes home. You told me you knew what the Guard was doing, and you’re still supplying them.”

“We’ve discussed why.”

Sam looked at the crane photograph. This time he could make out what was on the truck door. The logo was there, the animal split in two, half obscured by dust. He could hear a vehicle changing gears outside the window, though the road was several floors below.

He wanted to ask if anyone had told Dambrosky that a girl had died on the way to the yard. He drew a breath, then let it out, the question unasked.

“In Iraq you needed Marines who would go where they were sent,” he said. “Now you want civilians to carry the same load for you, with a recovery program for cover, and to stay on once they learn what they’ve been carrying. They’ll be where I was on that road, with no one around them armed.”

“Aaron is finishing the connection. Jasleen will tell James which recruiters see what your model finds, and he’ll switch it on for them. Let them finish, Sam.”

“James won’t help Mission Support recruit anyone.”

“James works for Delmere. I can put another engineer in charge of the installation.”

“He knows what you asked him to do to me. He showed me the assessment. He told me you asked if knowing about the observation would make me stop opposing the installation.”

Dambrosky had not looked away.

Sam heard the words after he had finished saying them. He remembered James leaning across the coffee-shop table.

Don’t identify me to him.

“He’s prepared to speak to the lawyer,” Sam went on.

“He can do that.” Dambrosky picked up the telephone on the side table. “He won’t continue handling the installation while he passes internal material to you.”

Dambrosky pressed a button and waited. Sam could hear the faint sound of a woman answering.

“Ask Jasleen to call me when she’s free,” Dambrosky said. “We need another engineer to get Civic Passage’s model working with ADICUS.”

He put down the receiver.

Sam stood and found his coat behind him. One sleeve had fallen under the chair. He pulled it free. The chair tipped against the desk, and he left it there.

“Don’t punish him for telling me what you did.”

“Then don’t promise me he’ll refuse his assignment.”

Dambrosky rose. Sam turned toward the door before he could open it for him.

❦

Laura called while Sam was waiting to leave the parking garage. He drove into a space on the next level and answered.

“Sarah asked if I knew you were coming,” she said. “I had to tell her I didn’t.”

“I wanted to bring her home.”

“She told you what she needed this morning. You agreed. She was shaking when she called me.”

Sam put his forehead against the heel of his hand. The garage light shone on the steering wheel.

“Did Tessa stay with her?”

“Yes. They went back to class together.”

For several seconds neither spoke.

“Come home,” Laura told him. “Don’t go anywhere else to fix something. Tell me when you’re on your way.”

“I’ll leave now.”

After the call, he sat with the phone in his hand until a message from James appeared.

Jasleen has removed me from the Civic Passage installation. Did you tell Martin I gave you the assessment?

Sam typed that he had been trying to stop the observation. He read the sentence and erased it.

Yes. I told him. I’m sorry.

James’s reply came before Sam put the phone down.

I asked you to let me speak for myself.

He left the message open. On the passenger-side floor, the work bag had tipped forward. His employee badge lay in the mesh pocket where he had put it that morning.
