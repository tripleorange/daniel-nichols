A Use for Good MenChapter Two

Chapter Two

The Meeting

Sam lifted the chair and set it two feet farther from the table. The wheels objected when they touched carpet again, but his knees cleared the metal frame under the tabletop.

Aisha Grant sat on the opposite side. A legal pad lay open in front of her, the date already written across the top. She had her reading glasses down on her nose and her pen held still between the lines. When she wrote, she wrote in whole sentences. Her gaze followed the chair to Sam’s knees, and one corner of her mouth lifted.

Janet Yoon looked up from the display controls. Her face remained solemn. Her hair was still damp from the parking lot, and a cardigan hung over the chair behind her that she had not had time to put on. “There’s a separate request form for employees with legs.”

Sam settled into the chair. “I filed it in 2019.”

Janet bent to the display cable, but not before a quick smile broke through. “Then it should be approved any quarter now.”

They were in the larger training room at Civic Passage’s Arlington office, the one room left that could seat visitors. Chairs were stacked against the back wall, and a child’s drawing hung pinned to a board next to an old emergency-contact sheet. Someone had drawn each member of a family the same height. Through the glass wall, Sam could see rows of vacant desks interrupted by plants someone still came in to water. Near the print station, a cart carried boxed headsets, spare keyboards, and three coffee mugs with a paper sign marked Free. He knew most of his colleagues now as shoulders inside video squares; he no longer remembered the ways they crossed a room.

He once spent three days a week here. Now the drive from Frederick felt like a visit to the office rather than a commute. He left before sunrise, crossed two counties in rain, and arrived early enough to discover that his old access card opened the garage. The floor upstairs was dark when he came out of the elevator, and the lights woke in banks ahead of him as he walked. By the time Janet arrived, most of it had gone dark again.

Graham Munley entered with a paper cup in one hand and a blue folder clamped at his side. He was a compact man who dressed one size better than the office, and he carried the folder against his ribs, the way he carried every proposal, as if it could be read through the cover. He stopped beside Sam’s relocated chair. “Good. We’ve begun with facilities.”

He put a blue folder at each place and stayed on his feet. “Delmere’s people are in the lobby. Before they come up, I want us able to say yes to something.”

Sam opened his copy. The first page was one line.

$12 MILLION / THREE YEARS

He read it again. Below it were two additional years at three million each, if Delmere chose to continue. Neither organization had signed.

“Twelve million committed if we make the agreement,” Graham said. “Not a succession of grants we have to win again every spring. We could tell people their jobs will still be here when the current funding ends.”

Aisha was no longer writing. Sam knew she was the person who sat with employees after everyone else had finished explaining a grant decision. He had never asked her how those conversations went.

“What does Delmere get?” Sam asked.

“A trial first. Up to a hundred veterans could volunteer to have us compare the model with our counselors’ assessments. Delmere offers six paid advisory assignments to people whose experience fits.” Graham took a sip of coffee. “After that, they want to discuss running the model inside their own recruiting system.”

“Our model?”

“Parts of it. The license is on page four.”

Sam found the paragraph about a later license, read it, and went back to the first sentence. He had expected a request for results. They were also asking how they could produce those results without him.

Janet’s phone lit up on the table. “They’re on their way up.”

Jasleen Bedi introduced herself as Delmere’s head of workforce intelligence. She took the chair that faced the door and set her things in a row before her, folder, phone face down, glasses case, squared to the table’s edge. James Balan came in behind her with his sleeves turned back twice and a printed screen folded in quarters in his shirt pocket. He had the engineer’s habit of looking at the equipment while people talked. When he connected his laptop, the wall display showed the Delmere logo for a moment before returning to a blue screen and the words No Signal.

James held up a silver adapter. “I was told this room supported outside systems.”

Janet had the remote in one hand and a white adapter in the other. “It supports them emotionally.”

A spare cable lay by Sam’s elbow. He left it there. Janet had not asked him to fix anything. She traded James the white adapter for his silver one, and the logo came back and stayed.

Sam opened his notebook. Before leaving home, he had written down the questions he intended to ask. He had also written wait in the margin, where nobody else would need to read it.

Jasleen drew a slim folder from her bag and placed six stapled descriptions on the table. “These are the assignments Graham asked us for.”

Sam took the nearest one. A distribution company wanted advice on keeping essential deliveries moving after an extended power failure. Three weeks, around a current job, with the fee on the first page. The other five asked different questions and paid the same way.

“I don’t want to send your people a congratulatory email and leave them waiting for work,” Jasleen said. “These clients have agreed to pay. If we can find six suitable people, they can start.”

“After our board approves the trial,” Graham put in.

“For the trial, we run the model here,” Sam said. “A counselor sees the record first. Someone else checks whatever the model adds. We don’t let a useful-looking suggestion become a qualification nobody has verified.”

“Agreed,” Jasleen said.

“And the veterans see the jobs before we send their names anywhere.”

Janet marked the page. “What happens to the person who turns down an offer, or starts and finds the work isn’t what they agreed to?”

“They stop. We pay them for the work they’ve completed.” Jasleen faced her. “Put that in the information they receive.”

Aisha leaned forward. “And the people who don’t get one of the six places?”

“They still get our assessment,” Sam answered. “Something they can use with another employer. Otherwise we’re asking them to help test a product and giving almost all of them nothing.”

James closed the presentation on his laptop. “Our recruiters use a system called ADICUS to match people with work. I lead the team that connects other software to it. May I ask about your model?”

Sam slid the assignment description alongside his notebook. “Of course.”

“The utility case in your paper. Did anyone tell the model to look for work in utilities?”

“No. We supplied his service history and the employers’ requirements. It found the match.”

“Could our staff run it?”

“Yes. They’d have to verify a person’s record and qualifications. Any employer should. But they wouldn’t be doing the model’s work for it.”

Jasleen drew the folder closer. “People like the ones you work with get filtered out before anyone talks to them. We can persuade a client to consider an unfamiliar background if we can explain what the person has actually done.”

“That still takes a person willing to listen,” Sam said.

“It does,” Jasleen agreed. “Finding someone worth listening to would help.”

He thought of the veterans who apologized for their résumés before handing them over. Years of decisions and work reduced to something they distrusted on the page.

James turned his laptop so Sam could see the ADICUS screen: a sample assignment, an empty place for candidates, and an empty profile with space for a capability and a short explanation.

Sam pointed to the empty space below them. “Where would you put something the person still has to learn?”

“In the explanation.” James clicked, and a panel opened at the side of the screen. He closed it again. “You want the missing training beside the capability, not behind a click.”

“If it’s going to affect who you offer the work to, yes.”

James wrote a note on the printed screen he had brought. “I’d rather fix this with you now than discover it after we’ve built the connection. Could we begin with an hour on Monday morning? Nine?”

“Nine is fine,” Sam said. “I’ll show you the pieces: what the model reads, what it recommends, and the explanation that goes with each result.”

“We’d be interested in discussing a purchase,” Jasleen said. “The trained model, the software that connects it to a system like yours, and your rights to the invention. Substantially more than twelve million. We’d want to finish the evaluation first.”

Graham set down his pen. “I’ll hear an offer. For now, we’re discussing a license.”

Sam looked at the six descriptions between them. “Would we still be able to use it for our own people?”

“We could include that,” Jasleen allowed. “But Delmere would want to choose where else we developed and used it.”

Sam heard the words where else. He had built the model for the people who sat down in front of Civic Passage’s counselors. Delmere wanted it for people he had not met and would not be told about. He wrote nothing down.

Graham opened his binder. “Whatever we agree, the model goes nowhere yet. Alderwick holds it until our people have watched Delmere run it there and the board has voted.”

“We can agree to that,” Jasleen said.

Sam read the paragraph Graham turned his way. Civic Passage would name the people who went to Alderwick to watch. The draft did not put Sam’s name in that sentence.

“And you’d expect me to help examine it,” he said.

“I would,” Graham said. “Along with the people you’ll work with here. They have to understand it too.”

Aisha looked up from her pad. “How much of your team’s ordinary work stops while they do this?”

Sam began to say none, then looked at his list. “Some. I’ll have to ask them which work they can postpone.”

“Ask your team first,” Aisha said. “Before you promise their time to anyone.”

“We’d want the model connected to ADICUS by October,” Jasleen said. “If that’s impossible, I need to know before I assign the people.”

“You’ll have a date from us on Monday,” Graham said.

Jasleen closed the folder. “Martin will be pleased we’ve found somewhere to begin.”

Sam looked up. “Who is Martin?”

“Dambrosky. He runs Mission Support for Delmere, the side that puts people and equipment into the field for clients. These first assignments came through his people.” She slipped her glasses into their case. “He’d like to meet you on Wednesday, if you’re available.”

Sam tried the name against his memory and found nothing certain. “Does he work out of Reston?”

“Now. He came over when we acquired Wolmeck. He spent years in the field before that.”

Sam’s hand rested on the cover of his notebook.

Wolmeck. The logo on a vehicle door, a wolf’s head cut down the middle, the two halves a finger’s width apart, half obscured by soot. A man in civilian attire standing by a Marine. He remembered the mark more clearly than the man’s face. The soot had a distinct smell, greasy and close. It came now into a room that smelled of carpet shampoo and somebody’s coffee.

“In Iraq?”

“Yes,” Jasleen said. “You were there too, weren’t you?”

“With Marines. I was their chaplain.”

“I thought Graham had said that.” She paused with the glasses case in her hand. “Did you know Martin?”

“I don’t know.” Sam closed his notebook. “I knew the company.”

No one spoke. Then Graham gathered the assignment descriptions into a stack and asked Janet when she could get them to the counselors. She told him she could send them that afternoon. Jasleen stood and thanked her.

James unplugged the white adapter and returned it to Janet. At the door, Jasleen let Graham finish speaking to Janet. Sam joined her.

“Wednesday,” he said. “I’d like to meet Dambrosky.”

“I’ll arrange it.”

Graham walked them to the elevator. Sam picked up the spare cable he had left alone and coiled it at the base of the display. There was no reason to leave it trailing across the table.

Graham came back without his cup and did not sit. “Janet, when can you put the plan together? The work, the people doing it, and the time they’ll need.”

“Two weeks for a working plan. That gives the team leads time to check their estimates, and I can finish it after I’m back.”

“Back from where?”

Her hands hung above the keyboard. “I’m out next Thursday and Friday. The leave was approved in June.”

Graham set both palms on the table. “I don’t need every task checked in two weeks. I need three pages by Monday so I can give Jasleen a date.”

Janet turned one hand palm-up. “That’s different. I thought you wanted the full plan. If you want three pages by Monday, I—”

“This opportunity could keep our programs intact for years, and you’re giving me two weeks because you have leave.” Graham tapped the folder against the table. “I may have misjudged how much responsibility you were ready to take on.”

Janet’s fingers lowered to the table one at a time. Her mouth closed, and the color under her eyes seemed to fade. She faced the screen as if the schedule there required her full attention.

The ventilation came on above them.

Air moved through the ceiling grille with a faint rattle, then a steady mechanical rush. The room was cool, but dry heat moved across the back of Sam’s neck, carrying the suggestion of dust and diesel.

Sam set his pen down next to his notebook. “You asked her for an estimate for the full workload. Now you want three pages by Monday. Let her answer that before you tell her she isn’t responsible.”

Graham’s attention shifted from Janet. “I’ve been clear.”

Sam did not reach for the pen. “You changed the request and cut her off while she was answering it.”

Aisha capped her pen.

Graham held Sam’s gaze, then addressed Janet. “Start with three pages for Monday. We can add the detail afterward. Can you do that?”

Janet drew her chair closer to the table. The wheels made two soft clicks against the floor outlet. She turned back to her laptop, angling her shoulders away from Sam. “It’s okay, Sam. He’s right that we need to move faster. I can get it done. It’s a big opportunity.”

Sam’s attention stayed on Graham. “The way he spoke to you isn’t okay.”

Graham set the blue folder flat on the table. “She said she can handle it. Let it go, Sam.”

The heat did not leave the back of Sam’s neck.

He pushed the chair behind him and stood.

No one spoke as he moved past the corner of the table. He stopped at the presentation end, several feet from Graham. The aisle to the door lay open on Graham’s left. Sam’s hands stayed at his sides. Graham had to lift his face to meet his eyes.

“You asked her for one thing and blamed her for not giving you another. You do this every time you promise something before asking how long it takes. Then everyone else has to work late so you can say you delivered.”

Graham stayed where he was. His hand closed around the back of a chair. “Sit down, Sam, or I’ll take you off the project.”

Sam’s notebook lay open at the other end of the table. He could see the list of work he had agreed to take on. Graham could put someone else in that chair and present the model without him.

“You can’t take me off what I built. The model is the project. Without it, all you have is a proposal you don’t understand.”

Graham let go of the chair. “The model belongs to Civic Passage.”

For a moment nobody moved.

Across the table, Janet sat with both hands folded above her keyboard. Her chair was no longer angled toward Sam. Aisha stood, closed her legal pad, and gathered it beneath one arm. “We’re done,” she said.

Graham turned to her. “Aisha—”

“Separately.” She indicated the door with the capped pen. “I’ll contact each of you.”

Sam went back to his chair. His notebook lay open to the work he had volunteered, with Janet’s name beside his own. He clipped the pen to the cover and picked up the open notebook.

Janet kept her attention on the agenda on her laptop. Sam waited for her to look at him. When she did not, he kept his place at the table for several more seconds before walking out.

The door closed behind him, cutting the ventilation to a muffled rush. He pressed the elevator button and watched the number descend. Behind him, the floor was dark again except for the one bank of lights above the three of them.

You were the only one willing to stop him.
