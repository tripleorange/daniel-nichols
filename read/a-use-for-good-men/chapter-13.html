Chapter Thirteen

The Men Who Sent Them

Webb brought his own coffee and declined the chair Sam pulled out for him.

“Other one,” he said. “I drove up with the sun in my eyes. I’d like to stop looking at it.”

Sam moved around the little table. The room was meant for job interviews, with two chairs and a plant someone turned toward the window each morning. He lowered the blind halfway.

“That do?”

“It’ll do.” Webb set his cup next to a clothbound notebook. He was broader through the middle than Sam remembered. His hair, once shaved close enough to show the shape of his skull, lay thin and white above his ears. “You look the same.”

“No, I don’t.”

“No. But people expect you to say it.”

Sam laughed. Webb smiled and took off his jacket. For a moment they could have been waiting for other men to arrive, men whose names Sam would remember as soon as he heard their voices.

Webb laid the jacket across his knees. “You said Dambrosky.”

Sam described the model. It read a veteran’s service record and suggested civilian work. Delmere wanted it for their own recruiting. He told Webb what Dambrosky had said at Alderwick about the Guard. Webb listened and left the notebook closed.

“What do you want from me?” Webb asked.

“I want to understand why Wolmeck was sending requests into our operations shelter. You were there when those requests came in.”

“Some of them.”

“Tell me about those.”

Webb put a finger inside the notebook’s cover but left it closed. “No recording. You don’t use my name with Delmere, and you don’t copy this. If I decide to speak to a lawyer, I’ll do that myself.”

“May I ask Dambrosky about what you tell me?”

“You can ask him what his people were doing. Don’t read him my notes or tell him who sat in this room.”

Sam put his phone in his bag. He left his own notebook there too.

Webb opened to a page near the middle. A rectangle of cleaner paper showed where something once lay between the leaves. The writing ran downhill, steadied briefly along the ruled margin, then wandered again.

“My relief hated my handwriting,” Webb said. “I’d read this out to him before I left. Otherwise he called me back.”

Sam leaned close enough to see and no closer. Webb indicated an entry for four vehicles, followed by a place Sam remembered and a reference to an added stop. Beside it, in smaller writing, local security stores. Above that, in capitals, three letters and one word.

“OGA,” Webb said. “Other government agency. The lieutenant put that on the board when the people asking wouldn’t say who they were. Dambrosky gave him the word under it. I copied what was on the board.”

Webb turned back a page. Near the top was the shrine: two vehicles, religious support, Sam’s name shortened to one letter. Beside it, in the same small writing, CA, one Wolmeck.

“They came in with Civil Affairs that month,” Webb went on. “A clinic, pumps for the water project, your shrine. Always something we’d have sent anyway, with one of their vehicles joining at the river.” He turned the page forward and put his finger under the added stop. “Same town.”

Sam looked at the two entries. He had sat on the wall there to pull on his boots.

“You see what it was,” Webb said. “Your shrine got one of their trucks across the river with nobody looking in it. The run got their stores in on our fourth vehicle. Same town, same month. The board said religious support and local security stores, and both of those were true.”

“What were they storing?”

“I didn’t go into the yard. I can tell you what I heard before they sent you.”

Webb leaned back. The notebook stayed open with his hand on it.

“A Wolmeck man brought in the request. Dambrosky was with him. The lieutenant asked which unit was getting the supplies. Dambrosky said they were for an Iraqi force that was going to hold the district after we moved on. He called the force Tamarisk, as if it were a unit we should have heard of. He wanted our vehicles to carry it under the name local security stores.”

“The lieutenant wanted to know why the Marines weren’t being told that,” Webb said. “I remember it because it was what I’d have asked. Dambrosky told him to brief the destination and leave the local arrangement to the people handling it.”

Sam looked at the place written after the date. He remembered asking what the Marines were supposed to do there. He remembered how readily he accepted the assurance that somebody responsible knew.

“The lieutenant went to the phone, or the radio,” Webb went on. “I want to say the phone. I couldn’t hear the other end. When he came back he told us to add the stop. Marines changed the board. A Marine gave the drivers their instructions.”

“Who he called, I don’t know.” Webb closed the notebook around one finger. “I’ve thought about it since. I know that isn’t what you want.”

“It matters that Dambrosky was there.”

“Yes. He was there.”

“The night your run went in,” Webb said, “we heard mortars across the river. Outgoing, from the far bank, where your stop was. Six rounds. Maybe eight. I counted at the time. I couldn’t tell you now. Then small arms for about a minute, and it stopped before midnight.” He kept his finger in the notebook. “When I came on next, the board had contact across the river. Enemy KIA, no friendly casualties. Under it, the town. Cleared. No stop. I didn’t ask what cleared it.”

A woman laughed beyond the door. Someone answered her, and their voices receded along the corridor. Sam waited until he could no longer hear them.

“You said there were other requests.”

“Some were ordinary. A broken truck. A place to unload. Don’t put all of them in with this one.” Webb pulled his finger out and left the book shut. “Four days after the run, or five, an interpreter brought a man into operations. He had walked in from the town. He wanted to know where his brother was.” Webb laid his hand flat on the cover. “He told it in order. The afternoon of your run, four men walked to the yard to ask about the girl. His brother was one of them. A man at the yard wrote their names down while they talked, and then nobody let them leave. He said they were shut in the containers. That night mortars came down in the streets behind the crossing, and people died in their houses. Then Tamarisk took the old man who kept the tomb, because the four had gone to him first, before they went to the yard. He said the five of them were shot behind the yard, by the river. The old man they carried back and left in the road in front of the tomb. The force was telling the town they were insurgents and had died fighting. The interpreter’s word, insurgents. The four houses had Tamarisk’s men in them now. He said that last.”

“Were these the people Wolmeck supplied?”

“The interpreter used the same name. The man said some of our vehicles had brought in the load for those men. I was there when he said it. I didn’t see any of it.”

“Did he give names?”

“All five. I wrote them under the stop.” Webb opened the notebook again and turned it on his knee so that Sam could read. Below local security stores, in the same downhill hand, five names. The last began with a word Sam knew. Hajj Abbas.

Sam had never learned his name. He had the man’s gift on a shelf at home, holding up his daughter’s books, and had told Laura he could not remember who had given it to him.

“The officer went to find Dambrosky,” Webb told him. “They came back together. Dambrosky asked the interpreter if the family had somewhere else to stay. The man started answering before she’d finished translating. He kept pointing out the door.”

Webb eyed the lowered blind.

“The officer said he’d send someone to ask about the men. Dambrosky said taking supplies away from Tamarisk would make it harder to keep the district. They sent the next load two days later.” Webb opened the notebook, found the page and closed it again. “Two days. I had it right. I put it on the board.”

Sam tried to picture the man at the shelter door. He could summon the doorway more easily than a face he had never seen: the sagging flap, the ground worn smooth where men waited to be admitted. There were people he would not have passed without speaking to them. There must have been many he never met.

The boy in the red shirt had been standing beside the old man when the vehicles pulled away. Sam did not ask Webb about him. There was no one left to ask.

“What happened to him?”

“I don’t know. Nobody came back to ask. Not on my watch, and I was on it most nights that month.”

Webb rubbed his palm over the cover. “I thought I’d see something about it in the handover notes. I didn’t. After a while I stopped expecting to.”

Sam stood and lifted the blind an inch. He had no reason to do it. The room was neither dark nor hot.

“If the officers ever found anything about your stop, I never heard it,” Webb said. “I left that watch rotation before the end of the month.”

“I can tell you where the lieutenant’s call didn’t go,” Webb offered. “It didn’t go to battalion. It went to somebody’s office, not somebody’s unit. Whoever he called, the stop came back approved inside an hour, and I never saw paper on it.”

“The executive officer told me the operations staff would review it.”

“Then they reviewed a stop that wasn’t theirs.”

Sam let go of the cord. He had asked command for an answer nineteen years ago, and it had not arrived. What Webb had brought him instead was a man in a doorway asking for his brother.

Webb put the notebook inside his jacket. “There were things happening, Chaplain. But you’re putting some of them together too cleanly.”

“I haven’t told you what I think happened.”

“You keep asking what happened afterward. Nobody ever told me.” Webb stood. “I’m not telling you to stop asking. I’m telling you I don’t know.”

Sam walked him to the front steps. There were six concrete steps down to the sidewalk, with a pipe rail Sam had never used, and cars parked along the curb to the corner. Webb’s was somewhere among them. Webb checked one trouser pocket, then the other, and handed Sam his coffee while he searched his jacket.

“In the car?”

“Let’s hope not.”

Webb found the keys in an inside pocket and took back the cup. At the bottom of the steps he turned.

“I remember you coming through at night. Just to see how we were doing.”

Sam could think of no answer that would not ask Webb to reassure him. He raised a hand and stayed on the steps until Webb was at his car.

❦

Dambrosky was already due at Civic Passage at three to meet Evelyn Shaw, the board chair. Graham gave Sam the half hour after.

Sam spent the intervening hours at his desk. The floor went on around him. A counselor laughed through the wall, the copier ran a long job, somebody in the corridor asked who had the key to the supply closet. He had come up from the interview room with the caretaker’s name, and there was nobody on the floor he could say it to. Aaron had sent him a comparison of an applicant’s service record with the model’s assessment. A counselor thought it overstated the man’s repair duties, and Aaron wanted Sam’s help with the wording. Sam began a reply. Then Jasleen called about the work James had shut down. He asked her to arrange a conversation with a civilian employee in Meredan.

“Someone who lives there and works for Delmere,” he said. “I’d like to hear how supplying the Guard affects the people who live there.”

“I can ask our local personnel office if someone wants to speak with you,” Jasleen answered.

“Will their manager sit in?”

“Would you prefer that?”

“No. I’d prefer they could answer without a manager listening.”

She paused. “I’ll ask if someone is willing to speak with you privately.”

“Thank you.”

At three twenty-five Sam went downstairs. Evelyn was outside Graham’s office with Dambrosky, laughing at something Sam did not hear. She dangled a pair of reading glasses by one arm. She was tall, with white hair cut at the jaw. She had kept her coat on, and she laughed with her head back.

“Sam,” she said. “Martin tells me you’ve been making him earn his money.”

Dambrosky looked at him. “We’ve been discussing the work.”

“I’d like to speak with you afterward,” Sam told Evelyn.

“I have to leave. Send me a time for tomorrow.” She put the glasses into her bag. “And send Graham the names of those two foundations, Martin. We should talk to them before they finish their budgets.”

“I’ll make the introductions.”

She touched Dambrosky’s arm as she passed. Sam watched her cross to the elevators.

Graham came to his office door. He had his glasses pushed up on his forehead, and he looked past Sam at the elevators before he spoke. “You can use the room next to mine.”

“What foundations?”

“People Martin knows. Evelyn’s asked him to fill the vacant board seat. The directors will consider it Friday.”

“He works for the company buying access to our model.”

“They know where he works.” Graham lowered his voice. “That isn’t a reason to refuse every introduction he can make.”

Dambrosky was at the interview-room door. Sam followed him inside and stood while the man moved the plant to make room for his bag.

“Please leave that,” Sam told him.

Dambrosky set the plant down again.

Sam sat. He could hear his own breath before he spoke.

“In Iraq, who was Tamarisk, the force you sent us to supply?”

Dambrosky lowered himself into the other chair. He had put the bag on the table after all, against the plant, and he sat upright with his hands in his lap, in the chair the applicants used. “Which movement?”

Sam told him the place and the month. He did not quote Webb or describe their morning together.

“The men who were supposed to hold the district,” Dambrosky said. “They worked with the people employing us.”

“Who employed you?”

“Do you mean to tell Graham I sent Marines out on my own authority?”

“I’m asking who paid for the work.”

“People who wanted that district held,” Dambrosky replied. “I’m not going to name them in a nonprofit’s interview room.”

“The lieutenant wrote three letters on the board beside your request.”

Dambrosky rested his hands on the table. “Then you know what the lieutenant knew. We were supporting a local partner. We weren’t deciding American policy in a motor pool.”

Sam watched the man’s hands. One thumbnail was split near the edge.

“Those mercenaries shot four men who went to your gate about the girl, and an old man from the tomb. They shut them in your containers first.”

“Tamarisk reported contact that night. I read the report.”

“Did you know before the next load went out?”

“Yes.”

The answer was quiet. Beyond the door, Graham spoke to someone about an appointment.

“You supplied those men. Somebody told you what they had done. You sent the next load anyway. Why?”

“You’ve heard me on that,” Dambrosky said.

“You could have refused.”

“So could the officers who sent your vehicles.”

“The officers aren’t here,” Sam said. “You are.”

Dambrosky drew his hands back from the table. “We all had instructions. Some things you can’t repair from the position you’re in.”

Sam’s heel pressed into the carpet. That sentence hurt more than the three letters on the board. He had said versions of it to men who wanted him to do something beyond his power. Sometimes he had been right.

“You’re in a position now,” he said. “Stop the next one.”

Dambrosky looked at him for several seconds. “What are you asking me to change now?”

“I’m telling Graham I won’t help put the model into ADICUS. Not just Mission Support. Any of it.”

“James has already stopped the Mission Support work.”

“For now. You’re still shipping ammunition in with the relief stores, to men who hold the road against the families they put out, and you still want our model to find the veterans to move it for you.”

Dambrosky stood. “Say that to Graham, then. I won’t explain your decision for you.”

He lifted his bag clear of the plant.

At the door, Sam spoke again. “Were you at the yard that day?”

Dambrosky stopped. “For part of it.”

“Did you know we were coming?”

“We were waiting for you.”

Sam stood up.

“The morning after, I asked my executive officer whose load that was,” he said. “Nobody ever answered me. You put your stores under my visit to the shrine and under my prayer at the hood. If anyone across that river had learned what came in with the chaplain, it wouldn’t have been you waiting on that road for the next chaplain to come through.”

Dambrosky did not put the bag down. “Nobody learned.”

“Four men came to your gate that afternoon,” Sam said. “They learned.”

Dambrosky went out and let the door close behind him. Sam sat by the untouched plant. He had wanted to ask who we meant. He could yet open the door and follow Dambrosky down the corridor.

Instead he took out his phone and wrote to Graham. None of it, for any division of Delmere, and he wanted the directors to have that in writing before Friday. Then a shorter message to Priya and Aaron: could they see him before they left.

He read each once, which was less than he usually gave anything, and sent them.

Through the lowered blind, a bright strip lay across the empty chair. He remembered a Marine rubbing dust around a mirror, taking care over a small thing before the engines started.
