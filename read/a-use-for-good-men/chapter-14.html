Chapter Fourteen

The Staff Sergeant

The two hoses looked identical until the corporal turned one coupling toward the sun. “This one feeds the lift,” he said. “That one returns to the reservoir.”

Sam bent far enough to see the different fittings. “They didn’t make that easy to see.”

One corner of the corporal’s mouth lifted. “That’s how they know who read the manual, sir.”

“I suspected a moral test.”

It was the week after the run to the river. They stood beside a pallet of hydraulic lines in the forward supply yard. It was midmorning, and the sun was already on the backs of their necks. Shade cloth covered half the pallet and none of the people working around it. Beyond the corporal, Marines moved between stacks of tires, wooden crates, and steel containers whose old shipping colors showed through fresh tan paint. On the nearest, the contractor’s logo showed through the tan paint. Sam noticed it there before the run.

The corporal tilted the lift hose toward it. “We keep those in eighteen now. The lance corporal reorganized the whole section last week.”

White numbers had been painted along the open container’s shelves. A grease-pencil diagram covered a board near the entrance, each bin connected to an inventory line in handwriting clear enough that other people could correct the board without obscuring it.

“His system?”

“Yes, sir. He found two crates we had already reported missing.” The corporal looked at the hoses across his arms. “And a bin with nothing on it. It’s on the board as local security stores. He says there were sealed cans on it until the run last week, and nobody signed them back in.”

Sam knew which run.

“Whose stores?”

“Not ours, sir. That’s all the board says.” The corporal smiled. “Mostly he just turns up a part somebody put back in the wrong bin.”

A staff sergeant’s voice struck the side of one container. “Get back in there.”

The corporal lowered the hoses. He stopped smiling before he turned toward the sound.

Six Marines stood in a strip of shade beside the open container. Both steel doors were folded against its outer walls and hooked in place. Staff Sergeant Keene had a clipboard at the entrance, the same man who had handed Sam a Kevlar and a movement sheet on the first morning, his utilities as clean as they had been then. A lance corporal stood three steps outside it with dust on both knees and one hand pressed below his ribs.

“Staff Sergeant, I can’t—”

“You can.” The staff sergeant pointed into the container with the clipboard. “You laid out those bins. You wrote the list. Go find the couplings.”

The lance corporal looked into the dark length of the container. White inventory numbers marked the shelves on both sides. The nearest rows were filters and belts. The deeper rows disappeared past the doorway light.

Sam knew the lance corporal. He was the driver Sam sat with at the wash rack. His vehicle killed the girl, and he rode the rest of the run in the back, into the yard and out again. Two evenings earlier, they had walked the outside edge of the water point until the Marine decided he had said enough. Sam could not repeat what the Marine had told him. He could not repeat what Len had told him on the lane either, and Len had been down the same road again two days after him.

The staff sergeant turned to the other Marines. “Nobody goes anywhere until he brings out the couplings. Maybe an audience will help him remember how to work.”

One Marine braced a wooden crate against his thigh. Another had a hand truck tilted back beneath two boxes. Neither moved.

The lance corporal stepped into the container.

Heat stayed inside the steel even with both doors open. He passed the first shelf, then the second. His right hand found the edge of a bin and did not move.

“Rear left,” the staff sergeant called. “You know where they are.”

The lance corporal took another step. His shoulders lifted. He turned too quickly, struck one shelf with his forearm, and came back into the light.

The staff sergeant moved aside just enough to let him pass. “Again. If you can’t walk ten feet into a box, you’re no use to this section.”

The Marine came out onto open ground and halted. His mouth worked around breaths that did not seem to reach the rest of him.

“Staff Sergeant,” Sam said.

The staff sergeant looked over. “Sir.”

“Give him a minute outside.”

“He’s had three.”

“Then give him another minute.”

The staff sergeant shifted the clipboard to his other hand. “This inventory was due before noon. He built the bin map, and now everybody else is waiting because he won’t walk to the back of a box.”

The lance corporal stared at the ground. His fingers opened and closed once against his trousers.

“The others don’t need to stand here,” Sam said.

“They are working.” The staff sergeant faced the section. “They’re learning what happens when one Marine makes everybody else do his job.”

No one looked at the lance corporal. That made the audience worse.

The lance corporal was still fighting for air. Whatever Sam said to Keene could wait until the Marine was clear of the next order.

Sam stepped far enough into the staff sergeant’s sight that the man had to address him or turn away. He left the path out of the container open. “He can’t continue in there right now.”

“He says he’s fine.”

The lance corporal had said no such thing within Sam’s hearing.

“Did he?”

“He said it this morning,” the staff sergeant said.

“He is not fine now.”

“With respect, sir, this is my section.”

“I know whose section it is.” Sam kept his voice level. “Let him step away.”

The staff sergeant looked at the cross on Sam’s collar, then at the Marines by the container. The lance corporal still had one hand under his ribs. “Take him to the water point,” the staff sergeant told the corporal nearest him. “Five minutes.”

The corporal set down the crate and moved beside the lance corporal without touching him. They walked over to the shade at the water point. The lance corporal did not look at Sam.

The staff sergeant turned back to the others. “Put the hand truck down. Stay here.”

The Marine eased the hand truck upright and left both boxes on it.

The lance corporal could leave. The others still had to watch.

“Walk with me, Staff Sergeant.”

The staff sergeant put the clipboard under his arm. They moved several paces along the container row and stopped where they could see the open doors. Sam stood beside him rather than directly in front of him. The steel wall gave back the day’s heat.

“I have a manifest due,” the staff sergeant said. “He knows that container better than anyone in the section. He numbered the shelves and corrected half the inventory after the last handoff. Today he keeps walking out.”

“Then the count takes longer.”

“The count is already late. The resupply truck leaves this afternoon. If those couplings are sitting in that container and I report a shortage, the lift stays down while we wait for somebody to send us more of what we’ve already got.”

“That does not explain the audience.”

The staff sergeant pulled the clipboard from beneath his arm. His thumb pressed against the metal clip. “I told them to wait because I was tired of one Marine stopping everybody’s work.”

Sam looked across at the water point. The lance corporal was under the shade cloth with both hands around a canteen cup. The water point was the one green place on the camp, cattails and reeds along a bank where the river ran gray-green and slow. The containers behind it were the Marines’ own, tan, built to be flown in or trucked, nothing like the one with the shipping colors under its paint. The corporal who had walked with him kept several feet away, facing the yard.

“You know why he can’t stay in that container,” Sam said.

The staff sergeant’s eyebrows lifted. His hand dropped from the clip. “No, sir.” He glanced at the water point and back. “I know he keeps coming out.”

“You never asked him?”

“I asked why he kept leaving the inventory. He said he was fine.” The staff sergeant rubbed his thumb along the clipboard’s edge. “I thought he wanted somebody else to do it.”

“But you could see he couldn’t catch his breath.”

“I saw it.”

“So you made everyone wait?”

“I thought if everybody had to wait on him, he’d quit coming out.”

Sam looked again toward the water point. The staff sergeant might be lying. But Sam had accused him as though the two of them had heard the same private conversation.

“I shouldn’t have said you knew the reason,” Sam said. “You were wrong to make the others watch.”

The staff sergeant’s grip on the clipboard eased, though he did not answer.

“Who else can finish the inventory?”

The staff sergeant looked over at the corporal beside the pallet where Sam had begun. The two hydraulic hoses still rested across the corporal’s arms. “He can read the map,” the staff sergeant said. “He won’t know where the lance corporal put everything.”

“Then what will you do?”

The staff sergeant did not answer at once. Sam waited.

“Put him inside,” the staff sergeant said. “Keep the lance corporal outside when he comes back. I can read the item, and he can call the shelf number from the shade.”

“All right.”

The staff sergeant started toward the container, then stopped. “Can you tell me if this will happen again?”

“No.”

The staff sergeant did not answer.

Sam waited before continuing. “Ask him privately what work he can do. If he wants me there when you talk, I’ll come. I can’t tell you why this happens.”

“I didn’t ask you to tell me anything private.”

“No,” Sam agreed. “You didn’t.”

They walked back to the section.

The staff sergeant pointed to the ground beside the container. “Take the boxes to receiving. You two finish the tire count.” One Marine guided the hand truck to receiving while the other two crossed to the tire stacks. He tipped the clipboard at the corporal with the hoses. “Set those down. Start at the first line he hasn’t checked.”

The corporal placed the hoses on the pallet and came to the container.

“When he comes back,” the staff sergeant continued, “he stays outside. I read the item, he calls the bin, and you count what’s there before you move anything.”

“Aye, Staff Sergeant.”

The corporal entered the container. The staff sergeant stood at the doorway while he read the first number from the sheet. No one else stopped to watch.

At the water point, the lance corporal had lowered the cup. His breathing had slowed enough that his shoulders no longer rose with each breath. The other corporal stayed near him, looking out across the yard.

Sam did not approach. The lance corporal had already been made visible to too many people.

The two Marines returned after several minutes. The staff sergeant met them halfway between the water point and the container. He spoke quietly enough that Sam could not hear him. He pointed first to the strip of shade beside the entrance, then to the corporal working inside. The lance corporal listened without looking toward the others.

When the staff sergeant read the next item, the lance corporal answered from the shade. “Rear right. Fourth shelf. Blue mark.”

The corporal disappeared deeper into the container and returned with a coupling in each hand. He held them up for the lance corporal to see.

“Those are the ones,” the lance corporal said.

The staff sergeant marked the inventory sheet.

Sam returned to the pallet. The two hoses lay where the corporal had placed them, their couplings turned in opposite directions.

Sam continued his circuit. He kept seeing the staff sergeant’s surprised face.
