A Use for Good MenChapter Nine

Chapter Nine

Len at the Door

Len stood when Sam entered the waiting room. He wore a blue shirt with a crease down one sleeve and had a paper cup between both hands. By his chair was a small overnight bag. He had sat where he could see the door. He was heavier through the neck and shoulders than the staff sergeant Sam remembered. The hair that had been shaved to the scalp was gray now, cut short at the sides. The broad face was the same. He had his chin down.

For a moment Sam saw the man who used to stand outside the tent until the person inside left. Then Len set down the cup and crossed to him.

“You got taller,” Sam said.

“You didn’t.”

They embraced awkwardly. Len’s shoulder pressed against his cheek, solid and warm. Sam held on a moment longer than he meant to. When they separated, Len bent to collect the bag.

“I should have called first.”

“You don’t have to apologize for coming.” Sam bent for the bag. Len lifted it before he could take it.

“Is there somewhere we can talk?”

Len picked up his cup and followed Sam past the meeting room. A counselor raised a hand from her doorway, and Sam nodded and kept walking. In his office he moved the extra chair away from the wall and closed the door.

“I flew up yesterday,” Len said. “Denise found me a hotel. I kept telling her I’d call you, so she found me a hotel.”

“How is she?”

“Ready for me to come home with something useful to say.”

Sam sat behind the desk, then brought his chair around to Len’s side. He was conscious of doing it, of the small arrangements by which he used to tell a man he could begin. Len let him do it. He had set the bag between his feet and his hands flat on his knees. He watched Sam, not the chair.

“I’m not here for counseling,” Len said.

“All right.”

“I did the job.”

Sam looked at the bag between Len’s feet. He had expected trouble with the offer, perhaps an argument with Denise about taking it. He had not thought beyond Saturday.

“The transport work?”

“Yes. Finished it. They paid me this morning.” Len took his phone from his pocket but left the screen dark. “I need you to look at something.”

Sam moved the laptop over to them. “Show me.”

Len found a link and sent it. Before opening it, Sam read the headline in the preview: Guard units burn families out of coastal towns in Meredan. A news service he recognized. He knew little about the country beyond election arguments and a picture of its president shaking hands with someone in Washington.

“Was the shipment going to Meredan?”

“To the Meredan Regional Guard. That’s where Rusk says the shipment was going.”

“Who’s Rusk?”

“Owen Rusk. Delmere’s transport manager. The man I met Friday.” Len touched the phone screen to stop it going dark. “I’d like you to read it first.”

Sam opened the report.

In the first photograph, one corner of a metal roof folded down into a room. A mortar round had come through it in the night, the report said. Clothes hung from a line in the yard. Beyond the house, sunlight shone on water Sam took at first for a river. The caption said it was the sea.

A correspondent described visiting three towns after Guard units forced the residents out at gunpoint. Adults interviewed along the road said soldiers burned houses, seized fishing boats and kept people from returning. One man went back for his mother’s medication. At a checkpoint a soldier emptied his bag and told him to keep walking away from town.

The Guard’s spokesman said the coastal settlements sheltered armed smugglers. Moving the population, he said, would allow his men to make the area safe. The correspondent saw empty streets, demolished houses and soldiers turning residents back at the road. The report was dated the previous Tuesday.

Sam scrolled to the bottom for the clothes on the line. There was no further mention of the house.

Len watched him and said nothing.

“You helped send them weapons?”

“Ammunition. Sealed cans, loaded on the trucks in with the relief stores. I knew what the cans were before the cargo supervisor told me Saturday morning. I’d moved cans like that for two years in the service. I didn’t open anything, and I didn’t go with the trucks.”

Ammunition, in with the relief stores. Sam knew that cargo. He had ridden ahead of a load like it once, under canvas on a fourth vehicle, and asked whose it was. He still had no answer.

“Did you know then who it was for?”

“No. Rusk told me it was a government-supported recovery program. Coastal relief. He said the customer had approved the shipment. I took the customer to be the program. There were cargo specialists in the yard to handle it. My job was the trucks, when they arrived, and calling drivers when the times changed. The same work he offered me.”

Sam turned the laptop slightly away so he could look at Len and not at the house beyond his shoulder.

“Was anyone from your regular job involved?”

“No people. No vehicles. I took the time off, like we talked about. This was a different yard.”

“Did you ask Rusk about the ammunition?”

“I did. So did one of the workers. He asked me if we were allowed to be doing this there.” Len rubbed a thumb along the phone’s edge. “I told him what Rusk told me. Government work. People there whose job was to handle it. He went back to work, and I went back to mine.”

Sam could picture Len speaking to the man. He could almost hear the reassuring impatience of someone who knew how to get a day finished.

“What made you ask who was getting it?”

“Rusk offered me more days. He wanted an answer before this morning. I thought if I was going to keep doing it, I should know who we were supplying. He told me Saturday night. Said I could read about the program myself.”

“And you found this.”

“Denise did. I found the embassy pictures.”

Len sent another link. Sam opened it in a separate window. On a U.S. embassy page, an American official appeared with the Guard’s commander, whom the report had photographed at a checkpoint, in front of several vehicles. The accompanying text described training and equipment for Meredan’s security forces and support for its coastal recovery program, as part of continuing security cooperation. The date was in June.

“There,” Len insisted. “I didn’t make that up.”

“I didn’t think you did.”

“The first thing Denise asked was if Rusk was lying about the government. I showed her this. She said that wasn’t the only thing to worry about.”

Sam read the short embassy account again. Security forces, training and equipment, the coastal recovery program. The Guard was not named in it. Nothing on the page said who had authorized Len’s shipment, and nothing mentioned the families in the report. Both pages lay open, the official smiling in one and the ruined house in the other.

“I can’t tell you from this if Saturday’s shipment was legal,” he said.

“I know you’re not a lawyer.” Len leaned forward. “You’ve worked with these people. Your organization sent me to them. Are they really helping that force?”

“Rusk told you they were.”

“Yes. And now I’d like somebody else to ask him.”

Sam put his palm flat on the desk, next to Len’s cup, and left it there. His laptop had his notes for Tuesday, when his team was due at Alderwick to join the model to ADICUS. In two weeks, Delmere could be running the complete model on people Civic Passage had never met. He remembered Ruth outside the meeting room, one hand against the wall, telling him the client had asked what she would charge. Before she left, she was planning to ask the company to pay for her training. He wanted more people to come back with news like that. Sam looked again at the clothes drying in the yard. Somebody had pinned them up expecting to be back for them by evening.

“How much more work did he offer?”

“Enough that I wanted to say yes.”

“Have you?”

“No. I’m supposed to call him.”

Len lifted the cup from beside Sam’s keyboard, discovered it was empty, and set it down again.

“We could use that money,” he admitted. “I’ve been doing the arithmetic since Saturday. I can make it come out any way I want if I leave enough things out.”

Sam took out his own phone. “We can ask him now, if that’s what you want. With you here.”

Len looked at it. “Don’t tell him I came for you to straighten me out.”

“I’ll tell him you asked me to help you find out what you did.”

“I know what I did. I finished the job.”

Sam lowered the phone. “Then I’ll ask him who authorized the shipment and if Delmere is continuing to supply the Guard.”

“Yes,” Len said. “Ask him that.”

Len read out the number. Sam called and put the phone on speaker between them.

Rusk answered with his surname. Sam introduced himself, said Len was with him and explained why they were calling.

“I thought we’d covered this, Len,” Rusk said.

“We talked about the money and the dates. I’m asking about the Guard.”

“They’re a partner force. We work for people who make those decisions.”

“Who made this one?” Sam asked.

“I’m not discussing a customer’s contract on the telephone with someone I haven’t met.”

“My organization referred Len. We’re being asked to help you recruit more people. I need to know what we’re helping you do.”

“Then your management should speak to the head of Mission Support. That’s the Delmere division I work for.”

“Is Delmere continuing to supply the Guard?” Sam asked.

“Yes,” Rusk said. “We have work scheduled. Len was briefed on the recipient before accepting Saturday’s assignment. If he’s changed his mind about future work, he can tell me that himself.”

Len straightened. “You told me Saturday night.”

“I explained the government customer before you agreed.”

“You didn’t say Meredan. You didn’t say the Guard. You told me after I finished.”

Sam pointed to Len’s phone. Len opened Rusk’s messages and turned the screen to him.

“Your message identifying the Guard came at eight forty-seven Saturday evening,” Sam said.

“That doesn’t mean it was the first time we discussed it.”

Len’s hand closed on his knee. “It was the first time you told me.”

Rusk paused a moment. “Len, nobody is holding your pay. You did the work we agreed to. If you don’t want another assignment, don’t take one.”

“Will you correct what you just said?” Sam pressed. “About when he knew the recipient?”

“I’ll discuss the account of the work with our management. They can speak to yours.”

“You can correct it here. Len is listening.”

“I have nothing to add,” Rusk said.

Sam glanced at Len. Len was looking at the phone. “I ran your yard on Saturday,” he said. “I called your drivers every time your times changed. Say that part to your management too.” Rusk said nothing.

“I’ll contact James Balan,” Sam said. “Someone from Mission Support needs to answer these questions before we do any more work connecting our model to ADICUS.”

“James knows how to reach us.”

The call ended. Sam left the phone where it was. He could hear a cart rolling along the corridor, one wheel ticking at every turn.

Len opened the Saturday messages and held them out. His question was above Rusk’s answer: Before I agree to more days, who is the shipment for?

Sam read the reply again. Rusk identified the Guard and described the program as government-supported. Then he answered something Len had not asked. The cargo had come into the country as relief, he wrote, listed with the relief stores on the program’s own paperwork, and customs cleared relief at the port unopened. There had been no delay and there would be none. He had written it to settle a worker who was asking questions. He did not refer to an earlier conversation about Meredan. The messages fit Len’s account. Rusk could tell other people he discussed it first.

“Why would he say that with me sitting here?” Len asked.

Sam wanted to answer. He thought of the account Rusk could send to his employer, with a qualified man knowingly taking a job and raising objections only after he was paid. Other people would have to choose between the two accounts, and most of them would never sit in a room with Len.

“I don’t know,” Sam said. “But he may tell other people the same thing. I’d like you to speak to a lawyer who doesn’t work for us or Delmere.”

Len looked down at the messages. “I didn’t come here planning to sue anybody.”

“You don’t have to decide that to get advice.”

Sam stood to fetch water from the small pitcher on the cabinet. His legs felt stiff. He filled two glasses and brought them back.

“I want Graham and Janet to hear about this,” he said. “They can arrange help. Graham has to deal with Delmere about the model too.”

“Tell them about Saturday. Show them the messages.” Len took the glass. “Leave the old things where they are.”

“I will.”

Len forwarded the messages, and Sam watched them arrive before he wrote to Janet. Len was with him. The assignment was finished. Rusk was telling a different story about when Len had been told, and here was the report. Would she come, and arrange a time with Graham. Len read it over his shoulder.

“You can say I got paid,” Len put in.

Sam added that. He did not ask about the money.

Then he wrote James one line asking him to call before Tuesday.

“I thought you’d be pleased to hear from me,” Len said.

“I was. I am.”

“I know. I wish I’d called about something else.”

Sam sat beside him again. Len had the glass in his two hands, as he had had the cup, and was looking at his phone where it lay dark on the desk. The crease ran the whole length of the sleeve. He wanted to hear about the years in which neither of them called. He wanted the two of them to have done better than meet like this, after another day’s work neither could take back.

“Are you still doing weddings?” Len asked.

“Sometimes. Laura had one last month. I carried things.”

“That sounds about right.”

Sam smiled. A knock came at the door. He looked at Len, then went to let Janet in.
