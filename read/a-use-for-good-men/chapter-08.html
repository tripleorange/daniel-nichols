A Use for Good MenChapter Eight

Chapter Eight

The Tent

Sam asked the Marine how he knew when a cargo strap had to be taken out of use.

He had settled in the shade at the mouth of a disused concrete loading bay while two Marines worked at the rear axle of a truck. The bay was left over from the airfield’s earlier life, a concrete tunnel that stayed cool for an hour after dawn and then no longer. The third Marine brought him a strap and showed him the frayed place, then walked him through the points he checked and how the delayed replacement shipment affected the work. Sam asked two more questions. The Marine leaned against the outside face of the wall and answered them while looking out at the vehicle lane.

Sam listened. The Marine knew his work, and the strap mattered.

After the others rolled a tool cart over to the next vehicle, the Marine said, “You got a minute, Padre?”

Sam did not ask for the subject within hearing of the work lane. “I’ve got one.”

He stayed just inside the bay. The Marine stayed outside it, the concrete corner between them, close enough to speak quietly without having to face each other. At first the Marine sent his words toward the open yard. Sam answered the same way. Only after a while did the Marine turn his head when he spoke.

Sam’s notebook stayed closed in his breast pocket.

Fifty-three minutes later, the Marine pushed away from the wall. He rubbed both hands against his trousers, looked once at the truck, and returned to work without asking Sam to follow.

Sam did not leave the edge of the bay until the Marine was in the vehicle lane.

In the spring, when the airfield was new to him, availability had meant a time and a place. Now, in his fifth month, a Marine could find him from reveille until he lay down. The first man he asked usually knew where.

A generator carried the same low mechanical note it had carried all morning. For fifty-three minutes, Marines had started vehicles beyond the loading bay, called for ground guides, and worked without him.

His Religious Program Specialist approached through the vehicle lane with Sam’s schedule card in one hand.

“You told me noon,” Sam said.

The RP held up his watch.

Sam looked at his own. “That can’t be right.”

“I’ve been saying that since thirteen hundred, sir.”

“About the watch?”

“About today.”

The RP stopped at the concrete corner. He was a sailor assigned to work with Marines, as Sam was, but he carried the weapon Sam did not. He managed movement, equipment, schedules, and the practical work of keeping an unarmed chaplain alive. Lately he had also begun managing the number of people who could get to Sam at once. He was short and thick through the shoulders, in the new digital desert pattern. He carried more confidence than six weeks of training had given him. Sam had seen the fear under it more than once.

He handed over the schedule card. Three names had been moved into the margin. Another entry carried an arrow toward the evening.

“Who did I miss?”

“No one. Yet.”

Sam read the top line again. He knew each abbreviation and even so could not remember which appointment came next.

“The supply officer can wait,” he said. “Move the service walk-through to fifteen hundred. I can take the next one now.”

“The service walk-through is at fifteen hundred.”

Sam checked the card. “Sorry. I misread it.”

The RP took the card back. “Come on, sir.”

Sam lifted the canteen from the floor of the loading bay and tipped it. The cap clicked against his upper lip. Nothing came out.

The RP watched him lower it. “Bring that.”

They started across the support area. The camp had been an airfield before it was theirs, one long strip weathered and drifted over with dirt. The hangars along it had walls thick enough to be called bomb-proof, until the Americans brought bombs that went through them. Operations was inside one of them, the only place on the field with white bulbs strung overhead, because nothing of its inside showed from outside.

Navy chaplains called it deckplate ministry. Here the deck was dirt, plywood, gravel, and the metal footholds of parked vehicles. Sam stayed near the work and returned until seeing him did not announce trouble.

That morning, three Marines asked Sam for a minute before breakfast. The first conversation lasted through the opening of the chow line. Another Marine found him while he carried a box of service supplies between work areas. By noon, his meal sat unopened beside the RP’s gear while he moved a scheduled meeting to make room for someone who had finally approached him near the water point.

The Marines did not appear to regard any of this as extraordinary. They waited, joked with the RP, borrowed his tape, and returned to work.

A lance corporal crossing between vehicles raised two fingers. “Service still at nineteen, Padre?”

“Nineteen,” Sam called.

The RP looked at him.

“What?”

“Eighteen thirty.”

Sam turned back. “Eighteen thirty.”

The lance corporal changed direction without breaking stride and shouted the correction toward two Marines near the pallets.

At the water point, a corporal Sam recognized but could not place touched the brim of his cover. “You got a minute later, sir?”

“After the walk-through.”

The RP answered at the same time. “Seventeen thirty.”

The corporal looked between them.

“Seventeen thirty,” Sam repeated.

“I’ll find you, sir.”

They continued. Sam waited until the corporal was out of hearing. “You know, when people ask for the chaplain, they generally prefer an answer from the chaplain.”

“They prefer the correct time from anybody.”

The RP paused at a water can and filled Sam’s canteen. While the water ran, a sergeant approached with a question about the next morning’s movement brief. The RP answered it from memory. A Marine outside the communications shelter held up a small cardboard box of devotional booklets and asked where Sam wanted them. The RP pointed him to a covered pallet near the service supplies.

Sam drank half the canteen too quickly. The water was warm and tasted faintly of the container. He wiped his mouth with the back of his hand.

“Better,” he said.

“You haven’t eaten,” the RP said.

“I had crackers.”

“You opened crackers.”

Sam remembered the split plastic packet beside the RP’s gear, but not eating the crackers.

The RP dug into a cargo pocket and handed him a wrapped oat bar. “Eat that before you lie down.”

“You carry emergency chaplain food now?”

“I carry emergency food. You keep turning it into chaplain food.”

Sam opened the wrapper. The first bite dried his mouth enough that he needed the canteen again, but he finished the bar while the RP watched.

The RP led him away from the vehicle lanes to a small canvas shelter used for spare gear and occasional sleep. Someone had cleared one side for a folding cot. A poncho liner lay rolled at its foot, and a cardboard carton marked for printer paper served as a table.

“Twenty minutes,” the RP said.

Sam stopped outside the flap. “I have the walk-through.”

“In forty-eight minutes.”

“There are three names in the margin.”

“There were four. I moved one.”

Sam looked at the card in the RP’s hand. “You can’t move people without telling me.”

“I am telling you.”

“Before you move them.”

“You were with someone,” the RP said.

The RP opened the flap. “Twenty minutes, sir. I’ll be here.”

“Is that an order?”

“No, sir.” He considered the question. “It’s logistics.”

Sam stepped into the tent.

He set the canteen on the carton and took the closed notebook from his breast pocket before placing it alongside the canteen. The cot complained when he sat. He kept his boots on and lay back with one forearm over his eyes.

The RP lowered the flap but did not fasten it. A band of white light remained along the ground. Through it Sam could see the RP’s boots turn toward the open support area.

The generator’s noise filled the tent. Canvas lifted and settled above him. A forklift reversed somewhere beyond the communications shelter, its warning tone disappearing behind the engine noise and returning when the vehicle turned.

Sam counted his breaths until he realized he had started counting the seconds between sounds instead. He stopped at forty-seven. His shoulder blades pressed against two different metal bars beneath the cot fabric.

Heat gathered beneath the forearm over his eyes. Grit worked against one eyelid when he blinked. The muscles along his lower back kept tightening as if the cot required him to balance on it.

He began the Lord’s Prayer without moving his lips. He came to daily bread and remembered the oat bar. He started again, made it to debts, and saw the six pencil marks along the schedule card.

Sam lowered his arm and stared at the canvas roof. Forgetting the next line of a prayer he had spoken since childhood meant he was tired. He began a third time anyway.

The loading bay where the first Marine had approached him was out of sight. Three names remained in the margin. The corporal would find Sam at seventeen thirty. Service was at eighteen thirty, not nineteen.

None of those facts required him to stand.

His right hand loosened against his chest.

Boots approached outside.

They slowed near the RP, then stopped.

“RP. Chaplain in there?”

Sam opened his eyes.

The RP’s answer came at once. “The chaplain is unavailable.”

The cot fabric tightened as Sam gripped its edge.

Outside, the Marine shifted his weight. Gravel moved beneath one boot.

“He’ll be available at sixteen hundred,” the RP went on. “I can put you down, or I can come find you.”

There was a pause long enough for Sam to picture the flap lifted and the Marine looking in.

It did not move.

“Sixteen hundred’s fine.”

The RP asked for something too quietly for Sam to hear. A truck passed between the tent and the generator, carrying the reply away. Sam heard the scratch of the RP’s pencil against the schedule card.

“I’ll make sure he knows,” the RP said.

“Thanks.”

The boots turned and receded toward the vehicle line.

Sam was relieved. His fingers released the cot. His shoulders settled. For several seconds, he wanted only the footsteps to keep going.

Then he heard his own breathing and hated that he was grateful not to be needed for a few more minutes.

He could stand. He could lift the flap, call the Marine back, and listen to whatever the Marine wanted to tell him. The RP would not physically stop him.

The white band under the canvas stayed empty.

Sam lay with his arm over his eyes.

The generator continued at the same pitch. Once, a shadow crossed the strip of light and the RP’s boots shifted to let someone pass. No one else stopped.

Sam did not sleep. He lost the order of the sounds for a while, which was close enough.

The flap lifted.

“Time, sir.”

The RP was outside with the schedule card. Sam sat, and the cot frame clicked beneath him. His canteen was on the carton where he had left it.

He took the canteen and drank. “The Marine who came by.”

“Sixteen hundred. I have him.”

“Did he say what he needed?”

“No, sir. I didn’t ask.”

Sam put his feet beneath him. “Did he seem—” He stopped.

“He agreed to sixteen hundred,” the RP said.

Sam stood. The brief rest had not restored him. The schedule card no longer blurred when he read it.

The RP had drawn a box around sixteen hundred and written a name inside it. As two Marines passed close to the tent, he covered the entry with his thumb before handing the card over.

Sam slipped the card into his breast pocket. He picked up the notebook, checked that it was closed, and followed the RP into the heat.

The service walk-through was forty yards away. The RP pointed Sam to it and turned back to the active work lanes, where he would be when the Marine returned.

Sam had rested for twenty minutes. The Marine had a time. The request had not been lost.

You heard him ask.
