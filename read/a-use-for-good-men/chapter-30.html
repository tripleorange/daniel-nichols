Chapter Thirty

The Road Together

Don put his bag in the back seat and tried to move the passenger seat forward. Nothing happened. He bent to find the lever while Sam stood over the open trunk with a container of sandwiches.

“It’s electric,” Sam said.

“That hasn’t improved it.”

Sam leaned through the driver’s door and pressed the start button. Don’s seat moved with a low whine, stopping only when his knees nearly touched the glove compartment.

“Now I can reach everything,” Don said.

Ellen came down the porch steps carrying two travel mugs. Across the road, the trees were dark. She had pulled a coat on over the shirt she had slept in and left it open. Her breath showed in the porch light. The grass was gray with wet, and the cold came up through his shoes.

Sam set the sandwiches on the back seat. Ellen handed him the other cup and touched his arm.

“Call when you get there.”

“I will. Thank you for having me.”

“Come when you can stay longer.”

He had said he would before. She left the invitation there and turned to Don, who had got out to kiss her goodbye.

At six twenty they pulled away. Sam watched the house in the mirror until the bend took it out of sight. Don opened the directions on his phone, then set it low between them so the illuminated map would not shine against the windshield.

For the first hour they spoke about the road. Don told Sam the next exit had been rebuilt since they were boys, then realized they had already passed the one he meant.

The sky went gray on Don’s side of the car, then white. Fog lay in the low fields, and the cattle stood in it up to their bellies. Don read the names off the exit signs as they came, towns where one or the other of them had played a ball game or been driven to a funeral, and Sam could put a year to most of them. He had not driven this road in daylight with anybody in the passenger seat since the children were small enough to sleep across the back.

By the time the sun was on the passenger window, Don was no longer giving directions. He held his coffee in both hands and watched the hills.

“What happened with the boy?” Sam asked.

“Three days at home. The mother wanted the term.”

“And the one with the stitches?”

“Marla said he came in this morning and asked if the boy who pulled him was in trouble.”

Sam watched the truck ahead. He was glad the boy had somebody to ask.

They stopped when Don asked, although Sam would have driven to the next service area. When he came back from the restroom, Don was standing by the driver’s door.

“I’d like a turn.”

Sam moved the passenger seat back and tried to settle his shoulders. The white lines at the edge of the road appeared to pass too close to his door. Twice he moved his right foot toward a brake that wasn’t there.

Don noticed the second time.

“Are we stopping?”

“No.”

“Let me know if we are.”

He had sent Laura their departure time before leaving. Her reply came while Don was driving: Thanks. I’m taking the drawings to the venue this morning. Call at lunch if you’re stopped.

He wrote that he would. Then he checked for a message from Janet. There was none. He had no idea what Nadia was doing now.

“You can close your eyes,” Don said.

“I’m looking at something.”

“All right.”

Don kept driving. After a while Sam put the phone on his thigh. When he next opened his eyes, they were passing a sign he did not remember approaching.

❦

Priya called as Don pulled into a service station late in the morning. Sam waited until the car stopped before answering.

“Ruth Bell’s asked to speak to you,” she said. “Delmere called her yesterday. She’s sent me the offer.”

Sam sat straighter. “For the Guard?”

“The recruiter didn’t say. Ruth asked if we were involved. I’ve told her we didn’t arrange it.”

“Does Jasleen know?”

“I’ve sent her the email. Ruth wants to talk to you before she answers them.”

Don pointed to the shop and went inside.

Priya brought Ruth onto the call. Sam remembered her standing outside the meeting room with one hand against the wall, surprised that somebody had asked what she charged.

“Priya said you’re away,” Ruth said.

“I’m on my way back. A friend’s driving.”

“I’m sorry to catch you on the road.”

“We’re stopped. Tell me what they offered.”

“They want me to help plan deliveries to sites where they’re running short of supplies. Which deliveries go first, and the routes when a road closes. The recruiter had read the summary you gave me.” She paused. “Most people need me to explain how that experience could be useful to them. She was already telling me what she wanted my help with.”

Sam looked through the windshield at a man testing the pressure in a tire. He could tell Ruth not to answer the recruiter. That would be a very short conversation, and she would have the offer when he hung up.

“Did they tell you who would receive the deliveries?”

“They said government sites in Meredan. The first part could be done from here. I asked about the client, and she said the manager could explain more on the next call.”

“Ruth, I saw a proposal last week to ask you to plan supplies for the Meredan Regional Guard. I objected. James, the engineer I was working with, told them not to contact you.”

“You saw my name?”

“Yes.”

“Why didn’t somebody tell me?”

Sam looked down at his hand against the seat. James had sent the instruction in front of them. Sam had carried that small success into the rest of the morning and never asked what Ruth ought to know.

“You saw it and you let me take that call,” Ruth pressed.

“I should have asked Priya to contact you,” he admitted. “I thought we’d stopped the approach.”

“I haven’t agreed to anything. But I spent an hour last night trying to decide if I could give up another pharmacy shift.”

“I’m sorry.”

“What is it about the Guard?” Ruth asked.

“They went through the towns on that coast and drove the people out. They burned the houses behind them so there’d be nothing to come back to. I’ve read the reports. The man who runs Delmere’s Mission Support division told me he knew, and that Delmere would go on supplying them. The shipments go out as coastal relief. The Guard’s ammunition rides in the same trucks, under the relief paperwork. The man who ran the yard one Saturday learned who it was for that night, from a message on his phone. I’ve read it.”

“And you think this is the same job?”

“It sounds like it. I want to put the job description they sent you next to the request their recruiter was filling, the one that named the Guard. I’m going to Alderwick tomorrow morning to examine what they intend to do with the model.”

“For Civic Passage?”

“The directors asked me to come. I don’t work there anymore.”

Ruth was silent. Priya did not fill it for him.

“Did you leave over this?” Ruth asked.

“They fired me. I replaced a colleague’s program with an older copy I knew wouldn’t work, to stop the installation for a morning.”

“Without telling him?”

“Yes. He’d asked me to look at it that morning. He thought I was helping him.”

The service station’s loudspeaker called someone back to a pump.

“Priya still works there,” Ruth said.

“Yes.”

“Then I’d like her to keep helping me with this.”

“I will,” Priya said. “I’ll tell your counselor what you’ve been offered too.”

“I don’t want to lose the work I’ve already got,” Ruth said. “The distributor asked me back to help their managers practice the plan. I’m finally getting paid to do something I know how to do. I’d like more of that.”

“There’s nothing wrong with the work you did for them,” Sam told her. “I’m worried about the job Delmere is offering you now. You deserve to know who you’d be helping before you decide.”

He could hear her draw a breath. When she spoke again, she sounded tired.

“Can you find out tomorrow if this is work for the Guard?”

“I can ask Delmere to show the request behind that call, and put it beside what you were sent. Then I can show the directors what in your record made the model pick you. For that, Alderwick would need the records you gave Civic Passage, and your permission to use them.”

“For tomorrow,” Ruth said. “I don’t want them taking this as agreement to look for other jobs for me.”

“I’ll make that clear when I send it,” Priya said. “The records are for this examination.”

“Then yes. Send them,” Ruth said. “I want the directors to see exactly what I was offered. If it is work for the Guard, I want Delmere to explain why they didn’t tell me that when they called.”

Ruth asked about canceling the next call with the recruiter.

“Would you be willing to wait until we’ve spoken tomorrow?” Sam asked.

“I’ll tell her I need more time. She can wait a day.”

After Ruth left the call, Priya said she would send the records and the offer to Alderwick with Ruth’s written permission.

“How’s Aaron?”

“You can ask him tomorrow.”

“I will.”

Sam ended the call. Don came out of the shop carrying a paper sack and stopped at the car until Sam opened the door.

“They were out of most things,” Don said. “I got bananas. And something that may once have been a cheese biscuit.”

Sam took the sack. “We have sandwiches.”

“I remembered those after I paid.”

They ate at a table beyond the pumps. It was a concrete picnic table on a square of grass between the lot and the highway fence. The traffic behind rumbled as a light breeze rustled the paper the sandwiches had come in. Don’s sandwich had been cut in half, Sam’s in quarters. Sam turned one piece over, looking at it.

“She thinks you’ll forget you’re eating,” Don said.

“You know that?”

“She told me to hand you another piece.”

Sam laughed, then found he was hungry enough to finish all four. Don did not ask him who called. When Sam said there was something he could show the directors tomorrow, Don offered him more time before they left.

“I need to call Laura.”

“I’ll put gas in the car.”

❦

Laura answered from outside. Sam could hear traffic and the rattle of keys before she closed a door. He pictured her in the truck with the drawings rolled on the seat where he would have sat.

“We’re in Virginia,” he told her. “Don’s been driving. We stopped for lunch.”

“Did you eat anything?”

“Ellen packed sandwiches.”

“Then I’m confident you ate something.”

He could almost see the small movement at the corner of her mouth.

“How did the drawings go?”

“The manager liked them. The client wants another bed along the wall. I told her what it would cost, and she still wants it.”

“Good.”

“It is. I have to price the plants this afternoon.”

He watched Don lift the fuel nozzle from its holder. Laura sounded pleased. He could not remember when he had last asked to see her plans and not worried about where she would have to go to carry them out.

“I’d like to see the drawings,” he said.

“I’ll show you.”

“We’re going to stay near Alderwick tonight,” he went on. “I’ve booked two rooms. It means we won’t have the drive down from the house in the morning.”

“Have you already booked them?”

“Yes.”

“I wish you’d told me before I had to ask Ellen what you were doing.”

Sam looked at Don, then away. “I was going to send you the hotel information when we arrived.”

“I didn’t know whether to expect you here tonight, or whether you wanted me to come down. I knew you had to be at Alderwick in the morning. I didn’t know what you wanted to do before that.”

“I want to come home,” he said. “I don’t know what it will be like when I get there.”

Laura did not answer at once.

“I want you home,” she said. “I’m glad Don’s driving. I’m still angry about the way you left. You let me spend the afternoon thinking you’d be there when I got back. I came in and said your name before I saw the note.”

“I wasn’t sure you’d want to see me yet,” he said. “I thought if I sent you the address, you could decide without having to tell me no.”

“And I’d be wondering if you wanted me to come. You wouldn’t have asked.”

He stayed silent.

“I’d still like to see you,” Laura said. “I’ve missed you. That isn’t a question, so don’t think about it.”

He leaned forward, resting an elbow on the table. An engine started at the pumps and settled into a rough idle.

“Can I call when we’re finished tomorrow? Then we can decide about the evening.”

“Yes. I have work in the morning. I’ll keep the evening free.”

“You don’t have to—”

“I’d like to,” she cut in. “Please call me.”

He told her he would. Before they hung up, he asked which plants she was pricing. She named three, then explained why the one the client wanted would die against the wall.

Sam sent the hotel address after the call, with the time they expected to arrive.

❦

They were at the hotel a little after four. Sam had driven the last stretch while Don answered messages from the school. At the desk, the clerk asked if Sam was traveling for business, and he said yes before thinking about the answer.

Upstairs, Don left his bag inside his door and came back to agree on a time to eat. The two doors faced each other across a corridor narrow enough to talk across without stepping out. He needed to be at school on Monday. Ellen was looking at flights for him.

Sam took off his shoes and lay on the bed. A folded card on the pillow asked him to consider reusing his towels. He moved it to the bedside table and slept for most of an hour.

Sarah called while he was putting his shoes back on.

“Mom said you’re back up here.”

“Near Alderwick. In Virginia.”

“Is Don still with you?”

“Across the hall.”

“Good.” She paused. “I’m not ready to talk about Monday.”

Sam left one shoe untied. “All right.”

“I just wanted to call.”

“I’m glad you did. How was the exam?”

“Long. I knew some of it.”

“That’s encouraging.”

“I’d prefer to have known all of it.”

He smiled. Someone near her offered to fetch her something from downstairs. Sarah moved the phone away and answered, then came back.

“Tessa’s getting food. We have a film to watch for class. Three hours.”

“What film?”

She told him. He had seen it years ago and remembered very little except the ending. He was about to say so when she warned him not to tell her anything.

“I wasn’t going to.”

“You always say that right before you do.”

“I’ll keep quiet about it, then. Tell me what you think when you’ve seen it.”

They talked until Tessa returned. Sarah said she had to go, and he let her. She did not ask when he would visit. He stayed on the edge of the bed after the call, holding the loose lace between his fingers.

Sandra called before he went down to meet Don. Priya’s email had arrived.

“Victor’s got a demonstration ready for the morning,” Sandra said. “Three jobs Delmere wants filled, one from each team that will use the model, with the model’s picks for each. The one Priya sent is on his list.”

Sam stood. “The job they offered Ruth?”

“The request number matches. He was going to start with the other two.”

“Can we start with hers? I want the directors to compare what Ruth was told with the job Delmere was trying to fill.”

“I’ll tell him.”

“And the recruiter who called her. Victor can show us the screens, but she’s the one who spoke to Ruth.”

“I’ll ask.”

“And Aaron?”

“He’ll be here at eight. So will the directors.”

“Thank you.”

After the call, Sam took his notebook out of his bag, opened it to a clean page, and closed it again. Whatever he wrote tonight, tomorrow began with Aaron looking up when he came in.
