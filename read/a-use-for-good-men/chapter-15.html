Chapter Fifteen

Someone Outside

Sam parked behind Laura’s car and walked to the open side door. It was a farmhouse a family had kept up for weddings, white boards and a porch along the front, with cut fields behind it brown to the tree line. Inside the house, she was discussing where a tent pole would stand.

“People will put their bags there,” she said. “Then someone will trip over a bag instead of the pole.”

The woman with her made a mark on a drawing. Through the window behind them, Sam could see the lawn sloping down to a low stone wall. A tape measure lay open across the grass.

Laura looked up when he entered. She came around the table and took his hand.

“You took your time,” she said. She did not let go.

On the phone, earlier, she had told him a man was photographing her while she worked. She was inside with the owner of the venue, and she wanted him to come. Sam had closed his laptop and left Civic Passage.

The road out of Arlington had been slow for the first half hour and empty after that. He had not looked at the speedometer for the last of it. Laura had sounded like herself on the phone. She had told him where she was and asked him to come. She had not said hurry. He had kept the phone face up on the passenger seat and looked at it at every light. It had stayed dark.

“Is he still here?”

“Across the road.” Laura led him to the other window. “The gray car. Don’t go out there.”

A sedan was parked beyond the entrance, partly under a tree. One of its rear windows was open. Sam could make out a man in the driver’s seat, looking down at his lap.

“Did you get a picture of him?”

Laura took out her phone. “Twenty minutes ago.”

In her photograph, the man was by the car with a camera raised. He wore a dark jacket with a pale lining at the collar. The lens pointed at the lawn where Laura had been measuring. In the next image, his face was partly visible above the camera.

The owner joined them. She was about sixty, in a canvas work jacket, and her hands were rough from doing her own grounds. “I thought he was photographing the house. I called across and asked if he’d made an appointment. He got in the car.”

“Did he answer you?” Sam asked.

“No. I didn’t go any closer. Laura came inside.”

Laura put the phone on the table. “I’ve called the police. They’re sending someone when an officer is available.”

The owner nodded. “I have guests coming to look around at four, but you don’t have to leave. I’ll find us something to drink.”

She went through a doorway toward the back of the house. Laura picked up the drawing and moved it away from the window. Sam watched the car.

“What happened before you saw him?”

“We walked around the lawn. Talked about the tent. I came to the car for my tape and noticed him standing there.” She set the drawing with the sample books. “I’d seen that car outside the garden center this morning. I didn’t think anything of it then.”

“You’re sure it’s the same one?”

“The white patch on the back bumper caught my eye. I thought someone had backed into something freshly painted. Look at the photograph.”

Sam enlarged it. A broad white scrape crossed the gray bumper. It was visible on the car outside too.

He wanted to cross the road and ask the man whose pictures he was taking. He imagined the camera lowered, the man having to answer him. Sam put Laura’s phone down.

“Will you stay here while I call Dambrosky?”

“I’m already staying here.” Laura came to his side. “Why him?”

“I told him yesterday I wouldn’t help connect our model to Delmere’s recruiting system. Not for Mission Support, not for any part of Delmere. He knew I was going to ask Graham to stop all of it.”

“That doesn’t tell us who hired this man.”

“No. I’m going to ask him.”

Laura glanced at the car, then back to Sam. “Put it on speaker. I want to hear what he says.”

Sam moved away from the window and called. Dambrosky answered after several rings.

“Sam. Graham hasn’t spoken to you yet?”

“There’s a man photographing my wife at a work appointment. Did you ask someone to follow her?”

Dambrosky said nothing at first. Sam could hear another voice at his end, then the click of a door.

“Where are you?”

“With Laura. She’s listening.”

“I’d rather discuss this with you and Graham,” Dambrosky said.

“I’m the one being photographed,” Laura cut in. “I want to hear your answer.”

“You’ve been contacting people about work Delmere did before this partnership,” Dambrosky said. “People responsible for protecting that work have questions.”

The last people with questions about that work had been written down at a gate.

“My wife isn’t involved in your business,” Sam said.

“You discuss it with her,” Dambrosky said. “You work from your house.”

Laura’s fingers curled around the edge of the table.

“She was measuring a lawn with a client,” Sam said. “Your man photographed them, and now the client is waiting for the police in her own house. What are you trying to find out about that woman?”

“I haven’t seen a report from this morning,” Dambrosky said.

“So you’re not just watching, you’re actively monitoring.”

“I’m not going to describe our security arrangements on an open line, Sam,” Dambrosky said. “Nobody was told to approach her or her client.”

Sam looked over at the window. The driver raised his head, then lowered it again.

“You were having me watched yesterday?”

“You walked a visitor out of Civic Passage,” Dambrosky said. “Waited on the steps while he looked for his keys. We need to know who is receiving information about our work.”

Sam remembered Webb patting the front of his jacket. The keys were in the inside pocket. Sam had waited at his elbow, watching his hands.

He had not looked across the street.

“That man came to speak to me,” Sam said. “You don’t get to send someone after everyone who does.”

“You have obligations to your employer and their partner,” Dambrosky said. “Those people want to know what you’re doing.”

“Then they can ask me,” Sam said. “They can speak to Graham. They can call a lawyer.”

“Graham and I are speaking,” Dambrosky replied.

Sam felt Laura’s fingers close around his wrist. He looked at her. She pointed to the phone.

“Tell the person working for you to stop photographing me,” she said. “And my clients. I want you to tell him now.”

“I’ll see that nobody approaches you again,” Dambrosky allowed.

“Are you telling him to stop?” Sam pressed.

“I’m telling you what I’ll see to,” Dambrosky said. “I’m not going to make decisions about our security arrangements in this conversation.”

“Then I’ll tell the officer what you told us,” Laura said.

Dambrosky paused. “That’s your decision.”

Sam ended the call before the man could say anything else. He put the phone face down on the table and kept his hand on it.

The owner returned with three glasses and a bottle of lemonade.

“Was that someone who could help?” she asked.

“It was the man who knows what my husband did yesterday,” Laura said.

Sam pulled out a chair. He wanted to call Graham immediately and find out what Dambrosky meant. He wanted to know how many conversations were taking place in rooms he could not enter. Laura sat beside him and poured lemonade into his glass.

“We should write down what he said while we remember it,” she told him. “Then call whoever else we need.”

Sam opened a note on his phone. Laura read over his shoulder, correcting the order of two statements. When the owner went to find the recording from her entrance camera, Laura got up to help her.

Sam stayed at the table. He could see the gray car through the corner of the window. The driver started the engine and pulled away before the officer arrived.

Laura spoke to the officer on the porch. He was young and wrote everything down. When his radio spoke he lowered it without looking away from the page. She showed him her photographs and described the two places where she saw the car. The owner brought out the recording from the entrance camera and explained which part showed Laura crossing the lawn.

When the officer asked about the call, Sam told him Dambrosky’s name, employer and number. He gave the shortest account he could of the model and of what he was trying to stop. Laura supplied the words she remembered about being watched. The officer wrote them down.

Sam could have stayed there explaining Iraq. The officer was asking about a man with a camera, what he did, and who said he sent him. Sam answered those questions.

Before they left, Laura asked the owner to tell her if the car returned. She would send the revised tent drawing the next morning and come back to finish measuring with someone from the venue. The owner agreed and walked them to their cars.

“I’ll follow you home,” Sam said.

“All right.” Laura put her sample books on the passenger seat. “Call Janet before we go. She should know why you left. And what Dambrosky said.”

Sam stepped away from the open door and made the call. Janet listened to all of it and asked for the account he and Laura had written. She would take it to Graham and Aisha herself. Evelyn’s line went to voicemail. He left a message asking to see her that day, before the directors voted on Dambrosky’s seat.

The last call was the one he did not want to make. Webb picked up on the second ring and said nothing while Sam told him how Dambrosky had described the two of them on the front steps. Webb asked if Sam had used his name. Sam said he had not. Webb thanked him for the warning and was gone.

On the drive, he watched the cars behind him nearly as often as Laura’s ahead. A white van stayed with them through three lights before turning into a shopping center. He could see its brake lights when he looked back. Then the road curved, and it was gone.

❦

Laura had covered half the dining table with newspaper and six terracotta pots by the time Sam finished sending Janet the account. A flat of rosemary sat on the floor at her feet, each plant in a black plastic cell with roots showing through the bottom. The overhead light was on, and the window behind her had gone to black glass with the two of them in it. Sam sat beside her and began peeling the price labels from the pots. The first lifted in a strip and left a gray square behind.

“You made it worse,” Laura said.

“I exposed the underlying condition.”

She looked at him. One corner of her mouth lifted, then she took up the next pot.

“Rehearsal dinner?”

“Table assignments. Rosemary for remembrance, except the bride doesn’t want anyone to say the word remembrance.”

“Too funerary?”

“Too much like homework.” Laura loosened a plant in its cell. “These have to be ready for photographs tomorrow. I thought I’d have more of the afternoon.”

Sam rubbed the gray adhesive with his thumb. Laura’s tape had been stretched across the lawn when he arrived at the venue. The owner had brought it in while they waited. He remembered her winding it and putting it next to Laura’s bag.

“I’m sorry,” he said.

“I know.”

He set the pot down. “Webb was the visitor Dambrosky mentioned. He came to help me understand what Wolmeck was doing when we were in Iraq.”

Laura held the plant above the newspaper. “You haven’t told me what you found out yesterday.”

“Wolmeck armed a force on the river. Tamarisk, they called it. We carried what it needed in on our own vehicles. The board said stores. That night Tamarisk fired it into the town we’d driven through. Four men from the town had walked to the yard that afternoon to ask about a child who’d been killed on the road. The force shut them in containers at the yard. Before morning they shot them, with an old man they’d taken from the tomb, and called all five insurgents. The old man was the one who gave me the brick. Dambrosky knew all of it before he sent the next load. I asked him myself.”

She lowered the plant to the table.

“There was a movement I asked him about when we first met,” Sam went on. “I asked the Marines about it before we left, too. Why a stop was added. What they were supposed to do there.”

Laura waited.

“The child was a girl.” He looked into the kitchen, though nothing there required his attention. “Our vehicle struck her. I was sitting beside the driver.”

Laura’s fingers rested against the loose soil. She did not lift the plant again.

“She was playing beside the road,” he said. “I could see her before we reached her.”

He pressed his heel against the floor.

Laura moved her chair nearer. “I’m sorry about the girl.”

The words did not ask him for anything. He looked at her hand on the newspaper and put his beside it. She turned hers over and took his. Soil from her fingers marked the side of his thumb.

“I said a prayer before we went,” he told her. “For the Marines. For the people who lived along those roads. I meant it.”

“I know you did.”

He held her hand. He had sat in kitchens with people who could tell him the last ordinary thing they did before a death. For years afterward, the errand or the unfinished meal could remain more distinct than whole months in which nothing happened.

“There are things I can’t tell you,” he said.

“I understand that.” Laura looked at their hands. “How old was she?”

“I don’t know. Small. Eight, maybe.”

Laura was quiet for a while. “I was carrying Sarah.”

“Yes.”

She moved the plant away from his elbow. “Tell me when you’re having a day like this. That’s all. Don’t sit upstairs working out how long you can manage before I notice.”

Sam’s phone lit on the newspaper. Graham wrote that he wanted him in the morning. Laura set the rosemary in front of him.

“Hold that straight.”

He steadied it while she pressed soil around the roots. They worked on the remaining pots until the newspaper was covered with dirt and torn labels.

“If Delmere calls you again, I want you to tell me,” Laura said. “Even if you think telling me will make the day worse. I need to know what’s happening when I decide where to go and whom to ask for help.”

“I will.”

“And we’ll talk to the children together.”

Sam nodded. Since the drive home he had been trying to work out what he could tell Sarah. He had not got past the first sentence. Laura would be on that call too. Sarah would ask her the questions she would not ask him.

They carried the six samples to the kitchen window. Laura lined them along the sill and decided they were too small. Sam stood beside her while she measured one, then picked up the empty flat and took it out to the bin.
