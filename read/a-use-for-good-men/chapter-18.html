Chapter Eighteen

His Wife Draws a Line

Laura pinned a rosemary pot between her forearms and worked at a knot with both thumbnails. Newspaper covered the dining table. Saturday light lay low across it and showed every grain of spilled soil. The other five pots lined the edge nearest Sam, each with a narrow card pushed into the soil.

“The larger pot won,” she said.

“She thought these were too small?”

“She thought they looked cheap.” Laura pulled one end of the ribbon. The knot tightened.

The cards carried table numbers in her small block letters. Sam set his coffee on a clear corner. “You finished these after I went upstairs Thursday.”

“And sent the photographs. Yesterday she chose the larger pot.”

“How much larger?”

“Two inches. Forty-eight dollars across all the tables.”

Sam sat. She had been up before him, her hair pushed back with a pencil through the knot, and she had the patient look she kept for clients who changed their minds after the work was done. “Wouldn’t it be easier to cut the ribbon?”

“I can use it again if we get it off.” She pushed the pot across to him and held the knot where he could see it. “Can you get under that loop?”

He worked one thumbnail beneath the ribbon. Damp soil had darkened the green at the rim. The loop loosened, and Laura drew the end through.

“One,” she said.

She removed the wooden card and stacked it next to her work tote. On the floor, an open carton contained larger pots separated by corrugated sleeves.

“You’re keeping the rosemary?”

“Yes. The plants have received no complaints.”

He took the next knot. Through the window behind Laura, a squirrel worked its way along the fence, pausing at each post. Sam watched until it disappeared behind the shed. Below the fence her own beds were cut back for the year, the stakes pulled and tied in a bundle on the path. She did that in a weekend every October and did not ask him to help.

“The officer called again,” Laura said. “He wanted the original pictures from my phone. I sent them.”

“When?”

“This morning. You were in the shower.”

“You should have told me.”

She folded the ribbon around two fingers. “He said to call if we see the car or the man again. I’d hoped he could tell us more. I keep looking for that car every time I go out to the truck.”

“Dambrosky didn’t say he’d stop having us watched. He said he’d suspended it.”

“I read your message. I know he could send the man back.”

Sam looked at the front window. The sheer curtain shifted above the floor vent. He had moved one panel aside before breakfast so he could see the street from the stairs.

“What are you doing this afternoon?”

“A garden visit at two. Mrs. Henshaw wants to keep the old apple tree, and the people putting in her patio want to put a drain through its roots.”

“Can it wait?”

“The paving starts Monday.”

“Someone else could look at the tree.”

“She hired me to help her keep that tree. I need to see where they mean to dig before they start.” Laura took the second pot from him. “Her daughter will be there. I’ll tell you when I arrive and when I leave.”

“I could come.”

“You could. If you want to help measure and let me speak to my client.”

He loosened the next knot with more force than it needed. One rosemary stem caught under his knuckle. He moved his hand carefully until it sprang free.

“I don’t want you going on your own,” he said.

“Then come with me.”

She made room on the newspaper for the ribbon. He could go. There was nothing on his calendar, no call he had promised to take. He pictured sitting outside an unfamiliar house, watching both ends of the street while Laura was in a garden he could not see.

“Wouldn’t it be better to give it a few days?”

“For whom?”

“For us.”

Laura put the coil down. “I’ve told the venue owner what happened. I’ve called the police and sent them the pictures. I told Sarah we would call together tonight, and she said six was good. I need you beside me when we talk to Sarah. I also need to earn a living.”

“We can afford to lose one afternoon.”

“Her daughter is coming to meet me. I said I’d be there.”

“She’ll understand.”

“I want to keep the appointment, Sam.”

He looked at the pots still waiting. Two of the wooden cards leaned toward one another. He straightened the nearer card in its slot.

“They put him on the board,” he said.

“You told me.”

“After Graham sent them your account.”

Laura sat back. “Then she’s read it. I’d like to hear Evelyn explain it to me. At this table, with the picture in front of her.”

“We don’t know where they’re willing to go.”

“No. We don’t.” She reached for his hand and waited until he let her take it. “I saw the same car twice. I waited for you in that woman’s house. I didn’t like it. I want you with me today. But I need to go on working, Sam. I can’t put my clients off for weeks while we wait to find out whether anyone’s still watching us.”

He turned his hand under hers.

“I keep thinking about you walking out of the garden center,” he said. “If he came up to you while you were carrying plants, you might not get to your phone.”

“I didn’t know he was following me at the garden center. When I saw him at the venue, I took his picture and called the police.”

She rubbed the back of his hand once, then let go. “Come with me at two. We’ll finish here first.”

They loosened the last three ribbons. Laura tipped one plant into her palm while Sam broke apart the stiff paper sleeve around a larger pot. A little soil fell onto the newspaper. The root ball kept the shape of the smaller container until she eased it apart with her fingers.

“Hold this,” she said.

He held the new pot steady while she lowered the rosemary into it. She pressed fresh soil into the gap and turned it once to inspect the plant.

“Now that looks expensive,” he said.

“Forty-eight dollars more expensive.”

He laughed. She did too, briefly. They planted the others, and he carried all six to the kitchen window. Laura followed him to turn the bare side of one plant toward the light.

“I need the longer tape from the truck,” she said. “And the drawing board. Then lunch.”

“I’ll get them.”

“I need to find the board. You put it somewhere helpful.”

“Against the garage wall.”

“Which wall?”

He had to think about that.

“Exactly,” she said, and went out through the kitchen.

Sam gathered the newspaper inward. Next to Laura’s tote lay the printed estimate for the Henshaw garden, with the address and telephone number above her business name. He read the address. He could not remember whether that street ended in a circle or continued into another neighborhood.

From the garage came the scrape of something being moved, then Laura calling that she had found the board.

He took out his phone and entered the number on the estimate.

A woman answered on the fourth ring. The voice was an older woman’s. She gave her whole name, and there was a radio on in the room behind her.

“I’m Laura Artell’s husband,” he said. “She’s supposed to see you this afternoon. I’m afraid she won’t be able to make it.”

“Oh. Is she all right?”

“She’s not feeling well.”

He heard another woman’s voice near the receiver. Mrs. Henshaw said something to her, then returned.

“My daughter drove over to meet her. Should we ask the paving people to wait?”

“Laura will call you Monday.”

“All right. Tell her I hope she feels better.”

He thanked her and ended the call. His thumb hung over the dark screen. It was one appointment. He would drive out with Laura on Monday, or someone would send pictures of the tree, or the contractor would wait. None of those things required her to stand in a stranger’s garden this afternoon.

Laura came in with the drawing board under one arm and the tape hooked over her wrist.

“Who was that?”

“Mrs. Henshaw. I told her you couldn’t come.”

Laura set the board against the wall. She looked at the estimate, then at his phone.

“What did you tell her?”

“That you weren’t feeling well.”

“Give me the estimate.”

“I can explain it to her.”

“Give it to me, Sam.”

He handed her the paper. She took her own phone from the tote and called the number, moving no farther away than the kitchen doorway.

“Mrs. Henshaw, it’s Laura. I’m sorry about the confusion. I am well, and I will be there at two.”

She listened, her free hand flat against the doorframe.

“I’ll speak to your daughter about the tree before either of you agrees to anything. Thank you for waiting.”

She ended the call and stayed where she was.

“If something happened while you were there—” Sam began.

“You knew I wanted to go. You waited until I left the room, and you called my client.”

“I was trying to give us a little time.”

“She asked if she should find someone else.” Laura folded the estimate once. “She hasn’t even met me. You made me call her back and apologize. I’m going on my own this afternoon. You can watch the street from here.”

He stepped toward her. “Laura.”

She held up one hand, and he stopped.

“Don’t call her again. Don’t call the venue or the rental company or anyone else I’m working for. Talk to me.”

He could hear the refrigerator motor beneath her voice. On the windowsill, the rosemary leaned toward the glass. They had spent most of the morning getting it into the new pots.

“I shouldn’t have said you were ill,” he offered.

“You shouldn’t have called.”

She put the estimate in her tote and went to the counter. A loaf of bread rested against a saucepan. She lifted the lid, took two bowls from the cupboard and set one on the counter in front of Sam.

“There’s soup,” she said.

“I’m not hungry.”

“I am.”

She filled her bowl. He stood beside the other while she carried her lunch to the table and moved the last ribbon coil out of the way.

She is afraid of you.

He stood over the bowl she had set out for him, a head and a half above her, with his hands at his sides, and she had carried her lunch to the far side of the table and kept it between them. For the call she had stayed in the doorway with her hand on the frame, where a person stands who wants to know where the door is.

Laura’s mouth was closed. She tore a piece of bread from the loaf and set it down by her bowl. There was a place opposite her, where he had been sitting.

He put the unused bowl back in the cupboard.

“I have some things to finish upstairs,” he said.

Laura put down the bread. “Sarah is expecting us at six. She wants to talk about the man in the car, and about what she should do if somebody calls her about you. I need you on that call with me.”

“I said I’d be there.”

“Yes.” Laura’s eyes went to the stairs. “And while I’m still here, would you leave the door open? I don’t want to knock and wait to find out whether you can bear to speak to me.”

“I can bear to speak to you.”

“Then leave it open.”

“All right.”

He went upstairs carrying his coffee. In Sarah’s room the bed was smooth and the photographs above the dresser hung at slightly different heights. Above the desk, his own pages had begun to go up: the month and the place, the three letters from the lieutenant’s board, a question with nothing written under it. He set the cup down, opened his work laptop and read the same sentence in Aaron’s message twice.

Downstairs, a spoon touched the edge of a bowl. Laura’s chair moved back from the table.

He stood and pulled Sarah’s door toward him. For a moment he held it short of the frame.

He closed it until the latch caught.
