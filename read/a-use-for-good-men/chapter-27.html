Chapter Twenty-Seven

The Two Chairs

The hurt boy’s mother came through the gate on foot, from a car she had left running at the front. She was in hospital scrubs under an open coat, with her hair pinned up for work and the car keys in her fist. She went past Don and the nurse and took her son’s face in both hands to look at the chin, and the boy began to cry again, from the hands. The nurse said four stitches, maybe five, and gave her the gauze.

The mother looked at the other boy once on her way back through the gate with her son. She did not say anything to him. The custodian pulled the cone aside to let them pass and put it back.

Sam moved back against the wall. His visitor sticker lifted from his shirt at one corner. He pressed it down and kept his fingers there until an employee passed with a chair.

The other boy had not moved from the walkway. His knees seemed to be the only thing holding him up. Don took him by the shoulder, not hard, and sat him in one of two plastic chairs beneath the walkway roof, then went inside past Sam. He came back with a bottle of water, held it out to the boy and sat in the other chair.

He sat at an angle, partly facing the loop. The boy could look at Don without being made to face him. He took the bottle and held it between his hands, and then his hands went down between his knees with the bottle still in them.

Sam had carried water to a man once and sat where the man could see him. The bottle had waited on the ground a long time.

Outside, a cafeteria cart rattled over the joint in the covered pavement. The boy turned the bottle once between his palms.

Heat spread beneath Sam’s shirt. He could see the woman pulling off her abaya as she ran, the other woman raising her hand to keep the girls back. The driver had stopped. Another Marine took the wheel.

You did nothing.

His hand closed against his trouser seam. He had stayed until the low red lights came on along the lane.

The girl was still dead.

He took his hand away from the seam. The two chairs outside were blue. One had a white mark across its back, and the boy was wearing a jacket too big for him, somebody’s brother’s. Behind Sam, children off the buses were asking for breakfast.

Outside, Don had not moved. He had asked the boy nothing.

Don stayed until the counselor came out for the boy. He spoke to both of them, then returned through the side entrance.

“I need to be in the office,” he told Sam. “Ellen can take you back if you want.”

“I can wait.”

“You’ve been standing there an hour.”

Sam looked up at the clock above the corridor doors. He had not noticed it before.

“Come sit down,” Don said. “You can wait in a chair.”

His office was smaller than Sam expected. A desk stood under a window onto the front parking lot. On one bookcase were mathematics texts, binders and a chipped mug full of pencils. Behind the desk hung a framed pencil drawing of a bridge truss. The desk carried a blotter, the telephone and a legal pad squared to its edge. A card propped against the mug said Thank you for letting us try again, in three different colors of marker.

Sam sat and felt the strain along his lower back begin to ease.

“Coffee?”

“I can still taste the first one.”

“I warned you.”

Don sat in the chair beside him. Beyond the partly open door, the office phones rang and quit. He looked toward the sound, then back at Sam.

“I used to sit that way,” Sam said.

“Which way?”

“With somebody who didn’t want to talk. So they could see me without having to look at me.”

Don rested his forearms on his thighs.

“The driver in Iraq,” Sam began. “I brought him water when we got back. I sat beside the vehicle with him.”

“Last night you told me about the girl.”

Sam looked at the card on the bookcase. “That’s usually where I stop.”

“What happened to him?”

“He came to find me a few nights later. Three, I think. We walked. I took him back to his tent afterward and went to see him the next morning.”

Don nodded. He did not ask what the Marine had said.

“His staff sergeant couldn’t use him in the section after that. He made him my driver. It kept him working, and it kept him where I could see him. He drove me once. On the way back he put the vehicle up a canal bank, with the second vehicle behind us watching. I asked the executive officer that night to put him in for a consult. He had it the next morning. That afternoon, or the one after, he tried to kill himself with a generator. I’ve never been sure which, and I was there for the rest of it. The staff sergeant found him in time. I was at another base, and they flew me back to sit with him until a psychiatrist could see him. He went home that night.”

“Did he live?”

“He lived. He wrote to me about a year later. He was still in treatment, and he hadn’t tried again.”

Don looked at him. “So you saved a man that day.”

Sam let the words reach him. For a moment he did not argue with them, and his hands opened in his lap. Then he could see the road again, with the girl on it.

“I wanted to be back in the truck before we reached her,” Sam said. “A few seconds earlier, when there might still have been time to make the driver stop. Nothing I could do for him afterward would bring her back.”

“No.”

“The water was such a small thing.”

Don rubbed a thumb across the crease in his palm. “It was what I could bring him just now.”

Sam leaned back. Through the office window, the tops of the arriving buses moved past at intervals. Don watched one stop, then returned his attention to Sam.

“I told the executive officer what happened,” Sam said. “The morning after. I asked him whose load we’d carried and why we’d been sent to that stop. They were supposed to examine it. Nobody told me what they found.”

“Did you know then what the company was arming? That they’d shoot the men who came asking about her?”

“Not that morning.”

Don waited.

“I knew enough to ask,” Sam admitted. “I keep thinking I should have done more before we went.”

“What would more have been?”

Sam could see the watchstander looking past him toward the movement lane. The man had seemed to know what he was doing. Sam had asked a question and accepted his answer. He wanted, even now, to find a second sentence that would have made the man stop.

“I don’t know,” he said. “Something that worked.”

Don stayed with him. A student came to the open office door carrying a pink slip. Don took it, read it and pointed to the chair outside.

“Give me a few minutes,” he told the student. “I’ll come get you.”

The student left. Don laid the slip on his desk.

“I’m sorry,” Sam said.

“For being here?”

“For the morning.”

“You helped Marla. I’m glad you came.”

Sam nodded. He remembered the boy at the cafeteria doors turning back, his own voice calling Marla. The boy had looked once more toward the walkway before following her.

Don took the phone. “I’m going to ask Ellen to come get you. Go have some sleep.”

He made the call with the office door open. Sam heard him tell her that a boy had needed stitches and his mother had come for him, and that the other one was with the counselor. Don listened, thanked her and put down the receiver.

“Twenty minutes.”

Sam looked at the books on the shelf. “Do you miss engineering?”

“Parts of it.”

“Which parts?”

“The people who answered their email.” Don smiled a little. “I miss finishing a job and being able to leave it finished.”

“Why did you change?”

“I was tutoring. I wanted more of that. Then I taught mathematics, and somebody persuaded me this would be a sensible thing to do next.” He lifted the pink slip from the desk and put it down again. “Some days I think they just wanted my classroom.”

Sam smiled.

“Do you miss ministry?”

Sam looked toward the open door. A boy outside was telling the receptionist that his mother knew he was there. The woman asked him to take a seat while she called her.

“Sometimes I miss walking out to where the men were working,” Sam allowed. “I’d stop to ask what somebody was fixing, and half an hour later we’d still be talking.”

“You used to be good at showing up.”

“Webb said something like that.”

“Who is Webb?”

“A Marine I knew. He came to see me last week.” Sam tilted his neck hoping for a moment of relief from the rising tightness. “I hadn’t called him for years. When I did, I wanted something.”

“You could call him again.”

“I warned him about the people watching me.”

Don did not answer.

Sam watched a narrow band of sunlight cross the carpet as someone passed the outer window.

“I wanted work I could put down,” he said. “At Civic Passage, I’d hear that somebody had accepted an offer, and I’d go home and tell Laura about it. I’d know when he was starting, what he’d be earning. I could be happy for him without lying awake trying to work out what else I should have done. I wasn’t pretending it helped.”

“I believe you.”

“If someone was still unhappy after they got a job, they had a counselor. I liked not having to know what else was happening in their lives.”

A phone rang beyond the doorway. Sam waited for it to stop before continuing.

“When I thought about going back to a church, I thought about somebody waiting after everybody else left. Asking if we could talk.” He rubbed his hands together once. “I used to want to be the person they found.”

Don looked at him from where he sat.

“I don’t know when that changed,” Sam said.

He kept his eyes on the carpet. Help me, he prayed. He was trying to leave it at that, but before the receptionist returned he had begun telling God what he should have done for the driver.

The receptionist appeared at the door. “The superintendent is on line one. Mrs. Rowan is outside.”

Don stood. “Tell him I’m coming.”

Sam leveraged himself up out of the low chair.

“Sign out,” Don called after him. “I have enough trouble without losing a visitor.”

Sam wrote the time after his name in the binder and peeled off the sticker. Don had already picked up the phone.

Ellen was under the entrance roof with her arms folded against the chill. On the drive home, Ellen told him where lunch was and asked for anything that needed washing. He said he had brought enough clothes. She said she was putting a load in anyway when they got back, and the machine was by the back door. That was as near as she came to insisting.

In daylight the town was two streets of brick fronts along the state road, a bank, a hardware store, the diner with pickups nosed into the curb, and the church on the corner whose sign he had passed twice before daylight. Past the last houses the road climbed between brown pastures and stands of pine, with the hills close on both sides. Ahead of them the gap the town was named for showed as a notch of pale sky. Ellen drove sitting forward, close to the wheel, and slowed for a school bus going the other way.

At the house, Sam sat on the porch while she took a basket inside. By day the porch looked over the gravel to the road and the slope of trees across it, where the first color had come into the leaves. He opened Laura’s number and called before he could decide she might be too busy.

She answered after several rings.

“I’m at Don’s,” he said. “We went to the school this morning. Ellen brought me back.”

“Are you all right?”

“I’m tired.” He looked down at the gravel. “Can you talk for a few minutes?”

“Yes.”

He could hear her moving something, then nothing. A door closed on her side, and the noise behind her stopped, as if she had gone into another room to take the call alone.

“I’m sorry I left you that note,” he said.

“Why didn’t you tell me before I went to work?”

“You offered to stay with me. I wanted to leave by myself.”

“You could have said so.”

“I didn’t know how to say it without hurting you.”

“And then you wouldn’t answer when I called.”

Sam pressed the phone against his ear. He had wanted her to know that he was safe. He had wanted her to stop calling.

“I thought you’d ask me to come back,” he said. “I was afraid I would.”

Laura was quiet. Sam watched a squirrel stop halfway down a tree beside the driveway, its body flattened against the bark.

“I called Don,” she said. “I did that. You didn’t have to make me find the note.”

He closed his eyes.

“I know.”

“I don’t think you do yet.”

Sam opened his eyes again. Ellen passed inside with the empty basket. He could see her through the screen door and was grateful she did not come out.

“Tell me what you and Sarah have decided,” he said. “About being watched. I’ll listen.”

“She can tell you herself. When she’s ready.”

“Will you tell her I’d like to talk?”

“I will.”

“I won’t come to the college without asking her.”

“You already promised that.”

“I did.”

“I want you to answer when I call,” Laura told him. “And when Sarah’s ready to talk, please listen to her.”

“All right.”

A car passed on the road. Sam turned to it, then back to the open door.

“Are you going back for the examination?” Laura asked.

“I want to. I need to speak to Jasleen.”

“Then let me know what you decide.”

“I will.”

“Get some sleep first.”

“I will.”

“Sam.”

He waited.

“I’m glad you got there.”

“Thank you for calling Don.”

After she hung up, he stayed on the porch until the squirrel reached the grass.
