Chapter Twenty-One

The Breach

Aaron was eating yogurt with a fork. The spoon lay at the edge of his keyboard, sealed in the plastic sleeve that also contained a knife, a napkin and a small packet of salt.

“You haven’t opened it,” Sam said.

Aaron looked at the packet. “That would explain several things.”

He put the fork down. Sam stood over the desk with his coat on. He could smell coffee somewhere behind him, and the ordinary desire for a cup surprised him.

“Is James on the call?”

“No. Jasleen sent someone called Victor. He started by asking me what we’re installing.” Aaron peeled the plastic away from the spoon. “Did you speak to James?”

“Yesterday.”

“Is he all right?”

Sam took off his coat. “Dambrosky took him off the project.”

“I know. I meant James.”

“I haven’t heard from him this morning.”

Aaron turned back to his screen. He was preparing the program that would connect their model to ADICUS. Sam recognized the section they argued over on Friday.

“You fixed it,” Sam said.

“I think so. We test at nine.”

Sam leaned closer. Aaron moved the yogurt out of his way unasked.

When ADICUS showed a recruiter an ability the model had found in a man’s record, the model’s reasons would now show alongside it. Sam followed the change to the next section. Aaron had also kept ADICUS from losing the model’s warning when the ability it suggested depended on training the applicant hadn’t finished. It was careful work. A counselor using the results would be able to explain both the opportunity and what the applicant had left to learn.

“That’s good,” Sam said.

“You could tell me after the test too.”

Sam straightened. He wanted to sit beside Aaron and work. For a moment it could almost have been the morning he had expected: Aaron’s screen, a problem they both liked. Then he remembered Len at his door with the bag, and Dambrosky saying yes.

His phone vibrated in his coat pocket. Nadia’s message asked for a time to talk. He told Aaron he would come back and went to the small room where staff made private calls.

Nadia’s camera was off. A man said hello.

“This is Amir,” Nadia said. “The driver I told you about. We have spoken to a lawyer here. He would like you to hear what he told her.”

“Are you somewhere you can talk?”

“We aren’t at the office. We have half an hour,” Nadia said.

Sam sat. A calendar hung on the wall opposite him, open to September. The room had been built for somebody smaller. His knees touched the shelf that carried the telephone, and he kept his elbows in.

Amir spoke carefully, stopping once to ask Nadia for an English word. His voice was low and even. He gave the dates and the places first, the way a driver gives directions. He drove Delmere employees to the buildings the Guard used along the coast. Before that he had driven trucks for the recovery program, from the port where its relief cargo came in to the yards the Guard kept. Nobody told the drivers what was in them. Three weeks earlier, he drove two men to a municipal yard at first light, past houses whose roofs had been torn open in the night. The men unloaded radios for the municipality. A Guard officer signed for them and asked Amir to stay with the vehicle.

“I knew the street behind that yard,” Amir went on. “My cousin used to have a shop there. People came past carrying their things. Two of our trucks were at the end of the street. People were being put on them. One woman asked me to take her father. He could not walk well.”

“Did you take him?”

“The officer said I was waiting for our men. I asked again. He made me move the van inside the gate.” Amir stopped. Sam heard a chair scrape. “I watched the woman try to lift him. I stayed with the van.”

Sam pressed his palm flat against his knee. He wanted to tell Amir he had done all he could.

“What would you like me to tell Jasleen?”

“That I saw them make people leave. That our men were still working there. They came back to the van and we drove home. In the morning I took them again.”

“May I tell Janet too?”

“Yes,” Amir said. “Nadia told me who she is.”

Nadia returned to the phone. “The lawyer’s name is Leila Darzi. She runs Meredan Workers’ House, the group that found rooms for Amir’s relatives. We went to see her yesterday. Amir made a statement. I told her what happened when we asked our manager for help.”

“Will she speak to Jasleen?”

“She has already tried. Jasleen has asked her to call again this afternoon.” Nadia paused. “Our manager wants us both in his office today. His name is Colin Vale. He said the Guard has questions about the report we made.”

“How did he hear about it?”

“He didn’t tell me,” Nadia said. “Leila has asked him to let her attend. He won’t agree.”

Sam looked at the door. Through its narrow window he could see someone in the corridor with a mug.

“Can you stay where you are?”

“We have to go back to work,” Nadia said. “Leila knows when we are going. Jasleen knows too. I am telling you because Amir wanted you to hear him yourself.”

Sam asked them to send him Leila’s contact details. He said he would call Jasleen as soon as they finished.

“There is something else,” Nadia said. “Amir was afraid you would tell him to leave his job. He cannot stop paying for his mother’s room because somebody in America thinks he ought to be braver.”

“I wasn’t going to tell him that.”

“I told him you hadn’t asked me to leave my job.”

Amir said something to Nadia, and she laughed once and translated it. “He says when this is finished he is going to open a shop where his cousin’s was. He says you are welcome to come and buy a phone charger.”

Amir thanked him for listening. Sam heard the two of them speak briefly to each other before the call ended.

He did not leave the chair. He could see the woman’s effort to lift her father, though he knew he had not seen it at all. Amir supplied no description of her face or clothes. Sam’s mind filled the street with people from another place and another year.

He called Jasleen. She confirmed that Leila had gotten through to her and said she was trying to have Vale postpone the meeting until the lawyer could attend. Sam repeated Amir’s account, including his permission to share it with Janet.

“I’ll call you when I hear from Nadia,” Jasleen said.

“What has Victor been asked to do with our model?”

“He’s preparing to connect it to ADICUS. I haven’t asked him to resume work for Mission Support.”

“Dambrosky can tell him to.”

“I’ll call Victor and find out what he’s been told.”

Sam ended the call and sent Janet a message asking her to come find him when she was free. Then he opened the door for the woman outside.

“Sorry,” he said.

“It’s all right. I’m just calling to change a dentist appointment.”

Sam tried to smile. He went past the coffee to his office and left the door open. Across the corridor, Aaron was speaking into his headset. The office was the one the counselors had passed over, a desk, two chairs and a window that looked at the next building. He had never put anything on its walls. A cup from the week before was on the windowsill with a dry ring in the bottom.

Sam sat at his own computer and opened the shared folder containing the program Aaron was about to test. Graham had removed none of his access.

Last week’s version was in the folder where Aaron had saved it. It would not send a complete result. ADICUS would reject it in seconds, and the test would stop there.

Aaron had kept a backup of the revised program. Sam opened it. The changes he had admired that morning were all there. Nothing would be destroyed, and no veteran’s record would be touched. He had checked that before he let himself think about the rest.

He looked across at Graham’s office. The door was open. He could walk there now and repeat what Amir had told him. Graham would listen. Then he would tell Sam to give Jasleen time to get Vale’s meeting postponed. Aaron would keep testing the connection to ADICUS while Sam waited for her call.

At the bottom of the screen, Aaron’s message said they were ready at Alderwick.

Sam copied last week’s program into the shared folder. A box appeared, offering to replace the file already there.

A Marine had once shown him the frayed place on a cargo strap. Sam had asked how he knew a strap had to be taken out of use. The man showed him the points he checked. Aaron laughed at something on the call. Sam looked across at him, then clicked Replace.

For a few seconds nothing changed around him. The woman from the small room walked past with her mug. Sam waited for the relief he expected from having finally done something.

It did not come. The box had closed, and the folder showed last week’s file with the morning’s date beside it. He could put the other one back. Aaron’s backup was where Aaron had left it, and restoring it would take less time than replacing it had taken. He laid his hand on the mouse and left it there.

Across the corridor Aaron was still talking. He laughed again. Whatever Alderwick had sent had not yet reached the place where it would stop. Sam counted the seconds, lost the count, and began again.

He thought of Amir on the call that morning, saying the drivers were not told, and of Nadia in Vale’s office with a lawyer’s number and no lawyer. Those were the people he was doing this for. He said it to himself twice, and the second time it sounded like a man explaining himself to an officer.

Aaron called his name.

Sam stood. His legs felt stiff as he crossed to Aaron’s desk.

“The connection stopped,” Aaron said. “Did you change something?”

“Yes.”

Aaron took off his headset. A voice continued faintly from the earpiece on the desk.

“Why?”

“I put back the earlier version. I want them to stop until Jasleen has spoken to the people who can stop what Vale’s doing.”

“Who is Vale?”

Sam told him only that Delmere’s local manager had summoned two employees after they reported the Guard.

Aaron stared at him. “Then tell Graham. Tell whoever can help them. You can’t replace my work while I’m using it.”

“I haven’t deleted it. You can restore the copy you saved.”

“Of course I can restore it.” Aaron pushed back his chair. “I have six people at Alderwick trying to find a mistake I didn’t make. Priya has appointments downstairs in an hour. I was supposed to help her, and now we have to start these tests again.”

“You don’t have to start them again.”

Aaron’s face changed. Sam remembered the man standing to move a chair out of his way on Friday.

“I told you what I was willing to do,” Aaron said. “You didn’t agree. That doesn’t let you decide for me.”

Graham came out of his office. He looked at the two of them, then at the headset on the desk.

“What happened?”

“I changed the program before they tested it,” Sam answered.

“Was something wrong with it?” Graham asked.

“No.”

Aaron bent to speak into the headset. Sam could hear him apologizing. He said he would call back after he had restored the right files.

“Tell them it was on our side,” Graham cut in. “Say that before anything else.”

Graham asked Sam to come into his office. This time he left the extra chair against the wall.

Sam sat where he sat for their morning conversations. Graham closed the door, which he did not usually do, and stayed on his feet behind the desk with his hands on the back of his own chair.

“From the beginning,” he said.

Sam began with the call that morning and heard how long it took him to reach the folder. Graham did not interrupt. When Sam stopped, Graham asked whether he had meant to stop the tests.

“Yes.”

“Did Aaron know?”

“No.”

“Did anyone authorize it?”

Sam looked at the chair against the wall. Nobody had. He had not asked anyone, because he knew what they would say.

“No,” he said.

Graham picked up his phone and asked Aisha to come in.

By ten, Sam’s computer was on Graham’s desk. Aisha stood over it, waiting for Sam to disconnect the charger. She had brought a small carton for the dock and cables he used at the office.

“I can pack those,” Sam offered.

“Thank you,” Aisha said.

He wound the charger too tightly and had to loosen it before it would fit. Neither Aisha nor Graham hurried him.

Sam had told him about the call with Jasleen and left out the two names. Graham said he would ask Janet to join Jasleen’s effort, and that the tests with Alderwick would go on.

Now Aisha took the carton. She asked for Sam’s badge and office key. He unclipped the badge from its strap, took the key from his coat pocket, and placed them in front of the computer.

“We’ll arrange a time for you to collect anything you leave,” she said. “I’ll send the details about your final pay to your personal address.”

When she left, Graham sat behind his desk.

“I asked you to stay involved without doing the work you’d refused,” he began. “Aaron agreed to do it. You used the access I left you to stop him.”

“Only for a morning.”

“You didn’t know how long it would take us to find the change. You could have told Aaron before six people began looking for a fault. Victor was on that call. Dambrosky will have heard before you sat down in that chair.” Graham waited. “Your employment ends today, Sam. I don’t trust you with our systems after this.”

Sam watched a drop of water travel down the outside of Graham’s glass. The glass was on the corner where Sam sometimes put his own coffee during their morning conversations.

He wanted Graham to say that his warnings were wrong. He could fight that. He could bring Nadia onto a call, if she agreed, and make Graham hear the calm effort in her voice. He remembered Aaron bending over the headset to apologize to the people at Alderwick.

“Dambrosky is still going to use the model,” he said.

“The directors still have to watch Delmere use it at Alderwick before they vote to let it go.”

“After he’s removed the man who stopped him.”

“Then I want Aaron at the examination to check how Delmere’s engineers are using the model. He’s agreed to attend.” Graham rubbed his forehead. “I’m taking the Friday time Jasleen offered last week. Delmere already has three teams scheduled for that day. Aaron says he can finish the connection tests by Thursday.”

“Friday?”

“The fourteenth. Alderwick sends the model to Delmere at noon, if the directors say yes. The Monday date is gone.”

“You’re leaving him less time to check the model’s recommendations.”

“The changes you looked at this morning have saved Aaron some time. He wants the weekend to try records from people who held two jobs at once. He wants to be sure the model keeps each duty with the posting it came from. I’ve asked him to tell the directors which histories he hasn’t tested.”

“And you’re willing to send it before he’s checked that?”

“Yes, if the tests he finishes give us enough confidence. The directors can hold up delivery if they aren’t satisfied.” Graham put his hands on the desk. “I gave him that weekend, Sam. I’m taking it back. I need him and Priya seeing applicants on Monday. This morning she’s calling people to say we aren’t ready.”

Sam stood before Graham finished. Then he stayed near the chair, uncertain what standing obliged him to do next.

“Janet is speaking to our lawyer,” Graham went on. “I will speak to Jasleen myself. You can send anything further to my personal attention. You can’t go back onto the systems.”

Sam picked up his coat.

“I’m not asking you to agree with Delmere,” Graham said.

Sam looked at him. “Then don’t sell them the model.”

“That decision hasn’t been made.”

Graham came around the desk. Sam stepped into the corridor before the man could get to the door.

Priya was at reception with her appointment list. She was speaking to a man in a brown jacket who had arrived early. Sam recognized him from a workshop. He used to repair generators. He had his hands in the jacket pockets and his weight on his heels. He had come early to be sure of finding the building, and now he had nothing to do with himself. His interview at a food-processing plant was Thursday.

Priya told the man they would find another time that afternoon. He explained that his brother had driven him and had to be at work by one.

Sam stopped. He could take the man into a quiet room and rehearse the interview with him. They could talk through a generator repair until the man could explain how he had found the fault and put it right. Sam wouldn’t need his computer or access to the applicant records. He looked down the corridor for an empty room, as though he worked here.

Priya looked up. Her eyes moved from his coat to the empty strap where his badge usually hung.

“I’ve got it, Sam,” she said.

He nodded and went outside. The sun had come out while he was inside. It lay on the cars in the lot and on the brick. He walked out into it with nothing to carry.

In the car, he found the parking receipt before he found his keys. He flattened it on his knee. His hand was trembling hard enough to make the paper shake.

Laura would be at home. He could call her from here, before the engine, before the drive gave him forty minutes to find a softer word than fired.

Sam put the receipt in the door pocket and called her.
