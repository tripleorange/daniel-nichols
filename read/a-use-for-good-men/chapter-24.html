Chapter Twenty-Four

The Rope Line

The charter landed at Miramar on a Thursday afternoon in September 2003 and taxied for so long that Marines were standing in the aisle before it stopped.

Sam kept his seat. He had the aisle near the back, where the flight attendants had moved him on the first leg after watching him try the window. Across the row, the window showed a brown hill, a water tower, the tail of a gray transport, and then the open side of a hangar with a crowd inside it.

A gunnery sergeant came down the aisle with one hand on the seat backs. “Nobody goes anywhere until we’re formed up,” he said at each row. “Your mother can stand it ten more minutes.” He said it the same way every time, and the Marines laughed every time, more than it was worth.

They went down the stairs into a wind that carried fuel and, underneath it, something cut and green. Sam had not smelled a lawn in seven months. The sergeant major formed the squadron on the apron under the lip of the hangar roof and put Sam at the end of the staff rank, where his height would do the least harm to the line.

The families stood inside the hangar behind a yellow rope strung between traffic cones. Two Marines from the rear party held the ends. There were signs on bedsheets and poster board, balloons tied to strollers, a girl on her father’s shoulders holding a flag by the wrong end. Somewhere behind the crowd a band played something Sam knew from parades.

He found Laura near the last cone, where the rope met the hangar wall. She had the stroller turned sideways and one hand on it. Michael stood on the seat of a folding chair with his hand on her shoulder, which made him nearly as tall as she was. Peter held the stroller’s handle in both fists and watched the band.

The colonel spoke into a microphone that sent every third word back from the far wall. He said the squadron had done what they were sent to do. He said the word home several times. Sam watched the colonel’s mouth, and after a while he was looking past it to the open end of the hangar, where the light came in, and thinking about a gate of corrugated steel that two men had dragged shut.

He brought his eyes back to Laura and told himself to feel what the men around him were feeling. The captain beside him was crying without any change in his face. A lance corporal two ranks forward kept rising onto his toes and settling. Sam put his thumb under the zipper of the flight suit, where the buckle had been, and found nothing to loosen.

A driver who would not ask for help had gone home ahead of him. He had left a town Len had been through twice.

Bring everyone home safely.

The sergeant major turned and said the squadron was dismissed, and the rope was on the floor before he finished the word.

Sam stayed where he was. The hangar filled from the far side, and the staff rank came apart around him, men going past him with their covers in their hands. He walked to the wall because the wall was the one thing in the building not moving.

Laura reached him before he had decided how to cross the floor. She put her hand flat on his chest, over the zipper, and held it there. Then she kissed him.

“You smell like an airplane,” she said.

“I’ve been on one since yesterday.”

She looked up at him for a while. Her hair was pulled back harder than she used to wear it.

“Dad.” Michael had carried the folding chair with him and was standing on it again. “I’m taller than Mom.”

“You’re on a chair.”

“Still.”

Peter had come as far as Laura’s legs. Sam went down on one knee, which put his face at the height of the boy’s, and said nothing.

“Mom said you’d be in a flight suit,” Peter said.

“I am.”

“It’s dirty.”

“It’s been places.”

Peter put one finger on the zipper pull and took it away.

Laura lifted the baby out of the stroller. The blanket was yellow. “This is Sarah,” she said. “She’s awake. She’s generally awake.”

Sam took her the way he had been handed things all summer, with both hands and his elbows in. She weighed less than his helmet bag. Her eyes were open and fixed on nothing in particular, and her mouth moved once, as if she were trying out a word she had decided against.

He stood holding her while the hangar went on around them. It seemed to him that he was watching a man hold a baby, and that the man was doing it correctly.

“Say something to her,” Laura said.

“Hello, Sarah.”

The baby closed her eyes.

“That’s about what I get,” Laura said.

❦

Laura drove. Sam sat low beside her with his knees against the glove box and the baby seat behind him. Michael asked if he had seen a camel.

“Three.”

“Did they spit?”

“They didn’t think I was worth it.”

Peter asked nothing. He watched Sam’s hands on his knees as if they might do something.

The freeway climbed into hills that had been green when he left. They were the color of the road now. Laura told him which exits had changed and which neighbor had moved and that the Ruizes had a dog that barked at the mail. She drove the way she had always driven, both hands on the wheel, and she did not look at him while she talked, and he understood that she was giving him time to look at her.

A bedsheet hung across the garage door with letters cut from construction paper. WELCOME HOME MICHAEL’S DAD. The letters were different sizes.

“Michael’s class,” Laura said. “I traced them at the kitchen table. They cut them out. It was my day at the school.”

“You didn’t tell me.”

“It was a surprise. He told you on the phone in July.”

“He did,” Sam admitted.

Inside, the refrigerator was covered in drawings and one photograph of him in the flight suit, taken the week before he left. A casserole from somebody in the squadron sat on the counter with a note taped to the foil. The boys went to the television because they had been promised it. Laura filled a glass at the tap and gave it to him. She put Sarah in the bassinet in the dining room, where she could be heard from the kitchen, and came back and looked at him properly for the first time.

“You haven’t lost any weight,” she said.

She said it the way she would have said the roof had held. Sam heard what he had written her in March: that he was running the perimeter road before the heat came up, that she would have to get used to a thinner man. He had run four times.

“No,” he said.

“I thought you’d come back thin. Everybody’s husband comes back thin.”

“I had a desk and three meals.”

“I’m glad,” she said, and she was. He set his glass down before she noticed what his hand was doing.

She had the look of someone who had been awake for a long time in a house where everyone else was asleep. He had seen it on wives at the spouses’ meetings before he left and had thought he knew what it meant.

“Peter stopped sleeping in April,” she said. “Sarah hasn’t started.”

“And you?”

“I sleep when they do. It’s a system.” She folded the note from the casserole in half. “A car stopped across the street in June. Gray. Two men got out in jackets and looked at the house numbers.”

Sam put his hand on the counter.

“I sat down on the stairs,” she went on. “I didn’t decide to. I was standing, and then I was sitting on the stairs with the phone in my hand.”

“Who were they?”

“The water district. There was a leak at the Ruizes’.” She laughed. The boys looked over from the television and looked back. “I didn’t tell you on the phone.”

“No.”

“There wasn’t anything you could have done about it from there,” she said.

❦

At bedtime Peter was asleep before the light was off, a plastic truck under his arm. Michael lay with his eyes open and his arms outside the blanket, the way Sam lay in a cot.

“Are you going to be here in the morning?”

“Yes.”

“Mom said you might have to go to the base.”

“Not tomorrow. In the morning I’ll be here.”

Michael thought about it. “What about the morning after?”

“I’ll be here then too. I’ll go to the base and come back. That’s how it works now.”

“Okay,” Michael said, and turned over, and Sam stayed in the doorway until the boy’s breathing changed.

Afterward he emptied the duffel onto the bedroom floor because the clean things and the dirty ones were no longer different. The flight helmet in its bag. Socks. A notebook with sand in the binding. The wedge-marked fragment of clay, wrapped in a sock, which he had carried in the duffel’s outside pocket for three months and which had survived two helicopters and a cargo pallet.

Laura sat on the bed with Sarah against her shoulder. “What’s that?”

“A piece of a brick.” He unwrapped it. “From the river.”

“What river?”

“The Tigris.”

She held out her hand and he put it there. She turned it so the lamp fell across the marks. “It looks old.”

“He said it was old.”

“Who did?”

The caretaker at the shrine had closed Sam’s hand over it as they left, and Salma, when he showed it to her in the vehicle, had said it was old, and that the man wished the chaplain to have something from the town. The town’s name was in Sam’s mouth. So was the rest of it.

“A man gave it to me,” he said. “In a town we went through.”

“Which town?”

He took the fragment back and set it on the dresser with the marks facing the wall. “I’ll tell you about it,” he said. “Not tonight.”

She let it go. He heard her let it go, and went on sorting socks.

❦

He went back to work on Monday. The chapel office at the air station had kept his chair and given his desk to a chaplain from the group, who gave it back with apologies and a drawer full of someone else’s pens.

On Wednesday the wing chaplain came in with a folder and asked whether he was ready to go out with a captain from the group. A lance corporal from one of the squadrons still over there had died when a truck went off a road he had driven forty times. The parents lived in Escondido. The captain would say it. Sam would stay. The wing chaplain was a naval officer who looked like a Marine and dressed like one, high and tight, lean in the way of a man who ran long distances. The only things out of place on him were the aviator sunglasses riding on top of his head and the readers that hung from a chain.

They were on the porch at twenty to seven. The father opened the door in his work shirt and looked at the captain’s uniform, then at Sam’s, and said, “No,” before anyone had spoken. The captain said the words he had been given. The mother came into the hall behind her husband and did not come any closer.

Sam found the cups. He made coffee in a kitchen he had never been in, which turned out to be a thing he could do, and he stayed until the sister arrived from Temecula and took the mother’s hand. He did not say anything about God unless he was asked. The mother asked. He told her what he believed, and found that it was still what he believed.

On the way back the captain pulled into a gas station, turned off the engine and sat with both hands on the wheel. He was younger than Sam by ten years and had never done it before.

“You were good with the cups,” he said.

“It’s the part I’m sure of.”

The captain looked at the pumps. “What’s the part you’re not sure of?”

“The rest,” Sam said.

There were two funerals that month and a memorial at the squadron. He learned the committal well enough to say it with his eyes on the family instead of the book.

In between, the ordinary came back. A sergeant whose wife had run up the cards while he was gone sat in the chair across from him with the statements in a folder on his knee.

“She says I wasn’t here,” the sergeant said.

“You weren’t.”

“I was in Iraq, sir.”

“Yes. And you weren’t here.” Sam let that sit. “Both things are true. She’s telling you the second one.”

The sergeant looked at the folder. After him came a corporal whose girlfriend had moved to Phoenix without telling him, and a chief who had decided to retire and then undecided. Sam asked the questions he knew, in the order he knew them. Sometimes, while the sergeant answered, Sam was not in the room. He came back for the answer and had to work out from the man’s face how long he had been gone. When he could not help, he went back the next day. That still worked.

❦

One Tuesday he came home at noon so Laura could go to the school. She stood in the kitchen with her keys and told him where the bottles were and how Sarah took them and what Peter was allowed to watch, and then she stood there a little longer.

“It’s a system,” Sam said.

“It’s three hours.”

“Go.”

She went. Sarah took the bottle the way Laura had said she would. Peter brought him the plastic truck, which had lost a wheel, and stood with it held out until Sam understood he was being asked. Sam found the wheel under the couch and the pin in the truck and put them together with the baby in the crook of his other arm, and Peter sat down on Sam’s boot to watch it roll, and stayed there.

When Laura came back, the three of them were on the kitchen floor. She looked at them from the doorway with her keys in her hand.

“Don’t say anything,” she said. “I’m going to remember it like this.”

❦

He preached the second Sunday he was home. Forty people came to the chapel, most of them to the back half, and the organist was a retired master sergeant’s wife who played everything a little faster than written. Sam had drafted the sermon on the aircraft between Bangor and San Diego, in the hours when the cabin was dark and nobody was asking him anything.

He read the gospel. He went up the two steps to the pulpit and opened the folder and said the first sentence, which was about coming home, and which he had thought was a good sentence at the time.

The second sentence did not come. What came instead, in its place, with its own cadence, was the prayer from the lane. Keep our judgment clear. The people who live along these roads. He did not say it. He stood with his hand on the page and the chapel quiet in front of him and understood that he could not say either thing, the prayer or the sermon, and that the people in the back half were beginning to look at one another.

He closed the folder.

“I’m going to let the reading stand this morning,” he said. “Please rise for the hymn.”

The organist found the page. Forty people stood and sang a little faster than written.

Afterward, in the room behind the chancel, the wing chaplain hung up his stole and said with his back to Sam, “You all right?”

“I lost my place.”

“Where?”

“I’ll find it,” Sam said.

He told Laura the service had been short. She said short was fine.

He preached the next Sunday from the page and did not look up. By Advent he was giving the sermons he had written before February, and nobody noticed except Laura, who had heard them the first time.

❦

“Where did you go?” she asked once, in bed, with the light off. He had been lying still for an hour and had thought she was asleep.

“Nowhere. I’m here.”

“You weren’t.”

“I’m tired,” he said.

“I’m tired too.”

“I know.”

Sarah woke at ten past three. He had been awake since two, which he had not said, and he was out of the bed before Laura moved. He carried the baby down the hall and into the kitchen, where the drawings were, and the photograph of a man in a clean flight suit, and, from the window over the sink, the corner of the sign on the garage door, which nobody had taken down. She settled against his shoulder as if she had been doing it for months, which she had, with someone else.

The refrigerator ran, and stopped. He was in the kitchen with his daughter until it started again.
