Chapter Twenty-Six

The School

Sam shut off the alarm at five. He had slept in short stretches, and his left shoulder hurt when he pushed himself upright. At five forty-five he followed Don out to the older sedan, leaving his own car beside the house.

The road into town was black, with the grass pale at its edges in the headlights. Don drove with one hand and the heater on high. He said nothing for the first mile. They passed the crossroads, where the church sign was the only light, then a diner with its chairs still up on the tables, then a man walking a dog who lifted a hand to the car. Don lifted one finger off the wheel in answer.

A few minutes after six, Don parked at the main office entrance at Calder Gap School. In the exterior lights, the older brick center and its lower additions looked like one long brown wall. A covered walkway ran from the bus curb to a side entrance.

Don unlocked the entrance and held the door for Sam. The main office smelled of paper, floor cleaner, and coffee warming in a glass pot next to the copier. Don switched on the front lights, then pointed to a binder open at the reception window. “Name, time, and the employee you’re with.”

Sam wrote Sam Artell, 6:04, and Don Rowan. A sheet of white visitor stickers lay beside the binder. He printed his name under VISITOR and pressed the sticker to his shirt.

“You make everyone do that?”

“Every visitor.” Don took a radio from a charging rack. “Come on.”

“You said I wouldn’t be helping with students.”

“You won’t. When the buses start coming, stay inside the side door.”

A staff member arrived and set her bag behind the reception desk. She looked from Sam to the visitor sticker. “Mr. Rowan brought company before sunrise.”

“Old friend,” Don said. “Be gentle with him.”

She poured coffee into a paper cup and handed it to Sam. “It’s been sitting there since five.”

Sam took a sip. “I believe you.”

“I promised bad coffee,” Don reminded him.

A woman came through the side door carrying keys and a clipboard. She was twenty years younger than Don, in a fleece with the school’s name on it and running shoes. Her face was red from the cold already.

“Marla, this is Sam Artell,” Don said. “Sam, Marla Coyle, our assistant principal. She’s at the bus entrance this morning.”

Marla shifted the clipboard to her other arm and offered Sam her hand. “Good to meet you.”

Sam followed them down the short corridor to the bus entrance. Metal pans struck a counter beyond the cafeteria doors. A custodian passed with a stack of orange cones, and the school nurse came in behind him, carrying her coat and a small black case.

Don took a fluorescent vest from a hook, put it on and clipped the radio to it. He went out to the curb while Marla held the door for Sam. Sam kept just inside, leaving the way clear. He silenced his phone. He felt a dull pressure behind his left eye. When his lower back tightened, he shifted from one foot to the other.

Beyond the covered walkway, the custodian opened the gate to the bus loop and set down two cones. Behind Sam, someone rolled a rack of cafeteria trays across the tile.

The first bus arrived while the sky was turning gray. Its headlights came along the loop ahead of it, the smell of diesel came into the doorway before the bus did, and the children’s breath showed as they came down the steps. Don greeted the driver as students descended with backpacks and instrument cases. Marla sent them on to the entrance. Most answered her good morning with their heads down. Two argued about who had copied whose homework, then separated when she pointed one at the cafeteria and the other at a hallway.

One younger student lingered at the curb after the others entered.

“My brother’s on the next bus,” he told Marla. “I said I’d wait.”

Don pointed through the entrance. “Wait inside by the window.”

The student looked out at the loop, then followed Marla inside.

Two more buses came through. When a car entered the wrong drive, Don asked the custodian over the radio to direct the driver to the front parking lot. The custodian walked to the car, pointed out the correct turn, and returned to the cones before the next bus turned in.

Don came to the doorway between arrivals and touched Sam’s arm. His radio crackled, and he went back to the curb.

The fourth bus stopped with its door across from Marla. Six students stepped down. A boy came off it at a run, the way boys came off buses, and another caught the strap of his backpack from behind.

The strap held. The first boy went down across the edge of the curb and did not get up at once.

For a second there was no sound but the engine. Then there was the sound.

Don was at the boy’s side before Sam had moved. “Inside,” he called over his shoulder. “All of you, through the doors.” Marla turned the others toward the entrance with the clipboard, and a man at the cafeteria doors held them open while they went through. Sam moved back as they crossed the threshold.

The boy who had pulled the strap stood where he was, with his hands at his sides. The first boy was sitting up now, with Don’s hand flat between his shoulder blades and blood coming from his chin onto his jacket in a line that did not stop.

Sam’s shoulder tightened against his neck. His hand was on the doorframe. He did not remember putting it there.

The nurse crossed the pavement with her black case. Don stood and stepped back to give her the room, and the other boy took a step backward too, as if the two of them were tied.

The plastic edge of Sam’s visitor sticker tapped his shirt as he stepped to the threshold.

“Sam.” Don was under the walkway roof, one hand extended toward Marla’s group. “Go behind them. If anybody stops, turns back, or leaves the group, tell Ms. Coyle.”

Sam turned from the doorway. Marla was already leading the students down the short corridor toward the cafeteria. He followed at the rear.

One boy stopped at the cafeteria doors and looked back at the walkway.

“Ms. Coyle,” Sam called. “A boy stopped at the doors.”

Marla turned. “With me, please.”

The boy looked once more toward the walkway, then followed her. Sam stayed behind him until the group entered the cafeteria.

Marla led them to two tables. One asked after the boy on the ground. She said the nurse was with him and that she would tell them when she knew more.

Sam left them with Marla and returned to the bus entrance. The hurt boy sat on the bottom step of the side entrance with the nurse crouched in front of him, holding gauze under his chin with one gloved hand and talking to him the whole time. The custodian had put a cone over the place on the curb.

The other boy kept three steps from them. Nobody had told him to move, and he had not moved. He was the biggest of the six who had got off the bus, a head above the boy with the gauze.

The man who had held the cafeteria doors came back to the bus entrance. He wore a cafeteria apron over a sweatshirt and a lanyard over the apron. He had not come all the way through the door before he spoke. “That one again. He shouldn’t be on the walkway with the little ones.”

Don turned from the step. “A boy fell,” he said. “The nurse is with him. Go back to Marla.”

“He didn’t fall. He was pulled.”

“Go back to Marla.”

The man looked at Don, then turned away from the entrance and went back to the cafeteria.

Don turned back to the step. At the far end of the loop, the custodian waved the next bus on to the front of the building. The hurt boy held the gauze himself now, both hands under his chin, and the nurse was writing on the back of her glove. The other boy stood where he had stood since the strap held, his hands at his sides.
