Chapter Thirty-Three

The Things He Kept

On Wednesday morning, Sam took his mug out of the dishwasher and carried it upstairs. He set it where his hand expected it to be, to the right of the keyboard, then moved it away before the damp ring reached the papers beneath it.

The job application was open. It wanted his most recent employer, dates and reason for leaving. There was room for four thousand characters.

He typed Position ended following a disagreement about the use of technology I developed.

He read it twice. Aisha could have described a different reason in fewer words. He deleted the sentence and went downstairs to refill his coffee.

Laura was on the phone with a supplier. When she finished, Sam asked whether she wanted him to pick up anything while he was out.

“Where are you going?”

“I haven’t decided.”

She looked at the mug in his hand.

“I need a few things for dinner. I can send you the list.”

“Do that.”

He went to the grocery store and spent ten minutes looking for a kind of rice he had bought before. At that hour the aisles were full of retired men and mothers with small children. He was the only man there of working age. The packaging had changed, or he was remembering another store. When he called Laura, she said the ordinary bag would be fine. He brought home both.

In the driveway, he answered Priya’s call with one of the bags on the passenger seat.

“Is this a bad time?”

“No.”

“Janet said you got your things yesterday. I was with somebody when you came in.”

“I saw her.”

“She said.”

He waited. A leaf had caught under the wiper. He could see the dark outline where rain had pressed it flat.

“I wanted to tell you we’ve got the evening appointments,” Priya said. “They approved the extra hours this morning. We’ll open on Thursday evenings, starting tomorrow. Two counselors have asked to take the later shift.”

“The sale paid for it.”

“Yes.”

“Graham asked you to call me?”

“No. I’ve been trying to get this agreed since summer. I thought you’d want to know.”

Sam picked up the grocery receipt and folded it around his finger.

“I do.”

“There’s a man I’ve been trying to see for three weeks. He can’t leave his shift without losing the hours, and he can’t afford to lose them. Every time I get him on the phone, we spend the call looking for a time neither of us has. I can offer him tomorrow at six.”

“Has he taken it?”

“I’m calling him next.”

There was a smile in her voice now. Sam could imagine her leaning forward at her desk, ready to take the next number. He wanted to be glad with her. He had argued for later appointments himself. He had complained about an organization that helped people find work and then asked them to leave work to be helped.

“I hope he can come,” he managed.

“Me too. Ruth has her copy of the request. She read it once, sent it to her counselor, and said yes to the distributor. And Aaron looked into that question you left on his test results. You were right about the dates. He says the model can mix up what somebody did in two different jobs. He’s fixing our copy.”

Sam shut his eyes.

“Has he told Delmere?”

“I don’t know. I only asked if it affected our appointments.”

“If they’ve got the same version, somebody needs to tell them.”

“I’ll ask him.”

Sam opened his eyes. He had spent Friday trying to keep the model out of Delmere’s hands. Now he wanted someone to send them a correction. For a moment he wished he had kept his mouth shut and left their engineers to find the fault. But somewhere a recruiter would offer a man a job on the strength of a mistake Sam knew how to fix. The man would be the one standing in the yard on the first morning, unable to do it.

“Thank you,” he said.

“You’re welcome.” Priya paused. “I wasn’t calling to argue with you about the purchase. I wanted you to have some good news.”

He looked at the house. Laura had come to the front window and was watching him through it.

“Tell me if he takes the appointment,” he said.

“I will.”

He ended the call and took the groceries inside. Laura lifted the second bag of rice from the sack.

“We’re expecting quite a crowd.”

“One’s for the winter.”

She looked at him, then put both bags in the cupboard.

Over lunch he told her about the evening appointments. She asked whether he was pleased.

“I don’t know how to be.”

“You don’t have to be pleased they sold it.”

“I know they’re different things.”

He heard the sharpness and looked down at his plate. Laura had left off spreading butter on her bread.

“Don’t snap at me. Say it.”

“I wanted to tell Priya it wasn’t worth it. She was telling me about a man who can finally come in after his shift, and I wanted to tell her that wasn’t enough.”

“Did you?”

“No.”

Laura finished buttering the bread and put down the knife.

Sam pushed his plate a little farther onto the table. She had made sandwiches, and he had taken one apart and eaten little of it.

“I wish I hadn’t built it,” he said.

Laura looked at him, then at the pieces of bread on his plate.

“I don’t know what to say to that.”

She was looking at the sandwich he had not eaten. He left it where it was.

“You used to come downstairs to tell me when somebody found a job,” she said. “Sometimes you’d start before you were through the door. I’d have to ask you to go back and tell me who you were talking about.”

He looked up. She was smiling a little, but there were tears in her eyes.

“I miss that,” she said.

He remembered wanting to get downstairs before the pleasure wore off, before he opened another record and found somebody else he had not yet helped. Laura would look up from her work and listen.

“So do I.”

She stayed at the table until he picked up the sandwich again.

He slept for a few hours that night, then woke with the sensation that somebody had asked him a question. Laura lay beside him with one hand beneath her cheek. He listened until he could distinguish the refrigerator starting downstairs from a car passing on the road.

There was no question. He got up and went to the bathroom.

When he came back, Laura had turned over.

“Are you all right?”

“I’m awake.”

“I can see that.”

He sat on the edge of the bed. She moved her foot to make room.

“Do you want the light?”

“No.”

She reached for his hand. He let her hold it, then lay down with his back against her knees. For a while he counted her breaths. He lost track and began again.

In the morning, she asked him to come with her to the nursery. She had to inspect the last plants for the bed along the wall, then meet the client at four.

“I’d only stand in your way,” he said.

“You could look around. You used to enjoy looking around.”

“I have that application to finish.”

She glanced at the stairs.

“All right. I’ll be home by six. Call if you need me.”

He said he would. She kissed him before leaving, one hand resting briefly against the back of his neck. Her fingers were cold from the keys. He heard her car back down the drive, and in the kitchen her second cup of coffee sat where she had left it, half drunk, as it did on the mornings she was late for a client.

Sam went upstairs. He entered his employment dates and left the explanation empty. The next page asked for a reference. Graham’s name was already in his list of contacts. He could not imagine asking him to speak well of his judgment, or letting a stranger phone Graham before he had asked what Graham intended to say.

He closed the application unsent.

The books he had brought home were on the floor by the desk. He picked them up one at a time and looked for space on the shelf. Sarah’s books occupied the end nearest the window. He left them where they were and carried his own to the bedroom.

The empty box was on the landing. He folded its flaps inward, then did nothing more. Sarah would be home for a break before long. His papers covered the wall above her desk. Now she would have to sleep beneath the names and photographs he had spent the last few weeks staring at. She had let him use the room while she was away. He did not want her to come home and have to ask for it back.

He brought the box back inside. From the doorway the room was more his than hers. Her lantern hung where she had left it, and her photographs were above the dresser. The desk had been pulled out for his papers, and the pages went up the wall above it as high as he had been able to tape them.

At first he worked carefully. He photographed the arrangement before removing anything, then took down the notes in order. The photographs of documents could go together. His own questions went in a separate pile. He left the paint where tape had lifted it and made a note to ask Sarah what color she wanted there before trying to match it.

He went no further than the page with Wolmeck’s name on it. For weeks he had looked at it as if another hour might make the people behind it answer him. He put it in the box. Under it lay his copy of the questions he had written for Friday’s examination.

The first question concerned Nadia and Amir. He read it and remembered Jasleen coming into reception with her phone. They were out. He had heard Nadia answer her child. He set that page on top, where he could find it again.

His phone sounded. Priya had sent a message.

He took six o’clock. Thanks for asking. I’ve just sent him the directions.

Sam sat down on the bed. He typed that he was glad, then left his thumb above the send button. If he sent it, would Priya think he had changed his mind about the sale? He began to add that he wished they had found another way to pay for it. Then he read her message again. She was telling him that the man could come. Sam deleted the qualification.

He sent it.

He almost called her to ask what he could do from home. He could look at the dates problem with Aaron, explain which records were most likely to confuse it. Aaron had not asked. Sam put the phone down.

He went back to the wall.

By late afternoon, only a few sheets remained. The sun had gone off the window and left the wall gray. He had not turned on the light. His shoulders hurt from reaching above the desk. He pulled a piece of tape slowly, trying to save the paint, and his hand began to shake. He held it still with the other hand. The tape tore.

He stood there with the paper hanging crookedly from one corner.

There was no reason for the room to be so hot. The window was closed, but the heat had not been on when he came upstairs. He tugged at his collar, then found he could not take a satisfying breath. The air went in and caught. He tried again, harder.

The room tilted slightly when he turned for the door. He put one hand on the desk. His heart was going too hard for the small effort of taking paper off a wall.

He needed to sit down. He knew that. He lowered himself to the bed and bent forward, his elbows on his knees. The edge of the desk was inches from his face. He had moved it there himself, wanting more room for the papers.

For a few seconds he could think only of the next breath. Then he was back in Graham’s office, Graham standing with the untouched coffee in his hand, and Sam hoping to hear him say there was a way to come back. He still wanted his desk and the people who came by it, and a reason to be there the next morning. He wanted all of it even after Graham had told him Delmere’s money was in the account.

The sound came back: the short impact, the engine continuing. He did not lose the room. Sarah’s bed was beneath him, the box lay open on the floor, and still he could see the girl bend over the cap. She had moved it to a smoother place. She wanted it to spin longer. He had no idea what else she wanted that day.

He put both hands over his face. He could remember the driver’s shoulder beneath his palm. The bottle waiting on the ground. He had stayed. He knew he had. But when he tried to remember sitting beside the driver, the impact came back first, and everything he had done afterward seemed too late.

If the refusal on Friday had lasted, Delmere would not have the model this afternoon, and he would not be sitting here. The thought seemed certain until he remembered Nadia saying she was with her boys. He tried to hold on to her voice. A moment later he was remembering the street narrowing through the rear opening, the people left beside the wall.

The words were quiet, as if spoken by someone who had been waiting for him to finish arguing.

They were right to send you away.

Sam lifted his head. No one was in the doorway.

He listened for a footstep on the stairs. He could not tell whether somebody had spoken or whether he was hearing his own words. He gripped the edge of the mattress until the seam pressed into his fingers.

It would be easier to stay here until Laura came home and then say he had been tired. He knew how to do that. He could wash his face, carry the box somewhere she would not have to step over it, ask about the plants. She might know something was wrong, but he could make her ask often enough that she would eventually stop for the evening.

He found his phone.

His thumb missed Don’s name the first time. He returned to the list and pressed it again. It rang long enough for him to imagine Don in a corridor with somebody at his elbow. Sam was about to end the call when his friend answered.

“Sam?”

He tried to say hello. Nothing came out.

“Are you there?”

“Yes.”

“What’s happened?”

“I can’t seem to breathe properly.”

“Where are you?”

“At home. Upstairs.”

“Is Laura there?”

“She’s with a client.”

Don asked if he had fallen or hurt himself. Sam said no. He could hear himself taking short breaths between the words.

“I want you to get help there,” Don said. “Can you call for an ambulance?”

“I don’t think I need that.”

“I can’t tell from here. I can hear you’re having trouble.”

Sam looked at the open box. He had wanted Don to answer, and now he wanted to take back the reason for the call.

“Give me a minute,” he said.

“I’m going to call Laura. I’ll have to put you on hold. Will you stay there?”

“She’s working.”

“She needs to know you’re having trouble breathing. I can’t get to you from here.”

Sam pictured Laura with her notebook, listening while her client showed her where she wanted flowers. She had offered to take him with her that morning. He had told her he wanted to finish the application. Now she would have to leave the woman, come home and find him like this. He wanted to tell Don to wait, but he could hear his own breathing over the phone.

“Tell her I’m in Sarah’s room,” he said.

“I will.”

“Don’t tell her to hurry in the car.”

“I’ll tell her what’s happening.”

Don’s voice disappeared. Sam sat with the phone beside him and watched the screen, afraid that he would touch it and lose the call. The paper above the desk hung by one piece of tape.

When Don came back, he said Laura was about fifteen minutes away. She was calling an ambulance before she left the client’s house.

He could imagine the vehicle in front of his house, the neighbors watching from behind their curtains. He could tell Don his breathing was easier now, that he just needed a few minutes to rest. If Don believed him, perhaps Laura could cancel the ambulance before it got to the house.

“Sam?”

“Let them come.”

“All right. Stay where you are. They can come upstairs.”

“The front door’s locked.”

“Laura has her key.”

Sam put the phone on speaker. He tried to loosen his hands. One finger at a time, he took them away from the edge of the mattress.

“I was taking down the papers,” he said after a while. “In Sarah’s room.”

“All right.”

“I thought I ought to put the room back before she came home.”

Don waited.

“Are you still at school?”

“In my office. Everyone I need to see has gone. Stay with me.”

The pressure in Sam’s throat eased enough for him to swallow. He wiped his mouth with the heel of his hand.

“I told you about the girl,” he said.

“Yes.”

“When I try to tell anybody what happened to me, that’s where I go. I can describe the street. I can tell you where I was sitting.” He stopped to breathe. “I keep thinking if I could get through that part properly, I could come home from it.”

Don said nothing.

“But there were people before her. There were people afterward. They kept coming to me.” Sam looked at the doorway. He remembered the small interruption of a figure waiting at the edge of where he was working, the effort to look unhurried so a man could decide whether to stay. “I wanted them to come. I used to be glad they trusted me enough.”

“I believe you.”

“I’d listen, and they’d go back. Sometimes I’d sit there after they left and I wouldn’t know what to do with myself. I couldn’t go and tell somebody what I’d just heard. I still can’t.”

“You don’t have to tell me.”

“I know. But I can’t make you understand how much of it there is.”

The last words broke apart. He pressed his hand against his mouth, too late to stop the sound. Don stayed on the line.

Sam had cried before. He had done it in rooms where nobody could hear, briefly and angrily, as though it were another task he was failing to perform with sufficient control. This would not stop when he wanted it to. He bent over his knees and tried to draw air around it.

The paper slid down the wall and settled behind the desk. Sam watched it disappear, then bent closer to the phone.

“Are you still there?”

“Yes,” Don said. “I’m still here.”
