A Use for Good MenChapter Eleven

Chapter Eleven

The Other Purpose

Sam set the drive beside Sandra Hart’s keyboard and caught his sleeve on the empty chair. He pulled at it before realizing the chair was coming with him.

“Let go of the furniture,” Sandra said. “We’ll take the software.”

Priya Shah reached around him and freed the cuff. Her hair was pulled back through a band, and she had a pen behind one ear already. She did it without a word, the way she fixed a line of his code when she saw the mistake before he did.

Sam sat. Last night Priya and Aaron Cole had listened while he explained why he might ask them to stop work they were proud of. This morning Aaron brought him a coffee and did not ask if he had slept. He had come in a gray sweatshirt and running shoes, as he came to everything. He put the cup down at Sam’s right hand, where Sam would find it when he put his hand out.

Alderwick had given the model a back room on their one floor, with a window onto the parking lot and a table carried in for the week. The computer that would hold it was a plain gray box under the table with a lock on its face. Sandra typed at it with two fingers and did not look at the keys. Through the open door Sam could see a row of desks where nobody looked up, and the corridor that went to the front.

Sandra connected the drive to Alderwick’s computer and pushed the keyboard to Sam. Sam entered the password.

Sam knew Sandra from Civic Passage’s replacement of their career platform. She read him Graham’s instructions from the previous evening: the connecting software and the notes to James, and the complete model to stay on Alderwick’s computer until the board voted.

“And if the board says no?” Sandra asked.

“Then you don’t send it,” Sam said. “Graham will tell you whether to keep Alderwick’s copy for Civic Passage or delete it.”

She read the instructions again, then began copying the files from Sam’s drive to Alderwick’s computer. Sam watched the drive’s light blink. He could have kept it in his bag. Graham would have sent somebody else with another copy, and Sam would have been at home while Delmere showed that person what they wanted. He had brought it. That was all he had agreed to so far.

James came in with his laptop under his arm and took the chair across from Sam. “Dambrosky will be here at ten,” he said. “Jasleen asked him to speak to us.”

“Did she read the report?”

“I sent it. Graham sent it too.” James glanced at the door. “Is Len coming?”

“No. He’s agreed you can examine the referral and his messages with me.”

James nodded. “I’ve read them. Rusk told me he discussed the recipient on Friday.”

“He told Len on Saturday night,” Sam corrected.

“I saw what Len wrote before Rusk replied.” James opened his laptop. “I don’t understand why Rusk needed to say Friday.”

Sam looked at him. “You should ask Dambrosky that.”

A woman from Alderwick brought in a jug of water.

“We’re still working from the six profiles you sent for the first assignments,” James said. “Delmere’s recruiters have been matching those six people against other open jobs.”

“Which jobs?” Priya asked.

James turned the laptop to face them.

Ruth Bell’s name appeared below a request for help planning deliveries to the Guard’s new posts in Meredan. A Delmere recruiter wanted to approach her about a paid planning assignment. The work could begin from home. Travel would be discussed later. Sam read the description twice. The men at those posts were too far apart to send one another supplies.

Ruth could piece together incomplete stock records and work out which men were running short of supplies. She could schedule deliveries to get to them first and find another route if a road closed.

“Has anyone asked her?” Sam said.

“Not yet.” James opened a draft of the recruiter’s offer.

“She agreed we could send her profile for the job with the distributor,” Priya put in. “Did anyone ask her about considering other work?”

James searched the correspondence attached to the proposed offer. He shook his head. “I can’t find a request.”

“Then don’t contact her about this,” Sam told him.

James typed a message to the recruiter, leaving the screen turned toward them.

Do not contact Ruth. Her permission does not cover this assignment. Civic Passage has already objected to work for the Guard.

He sent it while they watched.

Aaron leaned forward. “Why are they testing our profiles against these jobs at all?”

“Mission Support asked if your results could fill gaps in their recruiting,” James said. “I let their recruiters compare the six profiles with their open jobs.” He looked at Sam. “I should have checked the permission before doing that.”

Asking Ruth would not fix it. She could say yes and go on scheduling supplies for men who had marched families out of their houses and would not let them back.

“Show me Len’s,” Sam said.

James opened the completed referral. It described Len’s work in the Marines, coordinating vehicles that carried sealed ammunition containers, and his current job managing trucks and drivers for the freight company. Below the qualifications, Rusk recorded a conversation about payment: Len requested that the first day’s fee be paid promptly and asked how many further days might be available.

“Len told him that?”

“According to Rusk’s note,” James said.

“Where did the next comment come from?”

James enlarged it. Dambrosky had added it himself: Worked with Mercer in 2003. Reliable when arrangements changed. Completed what he accepted.

Sam read the year again. Len had told him once, with nobody else near, what had ridden with him in 2003 when the arrangements changed. The man whose company changed them had now written Len down as reliable for it. He poured water into his glass too near the rim.

Priya put a folded paper towel beside it.

“Rusk and Dambrosky added those after the referral,” James offered.

“I can see who wrote them.” Sam set the jug down. “I’m sorry. I want to know how they used them.”

“I’ll ask him,” James said.

Someone knocked, and Dambrosky came in carrying a coat over one arm. There was rain on the shoulders of it and in his hair. He had the same suit on as in Reston, and he carried nothing else. He greeted James, then Sam, and listened while Sandra introduced Priya and Aaron. Sam did not get up.

“Graham said you wanted me here before you continued,” Dambrosky said.

“I do,” Sam said.

Dambrosky hung his coat on the back of a chair. He looked at the screen. “You’ve found Len.”

“Why did you add that comment?” James asked. “About him finishing what he accepted?”

“Rusk wanted someone who wouldn’t need replacing halfway through the day. I worked with Len. I said so.”

“Did you tell Rusk anything else about him?” Sam asked.

“I told him Len could do the work.” Dambrosky pulled out the chair and sat. “Len did do it.”

Sam turned the laptop enough for Dambrosky to see the two messages from Saturday night.

“Rusk is telling people Len knew who the shipment was for before he accepted the assignment. Len says he didn’t. Here he asks who receives it, after the work is finished.”

Dambrosky bent toward the screen. He read and left the computer alone.

“Rusk says he discussed the customer earlier.”

“He discussed a government customer,” Sam said. “That isn’t the same as telling Len the name of the Guard.”

“I wasn’t at their meeting,” Dambrosky replied.

“Then tell Rusk to stop presenting his account as settled. Ask him what he actually said,” Sam pressed.

“He has already told me,” Dambrosky said.

“And you believe him?”

“I have his account and Len’s. I’m not going to accuse my manager of lying because you believe Len.”

Sam looked at the two messages. He had come to get the date corrected. With the man in front of him, it was the smallest thing in the room.

“Len was told it was coastal relief,” he said. “For one day he ran a yard. Ammunition went out of it in with the relief stores. He didn’t open a can. He didn’t go with it. He was told who it was for after he’d finished, and he’s the one who had to answer a worker who asked. Your manager put the rest in writing. He told Len it came in as relief on the program’s paperwork, so customs never opened it. He wrote that down so Len would stop asking.”

“The cargo was handled by people qualified for it,” Dambrosky said.

“I’m not asking about handling.” Sam pushed his chair back far enough to stand, then stayed in it. “You put it under a relief program because nobody fires on a relief truck. The day somebody on that coast finds out what’s in with the relief, it’s the drivers who pay for it. The people who unload it. The next truck that really is carrying food. Len did that for you on Saturday, and nobody told him who it was going to until he’d finished.”

Dambrosky watched him.

“The Guard is putting people out at gunpoint and confiscating their fishing boats,” Sam said. “Are you continuing to supply them?”

“Yes.”

James faced Dambrosky. Sam waited for the qualification.

“We support an approved partner,” Dambrosky continued. “The relationship didn’t begin with this shipment.”

“Approved by whom?”

“By the people who pay for it.”

“I read the embassy account,” Sam said. “I also read about the man who went home for his mother’s medicine.”

“So did I,” Dambrosky said.

Sam was on his feet now. The chair pressed against the backs of his legs. Dambrosky took his glasses from his shirt pocket and placed them on the table.

“The people authorizing our work want the Guard to hold that coast,” he went on. “They know the Guard has cleared the settlements. That is how the coast is being held.”

“Then why are you still doing it?”

“If we withdraw, somebody else decides who controls that coast. Those families still won’t get their houses back.”

“Your supplies help the Guard stay there,” Sam said.

“Yes,” Dambrosky agreed. “They do.”

Sam looked at Ruth’s name in the list next to Len’s. He saw her across the table at Civic Passage, talking about evenings in the pharmacy and an assignment she wanted to do again. Dambrosky did not have to frighten her into taking work. Sam could imagine her accepting an offer to work from home, planning deliveries for the Guard and being thanked for doing it well. She might never see the families those men had forced out at gunpoint. Travel would be discussed later. By then she would be routing trucks to the posts of the men who had torched that coast and driven its people out, and nobody would have told her what was in them.

“Have you asked your customers to put pressure on the Guard?” he said. “Those families should be allowed to go home.”

“That isn’t a condition of our work.”

“Then make it one,” Sam said.

Dambrosky picked up his glasses. “Sam, I didn’t come here to negotiate the government’s policy with you. Jasleen asked me to explain the work. I have.”

James closed the proposed offer and opened the message he sent the recruiter.

“We aren’t approaching Ruth Bell,” he said. “She agreed to a different use of her profile. I’m also stopping my team’s work on connecting the model to ADICUS for Mission Support until I’ve spoken to Jasleen.”

Dambrosky read the message. “On whose instruction?”

“Mine. I run that team.”

“Then speak to Jasleen before you decide which of Delmere’s divisions get to use the model once it’s in,” Dambrosky said.

“I will. We won’t do any more work for Mission Support this morning.”

“I won’t help you use the model to find more people to supply the Guard,” Sam added.

Priya closed her notebook. Aaron stayed bent toward the screen.

Dambrosky looked from them to Sam. “Civic Passage has made an agreement. Graham knows what it would cost to end it.”

“He knows what I asked him to do,” Sam said. “I’ll tell him what you said.”

“Tell him all of it. We aren’t choosing targets. We aren’t ordering those men into houses.”

“You’re helping the Guard perpetrate their violence under the guise of a relief project,” Sam seethed.

“I’ve answered that.” Dambrosky stood and lifted his coat. “Jasleen will want to speak to you.”

He paused at the door and looked at James. James stayed in his chair. He had both hands on the edge of the laptop and kept his eyes on the lid. After a moment Dambrosky put on the coat and left.

No one spoke until the outer door closed.

Sam sat down. Aaron slid the water glass back in front of him and did not look up.

Sandra turned her monitor around for Sam. The copy was complete. “I won’t send James the trained model. Does Graham still want him to have the connecting software and the notes?”

“Yes,” Sam said. “That’s his instruction. I haven’t been authorized to change it.”

James called Jasleen and left a message explaining why he had pulled his team off the work for Mission Support. He asked her to call him before they did any more work for that division.

“October seventeenth?” Sandra asked him when he put down the phone. “The Delmere teams still come in to use it?”

“That hasn’t changed. I’ll tell you if it does.”

Aaron sat back. “I’ll help you explain this to Graham,” he told Sam. “He should hear it from more than one of us.”

“So will I,” Priya said.

Sam looked at them. “Thank you.”

His phone vibrated against the water glass. Webb’s name appeared above the number he called in August. For a moment Sam could not remember which message the man would be answering.

He excused himself and stepped into the corridor.

“Webb?”

“Chaplain. You still using this number?”

“You called it.”

“I’ve left messages with strangers before.”

Sam recognized the patient irritation beneath the older voice. He stopped at a vacant desk.

“Thanks for calling back.”

“I was hoping your second message would tell me why you wanted to talk about Wolmeck.”

“They’re part of a company my employer is working with. I just sat in a room with Dambrosky.” Sam put a hand on the desk. “I need to understand what his people were asking us to do when we were there.”

“I can’t tell you all of it.”

“What you saw would help.”

Webb was quiet long enough for Sam to hear traffic at his end.

“I’ve still got my watch notebook. My own notes, nothing classified. Things I wrote so the man relieving me knew where we’d sent the vehicles. Don’t expect everybody’s reasons to be in it.”

“I won’t.”

Sam heard how quickly he answered. Across the corridor a woman approached the vacant desk with a bowl of soup. He moved before she had to ask.

“Could we meet?” he said.

“Tomorrow morning. I can come up. Find somewhere we won’t be interrupted.”

Sam gave him the office address.

“Just you,” Webb said.

“Just me.”
