A Use for Good MenAuthor's Note

Author's Note

In 2003, I served as a Navy chaplain with Marine units in Iraq. I returned home carrying experiences I could describe and others for which I did not yet have adequate language. Years later, the concept of moral injury gave me a way to understand some of that weight.

Moral injury can follow when a person participates in, witnesses, fails to prevent, or is unable to prevent something that violates deeply held moral convictions. It may also arise from betrayal by leaders or institutions. Moral injury can overlap with post-traumatic stress, but it is not the same thing, nor is it itself a psychiatric diagnosis. Its deepest wounds often concern meaning: what a person believes about duty, responsibility, goodness, faith, other people, and oneself.

This novel grew from my desire to explore that territory, especially the distance between responsibility and omnipotence. A person may have acted with courage, honored necessary limits, or made the best choice available and still hear a harsher verdict: You should have known. You should have stopped it. You did nothing. Moral injury can turn devotion itself into evidence against the person who was devoted.

That emotional territory is personal to me. The story is not.

This book is a work of fiction, not memoir, testimony, or a reconstruction of my deployment. Sam Artell is not me. The characters, families, organizations, programs, documents, conversations, and events have been invented or substantially transformed. No protected pastoral confidence has been reproduced, and no character should be understood as a portrait of a particular Marine, family member, unit, command, contractor, or employer.

Fiction allowed me to approach emotional truths without claiming other people’s stories. It also allowed me to ask what moral repair might mean when certainty is impossible, accountability remains necessary, and no one can undo what happened.

This novel offers no clinical answer. I hope only that it makes moral injury more visible, and that it leaves room for justice, mercy, humility, and the possibility of repair.
