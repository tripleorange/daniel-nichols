Chapter Sixteen

The Price of Stopping

Aaron was already in Graham’s office when Sam arrived. He stood with one hand on the spare chair, looked from it to Sam, and sat again.

“You can stay there,” Sam said.

“I was trying to let you get past.”

Sam sat by the door. Graham closed it behind him and stood with one hand on the knob.

“I’ve asked Aaron to finish the work at Alderwick,” he said. “The connection, and checking the results with Delmere’s people.”

Aaron looked directly at Sam. “I’m not doing the Mission Support part. James’s order still stands. Nothing gets connected for that division.”

“Dambrosky intends to use the model for his division.”

“I heard him at Alderwick.”

“Then why finish it?”

“Because I helped build it, and I want to be there when they test it with ADICUS.” Aaron moved his knee away from the desk. “Graham could send somebody who wasn’t there when they tried to recruit Ruth. If they’re still using it to recruit for the Meredan Regional Guard’s shipments, I want the board to hear that before they vote and Alderwick hands the model over.”

Sam looked at the extra chair. He had helped hire Aaron. They spent most of one winter arguing over a way to compare accounts of the same work written by different people. Aaron had taken the whiteboard most of those afternoons, with a marker he did not cap, and when he was wrong he said so before Sam had to.

“You aren’t obliged to do this for me,” Sam said.

“I’m not doing it for you.” Aaron’s voice softened. “I’m not doing it against you either.”

Graham sat. His jacket was over the back of his chair. Sam had not seen him with it off in that office. “You still have your other work, Sam. I want you in the room when Delmere runs the model at Alderwick, before the board votes on delivery.”

“Have you spoken to Jasleen about the people watching my wife?”

“I spoke to her this morning. She said she hadn’t known anything about it. She’s taken it to their chief executive. Our lawyer has your account and Laura’s permission to contact her.”

“What did the chief executive say?”

“Jasleen hasn’t called back.”

Sam waited. Graham opened a drawer, then closed it again empty-handed.

“There is something else,” Graham said. “The directors elected Dambrosky this morning.”

Sam felt Aaron turn his way.

“Evelyn knew about Laura?”

“Yes. I forwarded your message last night.”

“And they appointed him anyway,” Sam said.

“Evelyn asked whether the man had approached Laura. I told her he’d stayed across the road. She said the man with the camera had gone too far. She’s asked our lawyer to take it up with Delmere, but she didn’t want to delay the appointment.”

“Why not?”

“He offered to bring people to our next fundraising dinner. People Evelyn has been trying to reach for years. She believes we can deal with the complaint and still accept his help.”

“He had my wife followed to a client’s house,” Sam said. “Read it again if you need to. He had her followed, and this morning they put him on the board.”

“I read your account.” Graham drew a folder closer. “Jasleen has also sent a purchase proposal. Thirty million dollars.”

Aaron sat back. Sam stayed where he was.

“For what, exactly?” Sam asked.

“Everything we hold. The model as trained, the connecting software, the patent application. We could go on using the model in our own services. Delmere would decide where else to use it.”

“What happens to the stop on Mission Support?”

“It applies to the installation we’re preparing now, under the license. If they buy the model outright, that agreement ends. Delmere would own it and connect it where they chose.”

“They’re offering to buy the right to do what we’ve told them we oppose,” Sam said.

“They’re offering to buy something valuable. We would have to decide what terms we can accept.” Graham looked at Aaron, then back to Sam. “Dambrosky has disclosed his Delmere interest. He won’t vote on the sale. The other directors will have to make that decision.”

“After listening to him.”

“After listening to us too,” Graham said.

Sam read the first paragraph of Jasleen’s letter. She described the model as a way to find experience that existing searches failed to recognize.

Graham put a hand on the corner of the proposal. “You know what this would pay for.”

“For how long?”

“For years, Sam. We could hire more counselors here and open offices in other cities. We could set enough aside to keep paying the staff when donations fall.”

“There’s something else you should hear before you speak to them,” Graham went on. “Martin has told Evelyn and me that if we refuse him, Delmere will build a model of their own. He says they could have one working by spring.”

“Does she believe him?”

“She wrote it down.”

Aaron looked down at his hands.

“Send me the proposal,” Sam said. “And a time to speak to the directors.”

“I will.” Graham withdrew his hand.

Sam stood. He could have left Aaron in the office. Instead he held the door while his colleague gathered his notebook.

They walked past the reception desk together.

“Let me know how the installation is going,” Sam said.

“I will. I’d still like your help if you see a problem.”

Sam nodded, then understood how Aaron could take it. “I’ll tell you if I find one. I haven’t agreed to finish the installation.”

“I heard you.” Aaron stopped at the stairs. “Priya wants to have lunch with both of us. She told me to ask before you said you were busy.”

“I’m busy.”

“That was what she expected.”

“Tell her I’ll come down,” Sam allowed.

❦

Dambrosky called before lunch. Sam let the phone ring twice, watching the name above the answer button.

“Graham said you’d spoken,” Dambrosky said when Sam answered.

“He told me about your appointment.”

“Then we should try to work together.”

“Are you going to stop having people watch Laura?”

“I’ve told the firm to suspend the observation.”

“Permanently?”

“It’s suspended. Jasleen has asked for an explanation.”

Sam wrote suspended on the back of an envelope.

“What do you want?”

“To resolve the problem with Len. I’ve gone back over what Rusk told him. Rusk discussed government transport on Friday. He didn’t identify the Guard until Saturday.”

“You weren’t going to accuse him of lying.”

“I asked more questions.”

“He can correct it with Len’s lawyer.”

“I can arrange that. We’ll withdraw Rusk’s statement that Len knew the shipment was for the Guard before he took the job. His lawyer will have it from us.”

“Then send it.”

“I want you to stop asking Civic Passage to abandon the entire installation. I’ll give Len’s lawyer the correction, and you tell Graham you’re willing to continue on the terms we’ve agreed.”

Sam’s pen hovered above the envelope. Len could refuse another job. He couldn’t stop Rusk telling people he had known all along whom he was helping. If that story got to his employer, Len would have only his own word against Delmere’s. A correction from Dambrosky would change that. Sam could hear Len telling Denise already.

“Tell Len’s lawyer,” Sam said. “But don’t make it depend on whether I stop opposing the installation.”

For a few seconds Sam heard only a low murmur at Dambrosky’s end. The man spoke to someone away from the phone, then returned.

“People are still going to take these jobs, Sam. You can keep arguing with us, or you can help Len get his name cleared.”

Sam put down the pen and stood up. He remembered Ruth’s name beside Len’s on the screen at Alderwick.

“Send the correction to Len’s lawyer,” he repeated. “I’m still opposing the installation.”

Dambrosky ended the call.

Sam wrote the word to Laura the way Dambrosky had used it, suspended, and said the man had promised nothing past that. She wrote back that suspended was his word and not hers, and they would talk when he got home.

Then he wrote to Len: Dambrosky would withdraw Rusk’s claim, and Sam had promised nothing to get it.

Downstairs, Priya had found a table near the window. She had her coat over her shoulders with her arms out of the sleeves, the way she sat at her desk from October on. Aaron was opening a bag of chips with more effort than the task should require.

“Give it here,” Priya said as Sam sat.

Aaron gave the bag one last pull. The top came off in his hand, spilling chips over the table.

Sam helped him gather them. For several minutes they discussed Priya’s leaking kitchen ceiling. No one asked Sam to agree to anything. He ate half his sandwich before checking the time.

❦

When Jasleen called at two, Sam closed his office door. The office was small enough that the door, closed, was at his back wherever he sat.

Jasleen introduced Nadia Faris as a Delmere employee in Meredan. Nadia arranged transport for local employees and sorted out their pay. Her camera showed a plain wall, the back of a chair and one edge of a curtain.

“Nadia volunteered after our personnel office asked for someone to speak with you,” Jasleen said. “She understands you’re with Civic Passage.”

Nadia leaned closer to her screen. She was about forty and sat square to the camera. Her English came in short complete sentences that she did not hurry. “You help people find work?”

“Yes,” Sam said. “I built the part that reads a veteran’s record and suggests work. Delmere wants to use it to hire.”

“Then I would like to talk about the work.” She looked to one side. “Ms. Bedi, may we speak alone?”

“Of course,” Jasleen agreed. “Sam has my number. You have it too.”

Jasleen’s picture disappeared. Nadia watched until Jasleen’s name vanished from the list below the call.

“Are you recording?”

“No.” Sam moved his phone away from the computer. “There’s nobody else here.”

“Good. I asked my husband to take our boys out for something to eat. If they come back, I will tell you.”

“Is it late there?”

“Late for them to be rewarded with a second dinner.” She smiled briefly. “Please don’t write that down.”

Sam moved his hand away from the pen. “I wasn’t going to.”

“I was joking.”

“I know. I’ve been slow all day.”

She settled back. “Our office said you wanted to ask about the people leaving the coast. They asked me to tell you that the work is useful. Some of it is. I help people get home when a shift ends after the buses stop. I make sure someone asks about a missing payment before a family runs out of money. I am good at that.”

“I believe you.”

Nadia reached below the screen and came back with a tissue. “Please don’t send this conversation to our office. I need my job.”

“I won’t send it to them,” Sam said.

“I’ve asked for help here. When they asked for someone to speak to you, I thought I could try again.” She pressed the tissue between her palms. “But I’m afraid of what my manager will do if he hears what I’ve told you.”

Sam waited.

“Some of the men we transport spend the day helping the Guard. Last month one of our drivers, Amir, brought his aunt and uncle to the office. They said the soldiers came the morning after the shelling and put them on one of our trucks. For their safety, the truck’s driver told them. They wanted us to help them get home.”

“What did your manager do?”

“He told me to send them to the district office. They had already gone there. The man at the district office told them to speak to us because their nephew worked for Delmere.” Nadia looked down. “They were carrying a ceiling fan. It was the only thing they managed to take that they could sell.”

“Were you able to find them somewhere to stay?”

“I called a group that helps workers and their families find lawyers and temporary rooms. I’ve used them when employees couldn’t pay rent. They found a room for Amir’s aunt and uncle.”

“Are they still living there?”

“With another relative now. Amir says the Guard won’t let them go home.” Nadia began folding the tissue. “He went with two other men to ask when their families could go back. He told me the Guard let him and one of the men leave. They’re still holding the other man. His wife goes to ask for him. They won’t let her see him.”

“Has anyone at Delmere asked the Guard to release him?”

“I asked my manager to do that. He told me the man wasn’t our employee.”

“Do they want to leave the coast?”

“Leave?” Nadia looked up. “They want their house. The uncle has a boat on the beach below it. He asks Amir every week if anyone has moved it. He wants to know if it is still his.”

“Does Jasleen know what you just told me?”

“I don’t know. I know what I told the office here.”

“May I tell her and Janet Yoon, the colleague helping me at Civic Passage?”

Nadia looked down at the tissue. “Those two,” she said. “Tell Ms. Bedi I asked for help. Use my name. But ask her to call me before she speaks to our office. I want her to hear from me before my manager hears what I’ve told you.”

Sam waited before answering. He had heard that desire from people who hoped the presence of an officer might make another man behave differently. Sometimes it worked. Sometimes the man waited until the officer left. He had walked the edge of a water point once with a lance corporal who wanted his staff sergeant to go easier on him without being told why. The gravel at the edge was white in the dark, and the Marine kept his hands in his pockets the whole way round, and when they came back to where they had started he said he had said enough.

“I’ll ask her to call you first,” he said.

“Your company is helping ours find more people. I wanted you to hear about those families before you helped us hire anyone else.”

He imagined her calling again to say the Guard had killed the man they were holding, and himself listening with the model connected and Delmere’s recruiters already using it.

Aaron’s unanswered email was open on the other screen. Sam sat back from the keyboard.

“I don’t want another call from our local office,” Nadia insisted. “I want to speak to her.”

“I’ll call her now.”

Nadia gave him a number that was not the office’s and ended the call. Sam called Jasleen with Nadia’s voice in his ear and asked her to call Nadia first, before the local office. She said she would, that afternoon.

He told Janet in three lines, then returned to Aaron’s email.

He began to explain how Aaron could finish the connection, then deleted the instructions. Instead he wrote that he would examine the results with Aaron but would not help connect the model to ADICUS.

Sam sent the reply. Then he opened their shared folder. The connecting code was there, and nobody had taken away his access.

There was time to rewrite it.
