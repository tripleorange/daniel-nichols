Chapter Nineteen

His Own Name

James had chosen a table with four chairs and put his jacket on the one nearest the wall. When Sam came up, he moved the jacket.

“You want the end,” he said.

“I usually do.”

The coffee shop was alongside a grocery store on the edge of Frederick. It was warm inside and loud with the espresso machine. The glass had fogged along its bottom edge. Through the window, a man was persuading two children that a cart could carry groceries or children but would struggle with both. James watched them while Sam took off his coat.

“I bought you coffee,” James said. “Then realized I didn’t know what you put in it.”

“Coffee.”

“Good. I got that far.”

He pushed a cup across to Sam. A small paper bag lay against it, folded carefully at the top.

“I haven’t eaten,” James said. “If you don’t mind.”

“You asked me to meet you. You can eat.”

James took out a pastry, found that its chocolate had melted against the paper and tried to free it without losing the filling. He gave up and tore the bag down one side.

His message had come that morning while Sam and Laura were clearing the kitchen table. Could we talk somewhere near you? I can be there by eleven. Sam showed Laura the message and told her where they would meet. She nodded and went on putting the plates away.

Laura had called the boys the night before, one at a time, from the kitchen table, with the phone flat in front of her and her hands on either side of it. Michael offered to drive down and was told not yet. He asked a second time. Laura turned the phone so that Sam could answer him, and Sam told him the same thing. Peter listened to all of it and asked one thing, whether Sarah knew. Then they had called Sarah together. Sam had sat with Laura and answered what he could about the man outside the venue. When Sarah asked why anyone would photograph her mother, Laura had looked at him. He had given her the answer about the model, which was true and was not all of it. Sarah wanted them both on the phone again tonight.

James wiped his fingers on the torn bag. “I saw the photographs the observation firm sent Dambrosky.”

Sam left the coffee where it was.

“Of Laura?”

“Laura. You. Sarah.”

“Where was Sarah?”

“At her college. I’ll show you.” James opened the narrow bag at his feet. “I need to tell you why I have them first.”

“Show me my daughter.”

James took out a folder and found the page. He passed it across the table with the picture facing Sam.

Sarah stood outside a brick building beneath a long row of windows. One hand held an insulated water bottle emblazoned with the school’s crest. Her other hand was raised, two fingers extended toward someone beyond the edge of the photograph. She wore the blue sweatshirt with the cuff she picked apart whenever she studied. A young man near her had a bicycle between his knees.

Sam touched the lower corner without meaning to. Her face was turned away from the camera, toward whoever had made her smile. He looked for the thing in the picture that would tell him where the man had stood, a shadow on the path or the angle of a window, and found nothing.

“Wednesday,” James told him. “That’s the date on it. The firm sent these Thursday. Before Martin said he’d suspended the observation.”

The printed caption named Sarah and her college near Cumberland. Under it were the words public campus.

“Who took it?”

“I don’t know which person. The firm sent a group of images with their report.”

“Did someone speak to her?”

“The report doesn’t say they did. I can’t tell you that nobody spoke to her.”

“How close did he stand?”

“I can’t tell from the image. A long lens does some of that.”

“Or he stood on the path with her.”

Sam looked through the window. The man with the children was at his car. One child held the cart steady while the other tried to open the passenger door. No one was looking at the coffee shop.

“Does Jasleen have this?”

“I sent it to her yesterday. I asked her to call you about it.”

“She hasn’t.”

James took his phone from the table and turned it facedown. “Then you should hear it from me.”

He passed over the other photographs. Laura was by her truck at the garden center. Sam recognized the shirt she wore Thursday, one sleeve pushed above the elbow. Another page named the venue and the time of her appointment there. He pictured the man sitting in his car while Laura met with her client, then following her when she left.

The last image showed Sam outside Civic Passage. He was bent over his bag, looking for something. He remembered the irritation of knowing the badge was in it and being unable to find it.

“Why did they send these to you?”

“They didn’t. Martin added me to the correspondence. He asked if knowing about the observation would make you stop opposing the installation.”

“What did you tell him?”

“That it would make everything worse.”

“Is that all?”

James put the torn bag aside. “No. I told him to end it. Then I went to Jasleen.”

Sam turned Sarah’s photograph over. The page was blank on the back.

“When did he ask you to start advising him about me?”

“In July.”

James was across the table, his hands on either side of the opened folder. He was thin and sat upright. He had not touched his own coffee since Sam arrived.

“Before we met,” Sam said.

“Yes.”

James took out another set of pages. Sam’s name sat below the Delmere heading. The date was July 18. A photograph from the Civic Passage website showed him standing with a counselor and a veteran whose face he remembered before the name came back.

Sam had liked that photograph. He was listening to the other man, caught between looking solemn for the camera and laughing at something the man said.

“We were looking for methods our system didn’t have,” James said. “I’d seen the paper about combining duties across assignments. Jasleen asked whether it was worth approaching Civic Passage. I said yes.”

“And then you made this.”

“I wrote the technical section. I also sent Martin the video of your presentation.”

Sam found James’s name above a paragraph describing the model. James wrote that other engineers could run it and use it to find abilities that a veteran’s profile did not name. He described how it compared the work done across several assignments, finding strengths a recruiter might miss by considering each job separately.

Below the paragraph was a recommendation: buy all of it, the software, the model Sam had trained and the rights to it, not a license.

“You understood it before you came to Arlington.”

“I understood the paper. I needed to see it work and talk to you. That’s why I asked about the correction to the utility case.”

“Martin knew you wanted it.”

“Yes.”

Sam turned the page. His Navy service appeared under his civilian work, followed by Iraq and the year. Alongside that entry was a second source: a Wolmeck staff list, kept on file when Delmere bought the company. The excerpt listed him as a Navy chaplain providing religious support to Marines.

He read it again. His name, his role, the place. Nothing about the girl. Nothing anyone had told him while asking him to keep it private. Wolmeck had kept a list before this one. Five names on a clipboard in a yard on the river, and none of the five had lived out the night.

“Why did they use that?”

“Someone in Martin’s office found the older entry when they checked your name. Martin wanted it included.”

“Who?”

“I didn’t see who found it. I saw the entry after it was added.”

Sam moved his thumb off the date. “Was I in ADICUS?”

“Yes.” James pointed to a line above the sources. “It isn’t only recruits. When we’re deciding whether to approach an organization, we put the people there in ADICUS too. I recommended that we approach you.”

“Because of the model?”

“And because I wanted to work with you. I thought you’d help us find people our recruiters kept missing.”

“And the Navy?”

James looked at the next page before answering. “Martin wrote that part.”

Sam turned it over.

The judgments were short. Dambrosky expected Sam to resist a personal financial incentive. He expected the promise of more work for veterans to keep him interested. In a paragraph below them, he advised tying the new services for veterans to Sam’s staying on.

Artell is unlikely to withdraw if he believes the people he serves will lose assistance as a result.

Sam read the sentence aloud. He read it a second time, with his finger under his own name. James did not interrupt him.

“He wrote that before he knew me.”

“He’d seen your public work. He knew your background.”

“That is not knowing me.”

“No,” James agreed. “It isn’t.”

There was another sentence about asking colleagues whom Sam respected to encourage his participation. James’s name appeared next to Jasleen’s.

Sam looked up.

“Did he tell you to agree with me in that room?”

“He told me he wanted us working together. He didn’t tell me to change the screen. You were right about the training. I changed it.”

“Did he tell you to ask for the hour the next morning?”

“No.” James’s voice was firmer now. “I wanted the hour with you. You’d found a way to recognize experience our searches missed. I wanted to learn how you did it, and I thought you wanted my help too. I still want to work with you, Sam.”

Someone at the counter called a name. A woman at the next table stood, leaving a coat over her chair and a paperback facedown by her cup.

Sam looked back at the assessment. The older directory entry occupied less than three lines. For nineteen years he had kept what men told him in confidence to himself. A clerk’s list could place him in Iraq. A recording of him talking about veterans could tell Dambrosky where to press.

He knew the subjects on the pages. He did not recognize the man they had assembled from them.

“Why did you help him?” Sam asked, and raised his head.

James’s pastry lay in the opened paper, half eaten.

“I thought Martin would make you a better offer if he understood why you built the model,” James answered. “More money for Civic Passage, more people finding work. I wanted that too. Then he asked me what having your family watched would do to you.”

“Did you help him decide where they’d go?”

“No. I learned where they’d been from the same report you’re holding.”

Sam put the assessment behind the photographs. “I need copies.”

“You can take these. Laura and Sarah should see the pictures. I want your outside lawyer to have them too, with my account of how I received them.”

“I can call Graham.”

“I will speak to the lawyer myself. Ask Janet for a time tomorrow. Don’t forward the assessment around or take it to Martin.”

“He needs to know I have it.”

“Why?”

“So he knows he can’t do this without somebody finding out.”

James leaned forward. “He told you it was suspended. That didn’t stop him defending it.” He looked at the plate. “I’m back in that building on Monday. Let me give the lawyer what I have.”

“Are you afraid he’ll fire you?”

“Yes.” James did not lower his voice. “I also want to keep him from giving Mission Support the model.”

Sam opened his mouth, then shut it.

“The restriction is recorded,” he managed.

“Yes, it’s recorded. Martin can still replace me and tell the next engineer to finish connecting the model for Mission Support.”

“Don’t identify me to him,” James warned. “If you quote it, he’ll know where it came from. I want to say it myself first.”

“I won’t tell him you helped me.”

James watched him. “All right.”

Sam put the pages into the folder. Sarah’s photograph was on top. He moved it behind the others before closing the cover.

Outside, James stopped at his car and checked his phone.

“Tell Laura I’m sorry,” he said.

“You can tell her yourself.”

“I would like to.”

Sam held out his hand. James took it. For a moment Sam wanted him to come back to the house, sit at the dining table and explain the pages to Laura while Sam could listen.

“I’ll ask Janet to call you,” he said.

James nodded and got into his car.

Sam stood beside his own until James had left. The wind came across the lot with nothing in its way, and he got his coat closed before he took out the phone. Then he called Laura.

“I have something we need to show Sarah,” he told her. “Photographs. From before Dambrosky said he’d stopped.”

“Suspended,” Laura corrected.

“Yes. Suspended.”

“Come home. We’ll call her together.”

Sam agreed. Before starting the car, he sent Janet a message asking her to arrange for James to speak privately with the outside lawyer. He named no documents and attached nothing. James could tell her what he was willing to provide.

The folder lay on the passenger seat. He looked at its closed cover while the car warmed.
