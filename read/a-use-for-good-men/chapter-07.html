Chapter Seven

Useful Work

Sam was in Arlington most days now, in a small office off the counselors’ corridor that nobody else had wanted. It had a door. The corridor ran off the open floor past the copier and the counselors’ offices, each with a chair outside it for whoever was next. At the end was the small meeting room, with Graham’s office across from it. His own room had been a storage room. The desk sat too low for him, the extra chair was kept to the wall, and a calendar from 2019 that somebody else had hung still showed the month they had left it at.

On the first Saturday of September, Laura’s bride and her mother were in their seats beneath the cedars. Before it was light, Sam had walked the length of the walkway behind Laura and her flashlight, pressing each joint with his boot. The panels were wet, and the cedars dripped on them both when the wind moved. Laura walked ahead with the light down on the joints and said nothing until she reached the gravel. There she put the flashlight in her pocket and leaned on his arm until there was enough light to see the barn. That night he fell asleep on the sofa with one shoe on. Some evenings since, he had brought his laptop downstairs while she worked. Once she had to ask him to close it. Another time he noticed she had gone quiet, shut it himself, and asked her to begin again.

Webb had not returned his call. Sam sent one short email in case the old number was no longer checked. He did not send another.

On the last Thursday of September, Ruth Bell stopped outside the meeting-room door and put a hand against the wall. She was short, in a pharmacy polo shirt with a jacket over it, her hair pulled back and pinned. She kept her bag on her shoulder as though she had not meant to stay.

Sam stood from his chair. Through the glass, her counselor was gathering the papers from the call.

“Are you all right?”

Ruth nodded, then shook her head. “They asked what I’d charge.”

“For another assignment?”

“For two. I said I’d send them a price tomorrow.” She looked at him. “I didn’t ask what they were willing to pay.”

He waited while she took her hand from the wall.

“I’ve been telling people I’ll accept less than the last job paid,” she admitted. “I nearly did it again.”

The counselor opened the door and held it for them. Ruth went back inside. Sam followed when Ruth motioned him in, ducking under a paper decoration left over from someone’s birthday.

He had met her at the beginning of September, when she came to collect the summary of her service experience. When her counselor asked if she would tell the model’s developer what she found useful, Ruth had wanted to know that it would not take longer than half an hour. She worked evenings at a pharmacy.

Ruth put a folder on the table. “They’ve asked me to help their managers practice the plan. Different sites. More people.”

“Take tonight to work out how much time you’ll need for both jobs,” the counselor said.

“That’s why I said tomorrow.”

Sam sat at the end of the table. Ruth took out a sheet with his model’s familiar arrangement of headings. The paper was soft along its two folds.

“I nearly didn’t let you send the profile,” she told the counselor. “I thought they wanted a consultant.”

“They did,” the counselor agreed.

Ruth smiled down at the page. “I know that now.”

Her first assignment concerned the distribution company’s plan for essential deliveries after a prolonged power failure. In the Air Force she had managed spare parts for aircraft. Later she helped plan transport for exercises. Her résumé kept the two jobs apart. His model had put them together.

Sam remembered her pencil at that first meeting. She put a question mark against the proposed work and made him wait while she read it again.

“The man on the call today wanted everyone arriving at once,” Ruth said. “A hundred trucks, no power, and he wanted the same shift to meet all of them.”

“What did you tell him?”

“That the building hadn’t grown another door because he’d called it an emergency.”

Her counselor laughed. Ruth joined her, briefly, then tapped the folder.

“I was more polite than that.”

“I heard you,” the counselor said. “You were very polite.”

Sam could picture her saying it, the same pencil motionless in her hand.

On the sheet before her, Ruth had marked a second recommendation. Below it was the condition Sam and James had agreed to print with each capability. For this one it read: further training, and work under someone qualified, before she coordinated shipments that needed special handling.

“I’d like to do this too,” Ruth said, turning the page for the counselor. “If I can do the training without losing my evening shifts.”

“I’ll find out which providers offer it part time,” the counselor said. “Then we can look at employers who train people on the job.”

Ruth touched the line again. “That includes dangerous loads?”

“It could,” Sam allowed. “You know how to coordinate the people and equipment. You’d still have to learn the handling rules for the cargo, and work under someone who knows them.”

“I don’t want to learn those by getting them wrong.”

“You shouldn’t have to learn that way,” Sam told her.

She took back the page and put it in the folder. “I’m going to ask the distribution company to pay for the training. If they want me to do that work too.”

He opened his mouth to add what Civic Passage could do about a course but closed it. She was already deciding what to ask the client for.

“That’s a good question to put to them,” the counselor said.

Ruth closed the folder. “I thought you’d want to know if somebody used the thing you made.”

“I did,” Sam said. “Thank you for coming back.”

“I came back because I want more money. Thanking you was on the way.”

He laughed. She stood, took up her bag, and made another appointment with the counselor before leaving.

Sam left the room and went to find Janet.

❦

Janet was standing at the copier with her phone pressed to one ear. When she ended the call, he told her about the additional assignments and Ruth’s determination to set a price.

“Her counselor told me she’s asking about training now,” Janet said. “She wouldn’t discuss anything that took her away from paid work when she first came in.”

“She’s going to ask her client to pay for it.”

“Good.” Janet put her phone in her pocket. “I wish more people would ask their clients to pay for training.”

Graham appeared at the corridor door with his jacket over his arm. “You both have a minute?”

Sam looked at Janet. She closed the copier and followed Graham into the small meeting room. Sam took the chair nearest the door.

Graham left his jacket over the back of a spare chair. “The board has approved the three-year partnership and the license. Twelve million over the three years. The conditions on delivery stay as we discussed. We have October seventeenth reserved at Alderwick for the three Delmere teams to run it while we watch.”

Janet sat back. For a moment she looked at the empty chair opposite her.

“Then I can finish the counselor interviews,” she said.

“You can make the offers,” Graham told her. “I told finance this morning.”

Sam knew about the two vacancies. He had heard a counselor apologize to a veteran for the earliest time she could offer, then work through her lunch to see him sooner.

“And the purchase?”

“Jasleen hasn’t made an offer to buy it. We’re licensing the model, not selling it. These hires come out of the license money either way.”

Janet put both hands on the table. “When does the first payment arrive?”

“Monday. Finance has the schedule,” Graham said.

She nodded and took out her phone. Sam watched her enter something, erase it, and begin again.

“James wants your team and Alderwick together next week,” Graham said to Sam. “The six profiles we sent are in ADICUS now. Delmere’s recruiters can read them there.”

“Only the profiles,” Sam said. “The model that made them still isn’t at Alderwick.”

“I know,” Graham said. “That’s why they want you there.”

Sam’s eyes went to the window in the door. A counselor walked past with a young man who was talking with both hands. She heard him out before pointing out another room.

“I’ll arrange it with James,” he said.

Graham checked his watch and stood. “Thank you. Both of you.”

He took his jacket and left them in the room. Janet kept her seat, phone in hand.

“Two counselors,” Sam said.

“Two people who’ve been waiting for an answer from me.” She put the phone away. “I’ll call them after lunch.”

❦

Len called while Sam was putting the laptop into its case. The counselors’ doors were shut for the night, and the copier had gone quiet. What light there was came down the corridor from the one bank on the open floor that had stayed on. Sam closed the office door before answering.

“You busy, Chaplain?”

“I’m at work. Is this a good time for you?”

“I’m home. I wanted to tell you they paid me.”

Sam sat down. “For the advisory job?”

“Finished last week. They made a couple of changes after the last call. Today the money’s in the account.”

“How was the work?”

“Good. Somebody asked what would happen if they lost half their vehicles, and then stayed on the phone while I told them.”

Sam smiled. “An unusual client.”

“They called back. That was the surprise.” Len paused. “You can tell your people I said that. The work was as they described it, and they paid me.”

“I’ll tell Janet.”

“Tell her the counselor was good too. She read what I’d sent before asking me to explain it.”

“They’ve offered me another job,” Len went on. “More than advising this time.”

“Through Delmere?”

“One of their transport managers called. They need somebody to coordinate a government shipment. Check that the vehicles and the people are where they’re supposed to be, deal with delays. A few days. They said the first assignment showed them I could do it.”

“Would you be responsible for the cargo too?”

“No. They’ll have their own specialists handling it. I’d be coordinating the people and vehicles. I told him I won’t take charge of something I’m not qualified to handle.”

“And he agreed?”

“He did. I’m meeting him before we start so he can show me exactly what I’d be running.”

Sam had read all six of the first assignments and asked who could change them. The Delmere manager who wrote them had told him on a morning call how it worked: if a client wanted more than the description said, Delmere wrote a new description and the adviser agreed to it or didn’t. Nobody had sent Sam this one. A government shipment. The phrase sat where another had once sat for him, on a board in a supply yard in 2003: local security stores.

“Have you spoken to your counselor?”

“Yesterday. She asked for the description too. Delmere sent it. Janet’s office said they could continue handling the referral. I’m not asking you to check it, Sam.”

He did not hesitate over Sam’s name.

“I know,” Sam said.

“I’m telling you because I accepted.”

Sam looked at the closed laptop case. “When do you begin?”

“Tomorrow I meet the manager. The work starts Saturday. I’ve arranged time off.”

“A shipment of what?”

“He shows me tomorrow. I should have asked on the phone. I didn’t.”

“Does Denise know?”

“We talked before I said yes.” Len’s voice flattened slightly. “She doesn’t need to hear about it from anybody else.”

“I wasn’t going to call her.”

“All right.”

“I’m glad the first work went well,” Sam said. “And I’m glad you called.”

“I said I would.”

“You can call again.”

“I know where to find you now.”
