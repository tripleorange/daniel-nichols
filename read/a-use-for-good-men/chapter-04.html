Chapter Four

The Tomb of Ezra

In 2003, a helicopter set Sam down at an Army airfield in southern Iraq amid hard wind, dust, and hot exhaust.

He ducked beneath the force of it and followed the crew chief’s arm toward a low wall of sandbags. The desert-tan flight suit snapped against his legs. His helmet pulled at one shoulder, his duffel at the other, and the ground shifted behind a veil of sand each time he tried to place his boots.

The aircraft lifted clear as he came to the wall. Its noise traveled east and thinned into the larger machinery of the airfield.

A man in Army desert camouflage waited with a book-sized parcel sealed inside an opaque plastic bag. His uniform carried no rank or chaplain insignia. Neither did Sam’s flight suit.

“Chaplain Artell?”

Sam set down the duffel and shifted the flight helmet into his left hand. “Sam.”

“Ben Sorrel.”

They shook hands. Ben looked from Sam’s boots to the top of his head, then at the departing helicopter. “Did they fly you here folded?”

Sam glanced after the aircraft. “They asked me not to open fully until the rotors stopped.”

Ben’s smile appeared first at one corner of his mouth. At his side, an Iraqi woman held a brown folder closed against the wind with both hands. A patterned scarf covered her hair and the back of her neck. Dust gathered along the cuffs of her blue shirt.

“Salma makes sure we ask before we assume we’re welcome,” Ben said.

She held the folder tighter against the wind. “And I’m trying to keep the papers from blowing toward Iran.”

“Doctor Salma,” Ben added.

“Salma is fine,” she said. “I’m a doctor. I’m interpreting for this trip.”

Two Marine vehicles, sent down that morning by the unit he was going out to, sat beyond a shade canopy with their doors removed. A Marine stood beside the first while three others secured water cans and packs in the second. No rank or insignia showed on their utilities, which matched the dust better than Sam’s flight suit did. The suit was the air wing’s doing: Sam was one of their chaplains, and the squadrons had issued it to him because it was cooler than utilities and nobody minded. He was visible from the moment he stepped out of the helicopter and would remain so until someone found a wall tall enough to put behind him.

Ben nodded at the Marine beside the first vehicle. “The staff sergeant decides when we move.”

The staff sergeant came over carrying a Kevlar helmet and a clipboard. “Padre.” He offered the helmet to Sam. “Flight gear goes in your bag. Bag rides in the trail vehicle. You ride with us.”

Sam exchanged helmets and put the flight one inside the duffel. The staff sergeant was lean and kept his head shaved. His desert utilities were the older pattern, stiff and tinged green from the chemical bath against mosquitoes. He kept them as clean as a man could in that place. He spoke to Sam the way he would have spoken at an inspection, formal and a little put out at having two chaplains to escort when there was work to do, but under that Sam could hear a respect he did not put into words. The staff sergeant watched him tighten the Kevlar strap, then handed him the clipboard.

The top page was a movement sheet. Beneath it, a coverage request listed a forward Army position and a small Marine detachment attached there. The last chaplain visit was months earlier. Two units were still waiting for memorial services for men they had lost. Seven more requests were marked only private. At the bottom, someone printed in block letters:

ANY CHAPLAIN. WHEN AVAILABLE.

Sam read the line twice.

“That’s our last stop,” Ben said. “Army, Marines, anyone who hears we’re coming.”

Sam looked up from the page. “How long do we have?”

Ben indicated the coverage request. “Tonight and tomorrow morning, if the road stays open.”

“We could have to leave earlier,” the staff sergeant warned.

Sam returned the clipboard. “Then we use the time we have.”

With the rotor wash gone, Salma opened the brown folder. “Operations arranged for us to pass through Al-Nakhila on the way to the forward position. The caretaker will receive us if we arrive inside the window.”

Sam looked to Ben.

“The Tomb of Ezra,” Ben said. His hand rested briefly on the parcel. “Civil Affairs staff asked the caretaker to open the site, and he agreed. We can go in this morning.”

Sam nodded toward the parcel. “And I’m here because?”

“You are already going where I’m going. You don’t carry a weapon. You know enough Hebrew to catch me making a mistake.”

“Only if you read slowly.”

“That will do.”

The Marines at the forward position were the reason Sam had come. The tomb was on the way.

The staff sergeant tapped the movement sheet with one finger. “This is an Army movement using our two vehicles. First vehicle carries me, the driver, both chaplains, and Salma. Trail carries the rest and the gear. If I call movement, we move. If the crowd closes around either vehicle, we load. If the route changes before we reach the river, we turn south. We skip the shrine.”

Ben’s expression settled. “Understood.”

One of the Marines lifted Sam’s duffel into the trail vehicle and secured it beneath the cargo net. Sam checked the strap once, then joined Ben and Salma at the first vehicle.

Another Marine passed him body armor from the first vehicle. Sam pulled it over his head and fastened it. Ben tightened his own armor, put on his helmet, and held the parcel in one hand.

Ben glanced over the doorless Humvee. “The Army keeps adding armor and weaponry to these. Marines take off the doors.”

The staff sergeant rested a hand on the empty hinge. “We like to see who’s shooting at us.”

The staff sergeant went forward.

The open rear bench was built with three occupants in mind.

Sam turned his knees toward the open side and braced one boot beneath the seat in front.

The first hour passed beneath engine noise, hot air moving through the open sides, and the short radio exchanges between the two vehicles. Dust came at Sam from every direction and settled over the tan fabric in a color almost identical to it.

The staff sergeant angled his weapon through the passenger opening. In the trail vehicle, the Marines sat facing outward, weapons held toward the open sides.

They left the harder ground around the airfield and followed a road that narrowed as it went. Concrete walls gave way to low buildings, open lots, repair stalls, and stretches of field cut by irrigation channels. Palms began appearing in small groups before the river itself was visible.

Men watched from doorways and the edges of the road. Some stood alone. Others gathered near stopped trucks or in strips of shade. The Marines in front changed posture before Sam could identify why: the driver leaned nearer the wheel, and the staff sergeant scanned the road from side to side. There was no door between Sam’s knees and the road.

At a cluster of men around a disabled pump, the driver eased off the accelerator. Two men carried long-handled tools. A third raised one arm as the vehicles approached.

The staff sergeant spoke into the radio. “Keep interval.”

Salma leaned far enough to see past Ben. “The pump belt is broken.”

No one answered.

“Looks as though he’s asking us for a spare,” she continued.

The man kept his arm raised. Grease darkened both hands. Behind him, a boy stood inside the empty circle of a rubber belt.

“Do we have one?” Sam asked.

“No, Padre,” the staff sergeant said.

The driver’s shoulders lowered by a degree. He did not slow again.

By the second hour Sam could no longer feel his knees, and he had stopped noticing the open side of the bench. More than two hours after they left the staging area, the driver stopped in front of a row of shuttered shops. The staff sergeant listened to a radio call, answered with their vehicle count, and turned halfway in his seat. “Route is open. Access window shifted earlier. We get twenty minutes inside, then we load and continue.”

Ben checked his watch. “We asked for forty. Can you still give us that?”

“No, sir. Twenty minutes,” the staff sergeant repeated.

“Shifted by whom?” Ben asked.

“Not by us, sir.”

Salma closed her folder. “The caretaker has been waiting since morning. To him, we’re already late.”

The vehicles moved again.

Sam looked down at the clipboard resting between the staff sergeant’s boots. Any chaplain. The request did not distinguish Navy from Army or promise time. Seven private conversations would not fit before the next movement window.

Ben followed his gaze. “We will not reach everyone on that list.”

Sam did not look up from the clipboard. “We haven’t arrived.”

“There are two memorial services as well,” Ben went on.

Sam looked out at the road. “Then you take the people who ask for you. I’ll take the people who ask for me.”

Ben tipped his head. “And the people who ask for a chaplain?”

“Whichever of us is free.”

The Tigris appeared beyond the palms as a broad movement of brown light. The air near it remained hot, but it carried plants, mud, and water moving past old brick instead of the road’s dust and exhaust.

From the bridge the town was little more than a collection of earth-colored walls and low roofs, many of them broken at the corners and pocked by the dust storms. The blown dirt lay thick on everything like a patina of neglect. Date palms showed over the walls in clumps, and along the bank the green went thicker down to the water. At the bridge below the town, a white pickup pulled out from the shoulder and fell in behind the trail vehicle. The staff sergeant watched it in his mirror, said something into the radio, and let it stay. It crossed with them.

The vehicles stopped outside a low wall. Blue-green tile showed through dust along the upper edge of the entrance. A man in a white shirt and dark trousers kept to the narrow shade of the doorway.

Across the road, the white pickup had passed them in the last street and been backed into the shade of a wall. Its door was black with soot, and beneath the soot was the logo, the animal split in two. A man in a clean shirt was at the door, talking with two older men, his back to the road.

The staff sergeant stepped down first. Marines from the trail vehicle spread far enough to watch the road and the small gathering across it without closing the entrance. Once they were set, the staff sergeant looked back at Sam. “Dismount. Armor stays on. Leave the helmets here. No weapons go inside. You hear me call, you come out.”

Ben removed his helmet. “Twenty minutes.”

The staff sergeant checked his watch. “Nineteen now.”

Sam removed his helmet and handed it to one of the Marines. Ben passed his over. Their armor remained fastened as they crossed to the entrance. It covered the center of Sam’s flight suit, but his tan sleeves and trousers still seemed almost bright against the brick.

Salma spoke to the caretaker in Arabic. He answered while looking at the wrapped parcel in Ben’s hand, then stepped aside to receive them.

Inside the wall, Sam and Ben removed their boots at a worn threshold. Salma placed hers beside them. The caretaker led them through a small courtyard where sunlight lay hard against brick and tile, then into a cooler room.

The tomb occupied the center beneath cloth and a metal enclosure polished by hands. Hebrew remained visible above an inner opening. Nearby, Arabic script followed the curve of tile. An electric fan turned from one side of the room and clicked once on every rotation.

The caretaker touched the enclosure, then stepped back for Ben.

Ben removed the book from its plastic and opened to a marked page. He read in Hebrew, quietly at first. The language filled the room without becoming large inside it. Sam followed what he could: study, do, teach.

When Ben finished, the caretaker spoke to Salma.

“He asks what Ezra does in the passage.”

Ben rested one finger on the line. “He sets his heart to study the teaching, to do it, and to teach it.”

Salma translated. The caretaker listened, then placed his hand over his chest.

Study. Do. Teach.

The middle word stayed with Sam. A man could hurry toward the part where people listened.

Ben closed his eyes and prayed. Sam lowered his head. The fan clicked. Outside, a truck changed gears on the road, and a bird beat its wings twice somewhere in the courtyard.

Ben finished. The caretaker said something brief.

Salma’s expression softened. “He says your prayer is welcome here.”

Ben returned the book to its plastic. The caretaker touched his chest again and led them back to the light.

❦

Children waited outside the wall.

The Marines maintained a clear path between the entrance and the vehicles. The children kept to its edges until Sam stepped through the doorway in his flight suit, carrying his boots. The smallest looked at Sam’s bright tan sleeves, then crossed the path before anyone moved to stop him.

He wore a red shirt faded nearly pink at one shoulder. He came directly to Sam and placed his fingers against the tan cloth at Sam’s forearm. “You came.”

Sam lowered himself enough to bring the boy’s face nearer his own. “Yes. We came.”

The boy pressed the fabric once, as though confirming it. “You came. Thank you.”

Behind Sam, Ben stopped with one boot in his hand. Salma watched the boy without translating. The English was simple enough. Still, the boy had answered Sam’s we with you.

Sam smiled. “You’re welcome.”

The boy released his sleeve and stepped back into the other children.

Sam sat on the low edge of the wall to pull on his boots. As he tied the second one, laughter broke farther along the wall. Three children looked toward a boy sitting alone in a strip of failing shade.

His face and arms were deeply burned by the sun. Uneven growths changed the line of one cheek and crowded the skin near his mouth. When his lips parted, his teeth turned at angles that did not meet. He brushed a few empty seed shells off his trousers, then rested his hands in his lap and watched the visitors.

One of the other children called a word toward him. Two more repeated it, then moved away when the seated boy looked at them.

Salma’s hand tightened around the brown folder.

Sam stood. “What did they call him?”

She watched the children who spoke. “A devil’s child.”

The seated boy did not move.

“It tells you nothing about him,” Salma said.

Sam took one step toward the child.

The staff sergeant’s radio broke into a burst of speech. He listened, looked toward the road, then saw Sam moving away from the open path. “Padre, stop. Vehicles. Now.”

Sam stopped.

“Route changed,” the staff sergeant said. “We load now.”

The child looked uncomfortable but resolved to his situation. Sam could cross the distance in seconds. Marines sat in the second vehicle, and the unit waited ahead.

Sam turned back.

Ben and Salma went ahead of him. The Marines drew inward from the road without touching the children or raising their voices.

Across the road, the pickup was already moving. The man in the clean shirt was in its passenger seat. It turned toward the river before the first vehicle started.

The boy in red was beside the caretaker. Sam passed close enough to see a repaired seam beneath one sleeve. As he passed, the caretaker, an old man with a full beard and dry hands thick at the knuckles, took his hand and closed it over something hard and flat, the size of his palm, and said a few words.

At the vehicle, a Marine held out Sam’s helmet. He put it on and climbed into his place on the rear bench. The staff sergeant waited for the last Marine to get to the trail vehicle, took his seat beside the driver and tapped the driver’s shoulder. Both vehicles pulled away.

Through the open side, Sam saw the boy in red lift one hand. The other child remained seated beyond him, briefly visible between the wall and a palm trunk.

The road curved. Dust rose behind the trail vehicle and removed them both.

Sam thought he heard the boy again, too far behind them for two words to arrive whole.

You came.

He faced forward. There were people waiting.
