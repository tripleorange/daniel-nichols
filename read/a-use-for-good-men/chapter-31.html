Chapter Thirty-One

What They Can Stop

Aaron moved a chair away from the end of the table when Sam came into the room. For a moment Sam thought he was making space for him. Then Aaron lifted a coil of cable from the floor and laid it on the seat.

“Morning,” he said.

“Morning.”

Sandra was underneath the table connecting the large screen. Only her shoes showed. Janet was at the window, her phone to her ear. Sam put his bag down and let Aaron finish with the cable.

“Could we talk for a minute?”

Aaron kept one hand on the chair. “About Tuesday?”

“Yes. I’m sorry.” Sam had meant to go on. With Aaron in front of him, he could hear how much he wanted the answer to be that it was all right.

“I thought you were still helping me,” Aaron said. “When you came over that morning. I wanted you to see what I’d fixed.”

“I was angry with you for agreeing to finish it.”

“I know you were.”

Sam looked at him. Aaron had known and asked for his help anyway.

“I kept telling myself you were helping Dambrosky,” Sam admitted. “It made it easier to go back to my desk and change your files. I didn’t have to think about you sitting there, trying to work out what had gone wrong.”

Aaron let go of the chair. Neither of them spoke.

“Sandra is running the computer today,” Aaron said. “If you find something you think we should adjust, tell her. She’ll ask me.”

“All right.”

Aaron picked up two pages from beside the keyboard. “These are the test results. The things I haven’t checked yet are on the second page. I want you to read both.”

Sam stood at the table to read them. Aaron had finished testing the connection to ADICUS. He had set aside some service histories he wanted to try, including one in which a man had worked two jobs at once. Sam found his pen and wrote in the margin: what would it do with the same two records if their dates didn’t overlap?

Sandra emerged with dust on one sleeve.

“Sit there,” she told Sam, pointing to the chair next to hers. “Tell me if the print’s too small.”

On the screen, a Civic Passage capability summary appeared alongside an ADICUS window. It was the model’s page on one veteran: what the record showed the man could do. Sam could read the headings from his chair. More people were coming to the room than it had chairs for. A long table took most of the floor, the screen filled the end wall, and the one window faced the parking lot. The chairs were the ones with arms that had caught his cuff the first time he sat in one here, and somebody had brought in four more from other rooms, none of them matching.

Don had driven him from the hotel. At reception, he asked where he could wait, then sat with a book he had brought from home. Sam could see him through the open door now, looking for his glasses in the pocket of his jacket.

Janet ended her call and asked Sam to join her in the empty office next door. The phone stayed in her hand. One side of her jacket collar had turned under, and she had not noticed. The office had a bare desk and a chair pushed in.

“Leila’s at the compound,” she said quietly. “They still haven’t let her in. Jasleen is speaking to Trent.”

“Are Nadia and Amir still there?”

“Yes. Leila spoke to Nadia this morning. They want to come out to her, and the guards are still refusing to let them leave.”

“I’ve been on the phone with Leila since six,” Janet went on. “Every time it rings, I think it’s to say they’ve put them in the car.”

Sam checked his phone. Seven fifty-two. Eleven was three hours away. He followed Janet back to the examination room.

Graham arrived carrying a laptop and a paper cup. He greeted Sam, set down the laptop and asked Sandra where she wanted it. A few minutes later Jasleen came in with Victor. Dambrosky followed, closing an umbrella although no rain had touched Sam on the walk from the car.

The man hung it over the back of a spare chair and said good morning. He took the chair opposite Sam and put his phone down, screen up. That was all he put down.

On the large screen, Evelyn appeared at the head of a row of six directors’ pictures. Two of them sat in offices with the blinds down, one in a hallway with a coat rack behind her, one in a parked car. The last two had their cameras off and were names on gray squares. Janet checked that all six could hear, then introduced the people around the table. Dambrosky raised a hand when she named him as the seventh director and Delmere’s representative. He said he would take no part in Civic Passage’s vote.

Before Victor could open the demonstration, Jasleen asked for a few minutes with Sam, Janet and the directors. Graham went out with the engineers, and Sandra closed the door behind them.

“We need Celia Trent on the phone,” Jasleen said. “I’ve asked her again to cancel the transport and let Nadia and Amir leave with Leila. She says she’s still getting an account from Vale.”

“I’ve spoken to her too,” Evelyn said. “She told me that last night.”

“Then speak to her now,” Sam said.

The words came louder than he intended. Jasleen looked over at him. He put his hands on the table.

“They’re being held against their will. Nadia’s told us that herself. So has Amir, through Leila. How much more does Trent need before she tells her own manager to let them go?”

“She says Vale disputes what Leila told her,” Jasleen answered.

“Then she can dispute it with him after they’re out. He intends to hand them over to the Guard this morning.” Sam turned to the screen. “Evelyn, please stay on the call with her. Ask her to give the order while you’re there. Leila can take them somewhere safe if they’ll open the gate.”

Jasleen took out her phone. “I’ll put her on speaker.”

Dambrosky shifted in his chair. “Celia knows Vale is dealing with the Guard’s request.”

“They’re your employees, Martin,” Sam said. “They reported the Guard, and Vale is delivering them to those men. The last man who went to those men with a question hasn’t come home.”

“She also knows they want to leave,” Evelyn said. “Call her. We can wait.”

Jasleen set the phone on the table near the screen. It rang four times before a woman answered.

“Jasleen, I said I’d call you back.”

“I’m with the Civic Passage directors. You’re on speaker.”

“Celia, it’s Evelyn. You told me last night you would speak to him. Leila is still outside the gate.”

“Colin says the two employees agreed to be interviewed.”

“They agreed to speak with Leila there,” Jasleen corrected. “They’ve asked to leave. His guards won’t let them, and he won’t admit her.”

“And I need him to answer his damn phone,” Trent snapped.

“Then call him now,” Evelyn said. “Tell him to let them leave while you look into what happened.”

“I don’t take instructions from your board, Evelyn.”

“If you won’t give that instruction, I want you to explain to the directors why Delmere is keeping those two people there,” Evelyn insisted. “We’re all waiting.”

Sam looked down at Aaron’s test notes. The top corner was bent beneath his palm. He smoothed it, not reading it again.

“No,” Trent said. “Your board can put their questions in writing, Evelyn. I’ll speak to Colin. That’s all you’re getting with a room listening.”

The line went dead. Jasleen kept her hand near the phone.

Jasleen typed with both thumbs, read it over once, and sent it. “That’s to Celia,” she said. “Everything she hasn’t been reading since last night. Len’s statement. The recruiting request for Ruth with the Guard’s name on it. The email to Ruth that left the name out. I’ve told her our lawyers get all of it at ten, with this board’s account of her hanging up, unless Vale opens the gate.” She sent Leila a message after it.

Sam had hoped to hear that Nadia was with her children. Instead a woman he had never met had hung up on a room. Jasleen put the phone with her papers with the sound turned up.

❦

Janet opened the door and asked Graham to come back. When he sat down, she said James was waiting to join the call. Len and his lawyer would follow.

Dambrosky remained seated.

“I’d like Victor to show you the model working inside ADICUS first,” he said. “James hasn’t worked on the connection since Monday. Victor has finished it. We ought to look at what we’d be sending the recruiting teams today.”

“We’ve arranged for them to speak this morning,” Janet replied.

“Then take their statements and circulate them. Len’s lawyer can include the messages she wants you to consider.” Dambrosky looked at Evelyn’s picture. “We have people waiting to use the model at noon. I don’t see why they should wait while we hear a dispute over what a transport manager told one contractor.”

Janet left the call on hold and looked at Evelyn. Sam had been watching Jasleen’s phone. His bag was against his foot, the folder in it. He straightened in his chair.

“Len is one of the people we referred to Delmere,” Sam said. “He’s here to tell you what happened when he took the job. I want to hear him answer your questions while there’s still something you can do about the next referral.”

Dambrosky turned to him. “Nobody’s proposing to send Len back.”

“You’re proposing to use the same model to find other people. James dropped the work of connecting it when he saw the jobs Mission Support wanted to fill. Now Victor’s here to show us the connection working for all three.” Sam looked back at Evelyn. “Please ask James why he stopped. Before we sit through a demonstration of how well it works.”

Evelyn set down the paper she had been holding.

“Bring James in,” she told Janet. “Then Len. Martin, you and Jasleen will need to go out while they speak.”

“Of course,” Dambrosky agreed. He stood and buttoned his jacket. “I’d like us to have time to hear Victor too.”

“We will,” Evelyn said.

Jasleen left with him. The engineers stayed outside. Janet closed the door and named the six other directors and the three people remaining in the room before James agreed to speak.

His camera showed him at a kitchen table with a closed laptop at his elbow. Sam had worked next to him for weeks without learning whom he went home to.

“James, when you saw the proposal to recruit Ruth Bell, what did you do?” Janet asked.

“I told the recruiter not to contact her, and I told my team to connect the model for the other two recruiting teams and leave Mission Support off it. Nothing Mission Support asked for would reach the model.”

“Is Victor following that instruction?” Evelyn asked.

James glanced at the closed laptop. “I was taken off the project on Monday. I haven’t seen what he’s done.”

“Did you stop because the model was unsuitable for the job?” another director asked.

“No. It was very suitable. I didn’t want my team helping Delmere find people to supply the Guard. I’d read the report Sam sent. I couldn’t ask them to keep working on it as if I hadn’t.”

Sam’s eyes stayed on James while Graham asked about the connecting software. A week ago he would have answered it himself. James explained it on his own.

Nobody asked about the photographs or the assessment. Sam’s hand had found the edge of the folder inside his bag while James spoke. Dambrosky’s assessment of him was in it, and Sarah outside the brick building with her water bottle. Either would have turned the room, and James had said this was not permission to use them. He took his hand out of the bag and let James finish. At the end, James asked Janet to send him the part of the meeting record that summarized his account.

Sam thanked him before the call ended. James said, “You’re welcome,” to the room, and his picture disappeared.

Len joined, and his lawyer from her office. Janet again named the listeners.

Len had put the two Saturday messages on paper. He had them too close to his camera at first, then set them down and read the times.

“Rusk told me it was transport for a government-supported recovery program,” Len said. “On Saturday night, after we’d finished, I asked him who was getting the shipment. That’s when he named the Guard. He put one more thing in the same message that I hadn’t asked him. It had cleared the port as relief, on the program’s paperwork, with nobody opening it. He wrote that so I’d stop worrying about it.”

“You didn’t know you were moving ammunition?” Evelyn asked.

Sam leaned forward. On the screen, Len put one finger on the page and looked up.

“I knew on Saturday morning. The cargo supervisor told me. There were specialists there to handle it. I went ahead with my part of the job.”

One of the directors asked if he had raised questions during the assignment.

“A worker asked if we were allowed to move ammunition through that yard. I told him it was government work and he didn’t need to worry. That’s what Rusk had told me.” Len stopped. “I should have asked Rusk who would get it before I helped send it. I didn’t. That man had reason to worry. If anybody at the far end finds out what’s in a relief truck, it’s the driver who answers for it. I knew that from the Marines.”

“Has Delmere corrected their record of when you were told?” Evelyn asked.

“We’ve received no written correction,” the lawyer said.

Len looked at her, then back at the camera. “I want the record corrected. I didn’t know about the Guard on Friday. I found out after I’d done the job, when they’d asked me to come back for more.”

Janet asked for anything else they wanted the directors to hear.

Len’s lawyer slid a second page across to him. It was a copy of a notebook page.

“One more thing,” Len said. “In 2003 I was a reservist in motor transport when a Wolmeck man asked us to add a stop to a movement. What went out on our fourth vehicle was mortar rounds, in sealed cans. The card said stores, for a town on the river. I knew the cans. I’d been moving them for two years. That night we heard them go out, across the river. Two days later I took the second run through that town, with no stop on it. The force they called Tamarisk had men in the houses by the crossing, and the people who’d lived in them were walking south in the heat. A woman with a baby on her hip and a bundle on her head. An old man with a boy leading him by the hand. Behind the crossing there were roofs down into the rooms. At the tomb, the one our chaplain had been to, the door was standing open, and the old man who looked after it was lying in the road in front of it in a white shirt. Nobody had moved him. We were under orders not to stop on that stretch, and we didn’t. I told our chaplain and asked him to keep it until I could put my name behind it.” He set the page down. “Saturday was the same job. Relief cargo with ammunition in it, a government program for it to ride under, and a man who wouldn’t say where it went. It’s in my statement now, with my name on it.”

“Wolmeck,” Evelyn repeated. “The company Delmere bought?”

“Yes, ma’am.”

“Under the occupation, a force we armed was ours to answer for,” Len’s lawyer said. “Violently uprooting a town so a force can hold it is forcible transfer. The Conventions have had a name for it since 1949. The men that force took out of the town and shot were protected persons. There is a name for that as well.”

“And Saturday?” Evelyn said.

“Forcible transfer again. It doesn’t need an occupation to be a crime. A company that supplies that force knowing what those men do takes part in it. Put ammunition in with relief and every relief truck on that coast loses its protection, and so does everyone riding in one. Mr. Mercer was hired to move relief.”

Sam kept his hands flat on the table. He had never repeated a word of it. He had not needed to now.

He had kept it through the homecoming and through every evening Laura asked him where he went, and Len had set it down in two minutes, in front of a row of pictures, with his name under it.

Len thanked them for listening. Sam did not pick up his pen until the call had ended.

Sandra brought the others back in and drew her keyboard closer. “Are we ready?”

Sam looked at the clock. Eight fifty. He moved Aaron’s pages clear of her arm. “Yes.”

❦

Victor brought the recruiter onto the call. She opened the job description she had emailed Ruth on Wednesday. Priya had joined from Civic Passage. Ruth had given her written permission to show the room her records.

The recruiter opened Ruth’s profile. Below her name were the entries describing how she had managed spare parts for aircraft and helped plan transport for military exercises. Sam read the descriptions while the recruiter scrolled, then asked her to leave them on the screen.

“We need someone who can work from incomplete stock records,” the recruiter said. “The sites keep running short of supplies. We want her to plan the deliveries.”

“Could we stay with this assignment?” Sam asked. “Show us the request for this job.”

Sandra waited while Victor found the request. It occupied less than half the screen. Sam read it, then asked Sandra to enlarge the lines below the heading.

The internal request named the Guard. The email to Ruth did not.

“Who asked you to find someone?” Sam asked the recruiter.

“Mission Support. The request came back to us on Monday.”

“With the same duties?”

“They added the number of sites. Otherwise, yes.”

“Did you see James’s instruction not to approach Ruth?”

“It was in the correspondence. I asked whether it still applied. I was told the project was proceeding with a different engineer.”

Dambrosky leaned forward. “I asked Victor to finish connecting the model for all three teams. We’ve already told you that.”

“Who told her she could contact Ruth?” Jasleen asked.

“The manager was told to begin arranging interviews,” Dambrosky answered. “We need to know who’s available.”

Jasleen looked back at the screen. “Thank you. Stay with us a moment.”

The recruiter nodded. Behind her, a row of chairs was empty.

“Is Ruth qualified?” a director asked.

“For planning this, yes,” Sam said. “I’d want her experience checked with her as we would for any offer. She’s already done related planning for the distributor.”

“She’d need training before she coordinated dangerous loads,” Victor said, pointing to the condition under her name. “We’ve kept that beside the recommendation.”

“Yes,” Sam allowed. “She knows that. She was going to ask the distributor to pay for it.”

Sam asked the recruiter if she had considered Ruth before the Civic Passage profiles arrived.

“No. I was looking for people who’d supplied remote posts.”

Victor opened that earlier search. Ruth was not on it. On the list the model drew from Civic Passage’s six profiles, she was first.

“Sandra, run their request against Ruth’s service history,” Sam said. “Let’s see what they’ll get from the model we’re sending them.”

Sandra selected the records Priya had sent and ran the comparison. The recommendation appeared in the ADICUS window. Sam checked its explanation against the two service records it cited and could find no mistake in the experience it attributed to Ruth.

“What would you expect her to do first?” he asked the recruiter.

“Work out what the posts need and when they’ll run out. Some of their stock records aren’t complete. Then give the manager a delivery schedule he can use with the trucks and drivers he’s got.”

Sam asked Sandra to put the recruiter’s email and the internal request side by side again. In the email the Guard’s outposts became government sites. Ruth could begin from home. The manager would discuss any travel with her later.

Sam turned back to the recruiter. “Did you tell Ruth on the phone that she’d be planning supplies for the Meredan Regional Guard?”

“No. She asked about the client. I said the manager would explain the assignment on the next call.”

“But you knew who it was for. You had this request.”

“Yes.” The recruiter looked away from her screen, then back. “I was arranging for her to speak to him. I wasn’t offering her a contract on that call.”

“She spent an hour on Wednesday night working out if she could give up another shift at the pharmacy. She’d asked you who she’d be working for. Why couldn’t you tell her?”

“Then arrange the call today,” Dambrosky said. “The manager can tell her the supplies are for the Guard and answer her questions before she decides on the job.”

“Will he tell her why families can’t return to the towns those men control?” Jasleen asked.

“He can explain what the job involves. She’s free to turn it down.”

The recruiter had quit looking at Sam. He waited for her to say more, but she seemed to need someone to tell her whether to arrange the call.

“Victor, can you show us which recruiting teams will be able to use the model?” Sam asked.

Victor told Sandra where to open the settings. The list included Mission Support. Sam asked her to leave the list visible alongside Ruth’s recommendation.

“Can you remove that team?” Graham asked. “Leave the other two?”

“Yes,” Victor said.

“Would you show us?” Sam asked.

Sandra looked at Victor. He took her through the change. Mission Support’s name disappeared from the list, and he said their recruiters would no longer be able to submit an assignment.

“Who could put them back on that list after you’ve received the model?” Sam asked Victor.

“Someone with permission to change the settings.”

“At Delmere?”

“Yes.”

“Would they have to ask anyone at Civic Passage?”

Victor glanced at Jasleen. “Not to make the change on the computer. The model would be running on our equipment.”

“And it would recommend people for the Guard’s assignments as it did just now?”

“Yes.”

“The license would still say what they can use it for,” Graham said. “We could write that restriction into it before Alderwick sends anything.”

“Would you agree to keep Mission Support out of the license?” Sam asked Dambrosky.

Graham faced Dambrosky. For several seconds the only sound in the room was the fan in Sandra’s computer.

“No,” Dambrosky said. “All three teams need the model.”

Graham looked at the request on the screen before he turned back to Sam. “Then you want us to cancel the whole delivery. Today. The whole twelve million.”

“Yes,” Sam said.

“If we cancel the delivery, I have to work out how we keep paying the people we hired with those license payments. We may have to repay money we’ve already spent.”

“I wanted those counselors hired too,” Sam said. “I don’t know how to replace the payments. I wish I could give you an answer to that. But Martin’s just told you he intends to use the model for his division. If we send it, he can. You’ve heard what goes out with his relief stores. The people our model finds for him will learn who they moved it for once it’s moved. That was Len on Saturday. It was me once.”

He looked up at the directors on the screen.

“Please don’t send it.”

He took a drink from the glass Sandra had put in front of him. No one spoke while he set it down.

Dambrosky faced Jasleen. “Cancel the call with Ruth if that’s what it takes. We have other candidates. The teams are waiting for delivery.”

Jasleen stayed facing the screen.

“Write to Ruth now,” she told the recruiter. “Cancel the call. Tell her we used her profile for a job she hadn’t agreed to consider, and that we shouldn’t have done it. Copy Priya.”

“All right,” the recruiter said. “Do you want me to arrange the other interviews?”

“No. Cancel those too. My team isn’t going to recruit for the Guard.”

Dambrosky put both hands on the table. “You brought me this model, Jasleen. You asked for these teams to have it.”

“I did.” She picked up her phone, looked at it and put it down again. “And I told you why I wanted Mission Support kept out. You heard James. You heard me. When were you going to tell me you’d sent a recruiter back to Ruth?”

“Victor did the job I gave him. The recruiter was arranging an interview.”

“I know what she was doing. She’s just told us.”

Sam looked at Victor. He was sitting very still beside the screen, his hands clear of his computer.

Evelyn asked Sandra to leave the request visible. She wanted the six directors to speak privately with Graham and Janet before deciding. Dambrosky stood first. He picked up his umbrella, folded it again and kept it at his side.

“I’ll be outside,” he said to Jasleen.

She let him go. While the others disconnected their calls, the recruiter’s message appeared next to Ruth’s original email. Priya said she would call Ruth herself.

Sam asked her to let him know what Ruth wanted from him, then left the room with Jasleen. He could hear Janet asking one of the directors to unmute as Sandra closed the door.

❦

Don looked up when Sam came into reception. His glasses were halfway down his nose. He took them off, his place in the book unmarked.

“Do you want to walk?”

“They’re deciding.”

“We could get as far as the window.”

Sam sat with him instead. He could see the rain now, fine enough that it showed only against the dark cars outside. Don closed the book around one finger.

“I keep thinking of something else I should have said,” Sam told him.

“To whom?”

“Graham. The directors.”

Don waited.

“I could go back in.”

“Did they ask you to?”

“No.”

Across reception, Dambrosky was speaking into his phone with his back to them. A printer behind the desk drew in a sheet and returned it with a scrape.

Jasleen came out of the corridor holding her phone. She looked at Sam, then at a small vacant meeting room.

“Leila’s called.”

He followed her inside. Jasleen shut the door before turning on the speaker.

“Sam’s here,” she said.

“They’re both with me,” Leila said. “We’re at Workers’ House. Nadia’s husband brought the boys. Amir is speaking to his mother.”

Sam gripped the back of a chair. He could hear the words but they got lost on the way out for a moment.

“They left with you?”

“Yes. Trent called Vale, and Vale canceled the company car. They came out to me. We left together.”

Jasleen closed her eyes. She opened them again and asked if Nadia wanted to speak.

“For a minute,” Leila said. “She asked me to call you both.”

There was a rustle near the phone, then Nadia said Sam’s name. A child’s voice sounded behind her. Sam could not understand what the child said, but she answered him before returning to the call.

“I’m here,” she told Sam. “Amir is here too.”

“I’m glad.”

He had meant to say more. He drew the chair out and sat down.

“One of the boys won’t let me put my bag down,” she said. “He thinks I’m going out again.”

“You don’t have to stay on the phone.”

“Leila wanted you to know we arrived. I wanted to tell you.”

“Thank you.”

Nadia asked Jasleen to speak to Leila about their pay. She and Amir had both missed work while being kept at the compound, and she did not want Vale recording it as absence without permission.

“I’ll take that up,” Jasleen said. “Leila can send me what you want corrected.”

The child spoke again. Nadia said goodbye, and Leila returned to arrange a later call with Jasleen. Sam stood when it ended. The clock on the meeting-room phone read 9:56.

He had spent the morning imagining the drive to the Guard, the company car at the gate and the two of them put into it. Now Nadia was in a place called Workers’ House, trying to set down a bag while her son held on to it. Her husband had brought the boys. Amir was speaking to his mother. None of it had needed him in the room. He covered his mouth with his hand and held it there until he could take it away.

Jasleen stayed by the closed door. “I’ll let Evelyn know.”

“Tell her Nadia’s with the boys.”

“I will.”

Sam went back to Don. He told him both people had been released and had somewhere to stay. He gave no names. Don moved his bag off the neighboring chair, and Sam sat close enough for their sleeves to touch.

❦

Janet opened the examination-room door a little after ten. She asked everyone to come back in.

Evelyn looked tired. She had come nearer her camera than she had been all morning, and the page in her hand was folded.

“The directors have voted against authorizing this delivery,” Evelyn said. “The model stays at Alderwick. Graham is sending them the instruction now. We want the model retained for Civic Passage. Delmere’s offer to buy it is a separate question. We take that up on Monday.”

Graham turned his laptop toward Sandra as he sent it. Sandra read it and removed the noon delivery from the schedule on her screen.

Sam watched the entry disappear.

“Nothing goes out at noon,” Sandra said.
