Chapter Twenty-Nine

The Yard

An hour past the bridge, the relief driver followed the lead vehicle through a gate of corrugated steel that two men dragged aside and dragged shut again behind the fourth. The convoy leader was out and walking before the engines settled.

Sam’s harness was still locked across his chest from the stop. He had not tried to free it. The vest had the whole day’s weight in it. The cab smelled of hot canvas and diesel.

The yard sat at the edge of the town with the river beyond its back wall, and was walled on three sides. Over the walls the heads of date palms showed in clumps, and past them the green thickened along the river, which ran brown however the light took it, darker over the deep places. Nothing inside was built to stay: a burn barrel, a platform knocked together from scrap lumber, canvas stretched under dappled camouflage netting, though the enemy had nothing in the air. The fourth side was a row of olive drab shipping containers, the kind hauled up from the coast, with their doors facing in, and a generator ran somewhere beyond the wall behind them. Three trucks stood along the far side with their tailgates down. They were black with soot, not dust, the greasy kind the oil fires had left in the spring on anything that drove through the smoke, and nobody had washed it off. Someone had dragged a sleeve across the nearest door. In the clean stripe the logo showed through, a wolf’s head in two halves with a finger’s width of bare paint between them.

A crane stood inside the gate with its boom lowered to the ground. A hydraulic line hung loose from the cab, and somebody had chalked a date on the counterweight.

A man in a civilian shirt waited beside the first truck. He had a clipboard he did not look at and a watch he did. The convoy leader stopped next to him with a handheld radio at his ear and began the report, and the man kept his back half turned to the vehicles while he listened. Neither of them looked at the second one.

Sam could hear the shape of the report without the words. Civilian strike. No contact. The radio stayed at the convoy leader’s ear.

Behind Sam, the driver had not said a word since the corporal moved him. Sam did not turn around.

Men came out from between the containers. They were contractors, in tan from boots to ball caps, plate carriers over their shirts, their kit newer than anything the Marines carried. Two carried weapons slung across their backs. They went to the trucks without hurrying and began handing crates down. The crates were wooden, stenciled in Arabic and English, and the one that passed nearest Sam said PUMP above a part number. A box of filters followed it. Coils of black pipe. The men stacked them at the gate, where the crane’s boom lay, and went back for more.

Then the cans came past from behind. In the mirror Sam had watched two of the men pull the canvas back from the fourth vehicle; now they carried its load past his door, olive steel cans the length of a man’s forearm, a handle at each end, two men to a can. Those did not go to the gate. The men carried them between the containers and came back with their hands empty.

Len had climbed down from the third vehicle with the movement card against his chest. He stood by its fender where he could see the fourth vehicle and the containers. He was not writing. In the mirror, Sam watched him watch the cans go between the containers, and watched him look once at the man in the civilian shirt, and then down at the card as though it had a line for this.

“Two, stand by,” the radio said.

The Marine with the handset answered.

The man in the civilian shirt said something to the convoy leader and checked his watch again. The convoy leader nodded without lowering the radio. Beyond the wall the generator changed note and recovered.

Sam looked at the stripe on the truck door. He had seen the mark before, under tan paint on a container in their own supply yard, and on a door across a road with a man in a clean shirt standing beside it, and the thought went no further because the driver behind him had begun to breathe through his mouth. Sam listened to it. He kept his eyes on the door.

The last can went between the containers. The men at the gate took hold of the steel sheets.

The convoy leader lowered the radio at last and came back along the line on the far side, past the second vehicle without stopping. Behind Sam, he struck the third vehicle’s hood twice with a flat hand.

“Two, move,” the radio said.

The relief driver put them in gear. The man in the civilian shirt had not moved from the first truck. As the second vehicle passed him he looked in, past the relief driver, at the Marine in the back, and then at Sam. The face was shaved and rested, the first rested face Sam had seen since the bridge. Sam thought he would speak. He did not. He looked at his watch.

The crates stayed stacked by the gate with the pump uppermost. The crane’s boom lay beside them on the ground. Then the steel sheets came together behind the fourth vehicle, and the road took all of it.
