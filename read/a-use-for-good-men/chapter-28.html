Chapter Twenty-Eight

Other People’s Terms

Ellen left a plate at Sam’s elbow while he was reading James’s number. He looked up, surprised to find himself at the kitchen table. Midday sun came across the table from the back window. The only sounds were the washing machine by the back door and Ellen’s steps between it and the hall.

“Chicken salad,” she said. “The bread’s in the box if you want another.”

“Thank you.”

“You thanked me for lunch when you got up. Now eat it.”

He put down the phone and took a bite. It was nearly one. He had slept for more than two hours.

James had not asked him to call. Sam opened a message instead.

I owe you an apology. I’d like to speak when you’re willing.

He sent it before adding anything about Friday.

Ellen carried a basket of washing through the kitchen. Sam offered to help. She put a pair of pillowcases on the chair next to him and said he could start by finding out whether they matched.

He was holding them against each other when James’s reply came.

Ten minutes. Now is all right.

Sam took the phone to the guest room. He closed the door before making the call.

“I’m sorry I told Dambrosky you’d helped me,” Sam said when James answered.

“Was it just the assessment?”

“No. I told him about the question he’d put to you about my family.”

James said nothing. Sam looked at the charger trailing from the wall to the bed.

“I thought if he knew someone would tell the lawyer what he’d done—”

“I was going to speak to the lawyer,” James cut in. “Why did you have to tell Dambrosky before I’d even had the chance?”

When he spoke again, his voice was lower.

“I can’t get another job as quickly as he can take me off this one. I have to go back there.”

“Has he said anything else about your job?”

“No. I don’t know what he’ll do.”

Sam sat on the edge of the bed. He had been certain on Monday about what would make the man back off.

“Graham told me he’d fired you,” James said.

“Yes.”

“What are you going to do?”

“I don’t know about work.” Sam paused to take a slow breath. “I’m going to Alderwick Friday. The directors will be there.”

“To approve the delivery?”

“To decide. You stopped your team’s work for Mission Support. I’d like the directors to hear why.”

“You want me to speak to them.”

“Yes.”

“I can tell them what I did and what Dambrosky said in front of us. I can answer for how the model was connected up to Monday, when they took me off it. Victor has had it since, and I can’t tell them what he’s changed.”

“Then I’ll ask Janet to call you,” Sam said.

“She can ask me. I want to know who’s listening. This isn’t permission to give them the assessment or the photographs.”

“I understand,” Sam agreed. “They don’t leave my bag.”

After the call he remained on the bed.

Sam opened Jasleen’s number and called.

“I’d like to come to Alderwick,” he said. “If the invitation is still there.”

“It is. Janet was going to try you again.”

“I’ll call her. I can work with Sandra operating the computer.”

Jasleen paused. “Before we arrange it, I need to tell you what’s happened to Nadia and Amir. Nadia has asked to speak to you. Leila is with us on the call.”

Sam stood, then sat again. “Put me through.”

Jasleen introduced him. Leila Darzi said she was at Meredan Workers’ House. Nadia said she was speaking from the company compound.

“They’ve given us rooms here,” Nadia said. “Vale told us we must stay until the Meredan Regional Guard has spoken to us.”

“Have you tried to leave?” Sam asked.

“I went to the gate. I said my husband was waiting. The guard called the office. Then he told me Mr. Vale had not given permission for me to go.”

“Where is Amir?”

“In the room next to this one. He went to the gate this morning. They sent him back too.”

Sam could hear something scraping near her phone. Nadia said she was moving the chair away from the window.

“I went there again today,” Leila said. “They wouldn’t let me enter. I asked to see both workers. I spoke to Vale by telephone while I was standing outside. He told me they had agreed to an interview.”

“I agreed to answer questions with Leila,” Nadia insisted. “I didn’t agree to stay here. I didn’t agree to go with the Guard.”

“Neither did Amir,” Leila added. “I’ve spoken to him myself.”

Sam gripped the phone tighter. The family photograph on the dresser showed Don holding a small boy on his shoulders. One of the boy’s shoes rested against Don’s cheek.

“When does the Guard expect you to arrive?”

“Friday at six in the evening,” Nadia said. “Mr. Vale says company transport will take us. The Guard has given him the time.”

Sam looked at the date on his phone. “This Friday?”

“Yes,” Nadia said.

“That’s eleven in the morning here,” Jasleen put in.

An hour before noon. He saw the two things side by side: Alderwick sending Delmere the model at noon if the directors said yes, and the company car going in at the Guard’s gate with Nadia and Amir in it. He could not make either one wait for the other. The company would hand both of them to the Guard they had been supplying, because the two of them had asked questions. The last man who had gone to that Guard with a question was still inside.

“What has Celia Trent said?” he asked Jasleen.

“That she would speak to him again. She hasn’t told Vale to let them go,” Jasleen said. “I’ve left her two messages since.”

“When did you leave the first one?”

“This morning.”

“And she’s had all morning.”

Sam put his free hand against his forehead.

“I’ve also written to Delmere’s lawyer,” Leila said. “Nadia and Amir asked me to. I want the company to explain why Vale’s guards are stopping them at his gate.”

“Can somebody come and get them?” Sam asked.

“I can collect them if the company lets them out,” Leila answered. “I have rooms for them and their families.”

Sam lowered his hand from his forehead.

“Nadia, where are your husband and the boys?”

“At home. I spoke to them before you came on. My husband wanted to come back here. I asked him to stay with the children.”

Her voice changed on the last word. Sam waited.

“One of the boys keeps asking when I will be home. I told him I didn’t know.”

He looked at the closed door. He could hear footsteps and the wicker basket brushing the hall wall. Then the steps moved away.

“I’ll call Janet now and ask the directors to call Trent themselves,” Sam said. “They know why you went to Leila. May I tell them what’s happened since?”

“Yes,” Nadia said. “Tell them I want to leave. Tell them I’m asking them while being held here.”

“Amir has asked me to tell them the same thing,” Leila said. “They may call me directly.”

“I’ll ask Janet to call you,” he said. “You can tell her what you’ve already tried.”

“Please make sure they ask Trent to order Vale to let us leave,” Nadia pressed. “He has already told Leila we agreed to an interview. If they just ask him for an explanation, he’ll tell them the same thing.”

“I’ll tell them that.”

Jasleen said she would remain with Nadia and Leila. Sam left the call and opened Janet’s number before looking at the other messages on his screen.

She answered from a room where he could hear several people talking. He asked to speak with her alone. A few seconds later she told him to go ahead.

He told Janet the time, eleven on Friday morning, an hour before the model was due to leave Alderwick. He gave her Leila’s number and repeated what Nadia had asked.

“I’ll speak to Leila, then ask Evelyn to call Trent,” Janet said. “I’m going to find her now. Are you coming to Alderwick?”

“Yes. I’ll come on Friday. James said you could call him about it. He wants to talk to you before he agrees to speak.”

“I’ll call him,” Janet said. “Stay where I can reach you.”

Sam lowered the phone after she hung up. He could no longer open the shared folder where he had replaced Aaron’s program. The old impulse had not left him, the certainty that if he could get in and make one more change, someone would have to stop. He set the phone aside and let Janet make the calls she said she would make.

Ellen knocked at the door.

“I made tea,” she said. “Would that help?”

“Yes.”

He opened the door. She held out a mug until he took it.

“Do you need the room for a while?”

“One more thing, if that’s all right.”

“I’ll leave you to it.”

Sam thanked her. He left the mug on the dresser and sent Len a message asking to talk. Len wrote back asking where he was. When Sam told him, Len wrote that it was two hours, and that he would rather say what he had to say to Sam’s face.

Len’s pickup came up the drive a little before four. Sam went out to meet him. Len got down slowly, one hand on the door, and looked at the house and then at Sam. He had thickened through the chest since Iraq, in a barn coat and a cap he took off as he came around the hood. The low sun showed the gray in what hair the cap had covered. He looked Sam over as he had once looked over a line of vehicles before it moved.

“Your friend’s?”

“Don’s. He’s at the school.”

“Denise said if I was driving two hours I could at least bring something.” He reached back into the cab and brought out a pie in a foil tin. “She didn’t make it. She wants that known.”

Ellen took the pie at the door as if pies arrived there every afternoon, and put a mug in front of each of them at the kitchen table. Len sat where Sam had sat at lunch. He kept his jacket on.

“I spoke to my lawyer this morning,” he said. “Dambrosky still hasn’t put it in writing that I didn’t know who the shipment was for when I took the job. He told you he would.”

“I’m going to the examination at Alderwick Friday,” Sam said. “The directors need to hear what happened to you before they decide if Delmere gets the model. Would you like Janet to arrange a time?”

“I want them to hear me, with my lawyer there. I’ll tell them Rusk called it government transport when he hired me. He didn’t tell me it was for the Guard until Saturday night, by message, with the trucks already on the road. I’ll tell them what I did that Saturday, including what I told the worker who asked about the ammunition. I told him what Rusk had told me, and he went back to work.”

Sam looked down at the tea.

“You can tell it the way it happened,” he said.

“I intend to. Don’t make it sound as if I was never responsible for anything.”

“I won’t.”

“I’m going to tell my lawyer what I told you in Iraq,” Len went on. “I’ll tell her myself. Saturday was the same job, Sam. I’m not keeping it to a chaplain this time.”

“I’ve kept it nineteen years,” Sam said. “I’d have kept it the rest of the way.”

“I know you would. That’s why it’s me telling her and not you.”

Sam nodded.

Len turned the mug a quarter turn on the table. “I don’t know why Dambrosky wanted me.”

“He wrote that you were reliable when arrangements changed,” Sam answered. “I don’t know what he expected once you found out about the Guard.”

“I keep going over what I said to Rusk.”

“Do you want to go through it with me?”

“Not now. I did it the whole way up.”

He asked Sam to have Janet call the lawyer first to arrange a time, and Sam wrote it down. At the door Len shook Ellen’s hand and thanked her for the tea he had not drunk.

Don’s car turned in as the pickup was backing out. The two vehicles stopped in the drive for a moment, window to window, and then the pickup went on down the road.

“Who was that?” Don asked in the kitchen.

“Len Mercer. The staff sergeant from Iraq. Delmere hired him through us.”

Don looked down the road, though the truck was gone. “He drove up here?”

“Two hours.”

Don set his folder on the counter and stood there a moment with his phone in his hand. His collar was open and he let the counter take some of his weight.

“How is the boy?” Ellen asked.

“Four stitches. His mother took him home.” Don put the phone in his pocket. “She wants the other one suspended for the term. The superintendent wants to know why I sat with him instead of calling his mother first.”

Ellen asked him to take the rice off the stove. He listened with the oven mitt over his hand while Sam told him he had agreed to return to Alderwick.

“Friday morning,” Sam added. “I need to travel tomorrow.”

“You’d get back late,” Don said.

“Yes.”

Don set down the mitt. “Are you asking me something?”

Sam looked at Ellen. She was putting plates on the table.

“Would you come with me?” he asked Don. “I still want to sit across from Dambrosky and make him listen. That’s how I talked myself into telling him about James. I want to be able to talk to you before I go into that meeting.”

Don was quiet. Then he looked at Ellen.

“You’d need to tell the school tonight,” she said. “Your sweater’s still in the car.”

Don smiled and gave no answer. He took his phone into the front room and asked for Thursday and Friday. Through the doorway Sam could see him at the front window, his back to the room and one hand in his pocket.

Ellen put another plate in front of Sam.

“Sit down before he remembers anything else he needs to arrange.”

When Don returned, he said the superintendent had agreed, after asking if this was the week for it.

“We can take your car,” he told Sam. “We’ll leave early and stop when we need to. I want you to get a decent night’s sleep before Friday.”

“Thank you.”

Don pulled out his chair. The window had gone dark while they talked, and Ellen had switched on the lamp above the table. “Tell Laura when we’re leaving.”

Sam wrote that he had accepted the examination, that Don was coming with him, and that they planned to leave the next morning. Then he added that he would answer if she wanted to call after dinner.

He slid the phone back into his pocket. Ellen passed him the rice. He took the bowl in both hands before she let go.
