Chapter Thirty-Two

What They Bought

Dambrosky was the first to speak. “Thank you,” he told Evelyn. “Please let me know when you’d like to discuss the purchase.”

Then he turned to Sam. “Thank you for coming back.”

Sam had expected another argument. He gave a small nod. Dambrosky picked up his umbrella and left.

Evelyn asked Jasleen to thank Leila. For a few minutes the directors discussed who would speak to Delmere about the money. Sam kept his seat. His part in the examination was over, and there was no desk he could go back to while other people finished the meeting.

When the screen went dark, Aaron began collecting his pages. Sam held out the two Aaron had given him.

“You can keep those,” Aaron said.

“I’d rather you had them. I made one note on the second page.”

Aaron turned it over. Sam had written a question against a case involving duties performed at two overlapping postings. Aaron read it, took up his pen and added something below it.

“I’ll check that Monday,” he said.

Sam would have gone in on Monday if Aaron had asked. Aaron put the pages in his bag and thanked Sandra for the room.

Near the door, Graham stopped Sam with a hand on his arm, then let go.

“You helped them make the decision,” he said.

“Will they turn down the purchase?”

“The offer is still there,” Graham answered. “I have to tell them what refusing this delivery will cost us.”

Sam’s phone sounded. Priya had spoken to Ruth. Ruth wanted a copy of the request that named the Guard, and she was keeping the pharmacy shifts for now.

Sam wrote back that he would answer her questions if she wanted to call. He put the phone away. Graham was standing in front of him.

“I’m glad they refused today,” Sam said.

“So am I.”

Sam could not tell what else Graham wanted to say, and went to find Don.

At reception, Don was putting on his jacket. Sam stopped at his side and called Laura.

“We’ve finished,” he said when she answered. “They aren’t sending it today.”

“How are you?”

He looked at Don, the book under his arm, then at the room where Sandra was turning off the screen.

“Tired,” he said. “Relieved. I don’t think I’ve caught up with it yet.”

“Have you had lunch?”

“It’s not eleven.”

“That hasn’t always stopped you.”

He smiled. Through the glass doors he could see rain collecting along the edge of the entrance mat.

“Can we talk about tonight?”

“Yes,” Laura said. “Let me put these things down.”

Laura’s voice came closer as she returned to the phone.

“I’m here,” she said.

Don pointed toward the doors. Sam nodded, and his friend went outside to wait under the entrance roof.

“I’d like to come home,” Sam said. “Today. I don’t want you waiting until six o’clock to find out whether I mean it.”

“Good. What about Don?”

“He’s booked at the hotel tonight. Ellen’s found a flight for Sunday.”

“Then bring him with you. He can stay in Michael’s room.”

“I’ll ask him.”

“Are you coming for dinner?” Laura asked. “Or coming home?”

He had kept the hotel room as though he might need somewhere to go when this conversation ended.

“Home. I’ll get my things from the hotel.”

“All right. I have to go back to the nursery this afternoon. I’ll be home by five. There’s food if you need something before then.”

Sam thanked her. After the call, he stood at the window long enough for Don to turn and look for him.

Sam drove. Don had offered. Sam had said no, and Don did not ask twice. They collected the bags from the hotel, and after that there was nothing to do with the car but point it north. The rain came with them as far as the river and quit. The wipers went on scraping a dry windshield until Don leaned over and turned them off. Don asked nothing about what had been decided, or by whom. He named the exits as they passed, and somewhere in Maryland Sam noticed that his hands had come down from the top of the wheel to the bottom, where they rested on long drives when nothing was wrong.

❦

At home, Sam carried his bag upstairs and put it on the bed. Laura had changed the sheets. His book lay on the bedside table where he had left it, with a grocery receipt marking a place he could not remember reading.

He unpacked before going downstairs.

Don was in the kitchen with the refrigerator open.

“There should be something for lunch,” Sam told him.

Don lifted a covered bowl. “Is this for tonight?”

“I don’t know.”

“Better leave it, then.”

They found bread and cheese. Don looked for the plates in the cupboard where they had been in the old kitchen, and Sam reached past him to the next one.

After they ate, Don took his book into the living room. Sam had nowhere he needed to be. He went upstairs anyway, and came down again when he heard Laura’s car.

She brought in a tray of small pots, soil stuck to the damp cardboard. Sam held the door while she passed him.

“The rest can stay in the car,” she said. “These were rolling around.”

She set the tray on the counter and turned. For a moment he could not decide whether to reach for her. She came to him, put her arms around his waist and pressed her face against his shirt.

He held her. Her coat smelled of cold air and the inside of the car. He had imagined this part of returning often enough on the drive to make it difficult to believe he was standing in the kitchen doing it.

“Hello,” she said against him.

“Hello.”

She stepped back and wiped beneath one eye with her thumb.

“Where’s Don?”

“Reading.”

“He never reads when he comes to see us.”

“I think he’s been awake for most of the last two days on my account.”

Laura went to find him. Sam took her coat from the back of the chair and hung it with his own.

At dinner, Don told Laura how Sam had spent several minutes explaining which exit to take on the drive north, then fallen asleep before they got to it.

“There were signs,” Sam protested.

“There were excellent signs,” Don agreed. “I’m still wondering why I needed the directions as well.”

Laura laughed. She had made too much rice, and Don accepted more before Sam could warn him that she would keep offering until the pan was empty. They spoke about a tree leaning over the Rowans’ driveway, which Don meant to take down himself and Ellen meant to have looked at.

“She has a point,” Laura put in.

“I was an engineer.”

“Then you know why she’s asking.”

Sam laughed with them. A moment later he realized he was holding his fork above the plate, braced for the laughter to become something he would regret. Laura asked Don how close the tree was to the house. Sam lowered his fork and took another bite.

Later, while Don spoke to Ellen upstairs, Laura joined Sam at the sink and dried a pan.

“Will you be able to sleep here?”

“I hope so.”

“If you can’t sleep, wake me. I don’t want to wake up in the morning and find you’ve left the house without telling me.”

He shut off the water.

“I’m not planning to leave.”

“I know. I want you to tell me if that changes.”

He turned toward her.

“I will.”

She hung the towel on the oven handle and stayed there until he finished.

❦

On Sunday, Sam drove Don to the airport. Laura came with them and carried the paper bag of sandwiches she had made, despite Don’s insistence that food was available where he was going.

At the curb, Don pulled his bag out of the back seat. He hugged Laura, then Sam.

“Call me when you’ve picked up your things,” Don told Sam. Aisha had arranged for him to go to Civic Passage on Tuesday morning.

“I will.”

Don took the sandwiches. Sam watched him walk to the doors, then opened Laura’s door before going around to his own.

That evening, Ellen sent a photograph of Don at their kitchen table with half a sandwich in his hand. Sam showed it to Laura. She enlarged it to inspect the paper bag.

“He did save her one,” she said.

Sam set the phone on the table between them. He was glad Don was home. He wished Don were in the chair across from him.

❦

Janet called on Monday afternoon while Sam was in Sarah’s room. The office equipment had been collected while he was away, and the desk looked too large for the old family laptop he had dug out of a cupboard. He had a job listing open and had read its first three requirements several times. He had the experience they wanted. He had not yet clicked the application button.

He answered the phone standing up.

“The directors voted,” Janet said. “They’ve authorized the purchase.”

Sam looked at the screen. The application button was blue. Below it, the company offered flexible hours and paid time for professional training.

“Today?”

“This morning. Graham wanted to speak to you himself. I told him you shouldn’t find out when you come to collect your things tomorrow.”

“They’re selling it to Delmere after what we showed them?”

“Yes.”

He sat down again.

“What did they say about Mission Support?”

Janet did not answer immediately. He could hear a door close at her end.

“Evelyn said we were right to stop Friday’s delivery. She doesn’t think that means we have to turn down the purchase.”

“She watched them find Ruth for that job. She heard Trent hang up rather than answer her. She knows what he’ll do with it.”

“I know, Sam. I asked her how selling it would change what Martin wants to use it for. She said she’d rather tell you herself.”

Sam pressed his thumb against the edge of the desk. He had wanted her to say the vote had come too quickly, that somebody had missed the account from Len or had not understood Ruth’s assignment.

He looked up at the photographs and notes above Sarah’s desk for a page he had forgotten to show Evelyn, something that could make her call the other directors back.

“Has Graham signed anything yet?”

“Not yet. They’re expecting to sign tomorrow.”

“Then there’s still time.”

“They’ve made the decision. Graham has their permission.”

“Janet, please.” He stopped. He was making her stay on the phone while he searched for a way to make what she had told him untrue. “I’m sorry. I know you told them.”

“I’m sorry, Sam.”

“Will you be there when I come to pick up my things tomorrow?”

“Yes.”

“I’d like to see you.”

“I’ll come down when you arrive.”

He wrote to Evelyn while it was in him.

He asked her to call the directors back before Graham signed. Delmere would be able to use the model to find people for the Guard. He asked her the question he could not leave out. She had refused that on Friday. How could she make it possible on Monday?

He sent it before he could soften it, and went downstairs to tell Laura what Janet had said.

Laura was sorting the small pots by the back door. They were for the client’s bed against the wall, two kinds of lavender and a gray-leaved thing he did not know the name of. The labels for them were rubber-banded on the tray. She straightened and listened, one hand on the tray.

“What happens now?”

“Graham signs the sale agreement. Then he tells Alderwick to send Delmere the model.”

“Can they do that without asking you?”

“It’s theirs to sell. I made it while I was working for them.”

“You made it because people were coming home and couldn’t get anyone to see what they knew how to do.” Laura pulled out the chair beside her. “I remember you trying to explain it to me, before you had anything to show Graham. You wanted to help those people find work they could be proud of.”

You made it for them.

He looked down at the chair. Dambrosky would have the model tomorrow. All the months Sam had spent trying to make it better, all the times he had gone back upstairs after dinner because he thought he knew how to fix something, would be useful to him.

“Sam.” Laura waited until he looked at her. “You asked them not to sell it. They heard you. They’re choosing to do it anyway.”

“He still gets it.”

“I know. I was in the kitchen when she told you she hadn’t decided.” She kept her hand on the chair. “Come and sit with me.”

She moved the tray out of his way. He sat down and stayed while she finished attaching labels to the pots. After a while she asked him to hold a length of tape. He tore it where she showed him.

❦

Evelyn’s answer was on his phone when he woke on Tuesday. He read it in the kitchen before Laura came down.

Sam,

Thank you for writing to me. I would rather have it from you than hear it secondhand, and you are owed a real answer.

I voted to sell because I could not see who refusing would protect. Martin has the six profiles. He has ADICUS and a budget we will never have. He has told Graham and me that if we refuse him he will have a model of his own by spring, built by someone who never sat across a table from Ruth Bell. I believe him. Refusing him buys those families in Meredan nothing, and it does not keep the people you spoke for out of his trucks. It costs us thirty million dollars and the people that money would see.

On Friday I stopped a delivery because a recruiter had a woman on the phone under a false description, and we were the ones who had handed Delmere the tool. That was ours to stop. What Delmere does with a thing they own is theirs to answer for, and I mean to make them answer. The complaint about your family is with their lawyers, and I will see it through. The minutes will say why you asked us not to sell.

I know what this costs you. I am not going to pretend the arithmetic makes it even. I have looked at the waiting list for years, and when it came to it I could not vote to keep it.

Evelyn

He read it twice and could not find the sentence to answer. He turned the phone face down by the kettle.

❦

Aisha met him at reception with a cardboard box on a small wheeled cart. His mug sat between two books. Someone had wrapped the mug in paper, leaving its handle exposed.

“I checked the drawers myself,” she said. “There’s a list inside. If anything’s missing, call me.”

He looked past her into the corridor, then back at the box. It was lit the whole length and empty. The doors along it were shut. A phone rang behind one of them, three times, and was answered.

“Thank you.”

“Janet’s coming down. Graham asked if you’d have a few minutes for him before you leave.”

“Has he signed?”

Aisha paused. “He’ll need to answer that.”

Sam took hold of the cart. One wheel turned sideways and dragged until he moved it back. Aisha waited because he had not decided where to go.

Janet came down the corridor.

“Leave it here for now,” she said. “I’ll watch it.”

“Would you like me to come in with you?” Aisha asked Sam.

He did not want her to hear him ask Graham for his job.

“Thank you. I’d rather talk to him alone.”

Graham stood when Sam came in. There were two cups on the low table by the window. Sam recognized the second one as a visitor’s cup, white with the blue Civic Passage name printed near its rim.

“Aisha said you’d want coffee,” Graham said.

“Have you signed?”

Graham stopped beside the table.

“Both organizations have. The money arrived this morning. Thirty million dollars.”

Sam kept standing.

“And the model?”

“I’ve told Alderwick to send it. Sandra will let me know when Delmere’s engineers have it working.”

The window looked onto the strip of trees between the parking lot and the road. Sam had stood there with Graham when they were deciding whether they could afford another counselor. Graham had worked the numbers on a pad while Sam looked for another expense they could put off.

He sat in the chair nearest the door.

“Evelyn answered me this morning,” he said. “She thanked me for writing to her.”

Graham sat down across from him.

“She sent your message to the other directors.”

“Did any of them ask you to wait?”

“No. They told me to go ahead.”

Sam looked at the coffee. Graham had remembered that he took it black. He left it where it was.

“Delmere won’t ask us to repay the license fees they’ve already paid,” Graham went on. “That’s in the agreement. We keep that money, and the purchase price takes the place of the payments we were waiting for. We can pay our people. We can keep using what you built. I won’t have to tell somebody we’ve just hired that there isn’t enough money to keep them.”

“Do you think that’s what I wanted?”

“No.”

“Then stop talking as if I haven’t thought about them.”

Sam could hear himself breathing. He had come into the office determined not to raise his voice.

“On Friday I told the directors what cancelling could cost us,” Graham said. “They cancelled anyway, and I was glad. You were there.”

“Yes.”

“I’m glad we can stay open too. I’m going to have to tell the staff what the board has done. I would like to be able to tell them you understand why.”

Sam stared at him.

“You want me in the announcement?”

“I want you to let them thank you. There are people here who wouldn’t have a job without what you made. There are people we’re helping who would still be waiting for an appointment.”

“Then thank them for doing their jobs. Don’t tell them I agreed to this.”

“I wouldn’t say you agreed.”

“What would you say?”

Graham looked out the window.

“That you made it possible for us to help more veterans. That we intend to honor the work you did here.”

For a moment Sam wanted to accept it. He could imagine his name in Graham’s message, people remembering that he had done something besides damage Aaron’s files and leave under Aisha’s supervision.

“Would you tell them why I won’t be there?”

“I wouldn’t put your dismissal in a staff announcement.”

“Or what I asked the directors not to do.”

“Sam, they know you’ve objected.”

“Some of them know. The people you hire next month won’t. They’ll hear that I built this wonderful thing and that selling it paid for their jobs.” He put his hand on the arm of the chair. “You can tell them you’re going to hire more counselors, that people won’t have to wait so long for an appointment. But don’t put my name in that announcement and make it sound as though I wanted you to sell the model to Delmere.”

Graham was quiet. Then he said, “All right. I won’t use your name that way.”

Sam loosened his grip on the chair. His hand had begun to ache.

“Will you ask Sandra to copy me on Delmere’s confirmation when it’s running?”

“I can do that.”

“Please do it now.”

Graham typed the request and turned the screen his way.

“Thank you,” Sam said.

He stood. Graham stood with him and lifted the untouched cup from the table.

“I wish we could have found a way to keep you here.”

Sam had waited a week to hear those words. Graham was holding the cup, waiting for him to go. Sam thanked him for the coffee and went out to collect the box.

❦

Janet carried the books to his car while he carried the box. Outside, he saw that one corner of it had been repaired with several layers of clear tape. They put everything in the trunk.

“Priya asked if she could call you,” Janet said.

“About Ruth?”

“She didn’t say.”

“She can call.”

Janet closed the trunk. Sam waited for her to say something about the sale.

“I thought about a reporter,” she said. “The one who broke the Meredan story. I had her number on my desk for two days.”

“And?”

“Nadia and Amir are still in that country. Len’s name is on his statement.” She put her keys in her coat pocket. “I decided not to. I wanted you to know it was decided, not forgotten.”

He nodded.

“Are you eating lunch at home?”

“I expect so.”

“I could walk over to the place across the road with you. I’ve got half an hour.”

He looked across at the restaurant, where they had taken Aaron the day he joined.

“I don’t think I can today.”

“Another day, then.”

He wanted to promise. Janet seemed to see him trying, because she touched his sleeve and said, “You can let me know.”

“I’m sorry about that first meeting,” he said.

Janet took her hand from his sleeve.

“You were trying to stop me, and I kept going,” he went on. “I’m sorry I frightened you.”

“I didn’t want to be in that room,” she said.

He opened the door. She had said it looking at the restaurant across the road and not at him. “I’ll let you get back.”

At home, Laura helped him lift the box onto the kitchen table. He told her about the meeting while she unwrapped the mug. She set it next to the sink and folded the paper along its existing creases.

“Do you want to eat?”

“In a minute.”

He went upstairs with the books. His phone sounded before the landing.

Sandra had copied him on a message to Graham and Delmere’s technical manager. Alderwick had sent the model. Below her message, Delmere’s manager confirmed it was installed and tested, and that the recruiting teams could use it that afternoon.

Sam read the manager’s message, then Sandra’s again. He found her number and called.

“Does that include Mission Support?” he asked when she answered.

“Yes.”

“You didn’t send Ruth’s records with it?”

“No applicant records. They checked it with their own test material.”

“Did anyone ask if one team was supposed to be left off?”

“Not me. I send what I’m told to send, Sam.”

He stood on the landing, the books pressed against his chest. Behind him, the bedroom window had begun to rattle in the wind.

“Does it still need anything from Alderwick to keep running?”

“No. It runs on their computers now.”

After he ended the call, he tried to put the books on Sarah’s desk. The bottom one caught against the edge, and he had to set the whole stack on the floor. His fingers would not quite open when he wanted them to. He kept his hand against his leg until Laura called from downstairs.

“Sam? I’ve made you something.”

He meant to say he was coming. He took a breath and found he needed another before he could speak.

“I’ll be down.”

He left the phone with the books and went to her.
