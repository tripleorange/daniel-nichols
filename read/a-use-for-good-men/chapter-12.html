A Use for Good MenChapter Twelve

Chapter Twelve

The Girl in Red

The driver wiped the second vehicle’s mirrors with a rag already streaked black.

“You planning to make them clean,” Sam asked, “or just distribute the dirt?”

The Marine leaned away from the driver-side mirror and inspected the gray circle he had made. “That’s the clean side of the rag, sir.”

The sun had been up an hour, and the strip already had its glare. Four vehicles sat in the support-area lane, engines off and hoods hot from the morning checks, the fourth with a load lashed under canvas. A corporal worked down the line pressing fuel caps with the heel of his hand. Len Mercer stood beside the third vehicle with a movement card braced against its fender, comparing names to seats. No one hurried. Once the engines started, they would have to shout.

Sam had come from the operations shelter with less of an answer than he wanted. The shelter was the inside of the old hangar, under the strung bulbs. Against a bare wall a chalkboard stood propped, newer than anything around it, with crumbled chalk on its ledge and a dirty rag for changing what it said.

He asked Len and the convoy leader why the last stop had been added. Len knew the vehicle count and staging time. The convoy leader had received the route. Neither man could tell him what the Marines were supposed to do there.

Sam took the question to the operations watchstander.

“Do the convoy leader and the Marines know what they’re being used for there?”

“They know where they’re going.”

“That isn’t what I asked. Does the stop serve a military purpose?”

The watchstander looked past him toward the movement lane. “The responsible officers reviewed it, Chaplain.”

Sam believed the watchstander when he said responsible officers had reviewed the stop. He let the man return to his radio.

At the hood brief, the convoy leader traced the route on a laminated card. The first legs followed roads they had used. The final access leg did not.

“We vary the approach here,” he said, tapping the last two turns. “You do not stop on this stretch. Not for signs, animals, or people in the road. If a vehicle stops anyway, lead gets clear and trail holds its distance. Listen for sectors.”

The drivers leaned close enough to see the card. One asked about a narrow intersection. The leader answered with a hand motion showing where the first vehicle would take the turn.

Afterward the convoy leader walked the line, spoke briefly to each driver, and stopped near the second vehicle. “Chaplain, you want to get us started?”

The Marines nearest Sam fell quiet. One stood with a hand on the open driver’s door. Another drank from a plastic bottle and kept the water in his mouth while he waited.

Sam took off his cover. “Eternal God, keep our judgment clear and our attention where it belongs. Watch over the Marines in these vehicles and the people who live along these roads. Give our leaders restraint and good sense. Bring everyone home safely. Amen.”

The Marine with the bottle swallowed. “Short one.”

“You’re welcome,” Sam said.

Doors closed. Engines caught one after another, and the lane filled with diesel noise.

His RP had stayed back with the schedule card and the afternoon’s appointments. On the road the Marines in the vehicle were his security. Sam climbed into the second vehicle beside the driver. The Marine tucked the rag beneath his seat and adjusted the mirror one last time. Two Marines rode behind them, one with the radio handset clipped near his shoulder. Len boarded the third vehicle. The lead vehicle rolled to the gate, and the others followed with the road opening between them.

For most of the run, the route looked like other routes. It crossed the river on a bridge below the town, and the town lay along the far bank with its streets running back from the water. Dust lifted from the shoulders and remained suspended after they passed. The driver watched the lead vehicle, then the mirror, then the road ahead. When the distance narrowed, he eased off. When it opened, he recovered it without rushing. As A-driver, the one in the right-hand seat, Sam kept watch on the right.

The turn onto the access street came after a broad road where military tires had pressed the same pale tracks into the dirt. The new street was narrower. Low walls ran close to the road, with doors set into them and laundry moving above. No vehicle had room to turn around without help.

On the broader roads, people often stepped aside before the lead vehicle was on them. Here a man carrying a crate stopped in a doorway and watched the lead vehicle make the turn. A bicycle leaned unattended against the wall. The street had the undisturbed look of a place used mainly by the people living along it.

Sam looked back at the third vehicle holding its interval through the turn.

“This on your usual tour?”

“First time for me,” the driver said.

The lead vehicle held its speed ahead.

Ahead on the left, girls stood along a long wall the color of old sand. Some carried thin exercise books against their chests. Several moved through a metal gate while another group waited outside beneath the direction of two women in full black abayas. The school had let one session out as another arrived, or so it appeared to Sam. No bell carried over the engine.

Two older girls compared a page as they walked. A younger one hopped over a crack in the hard ground, turned, and hopped back as if she had forgotten which side she wanted. At the gate, one of the women counted girls with a finger while the other held the metal door so it would not swing into them.

The convoy leader sounded two short taps on the horn. One of the women extended both arms, and the girls nearest the road drew back toward the wall. The lead vehicle passed close to thirty miles an hour, its speed unchanged from the access leg.

The girl in red was several yards beyond the gate.

She crouched beside the wall with a blue plastic bottle cap between two fingers. She set it on its edge and flicked it. The cap turned once, wobbled, and fell flat. She picked it up, studied the ground, and moved toward a smoother strip where the hard-packed shoulder met the road.

She set the cap down again.

The lead vehicle cleared her. At that speed, the second was only a few seconds behind.

The cap spun. The girl followed its small circle with her head lowered.

Sam turned back to the right side of the street. Something red moved at the edge of his sight.

There was a short, blunt sound.

The engine note did not change.

The driver did not slow or swerve.

Sam looked at the driver. The Marine’s mouth was open, but no air seemed to leave it. Both hands remained on the wheel. His eyes fixed on the left mirror.

Shouting rose behind them.

“Two, keep moving,” the radio said.

The Marine behind Sam had the handset in his fist. “Moving.”

Sam twisted in his seat and looked through the rear opening. The blue cap was still rolling near the left shoulder. It tipped onto its side and settled in a shallow rut as the vehicle carried him away. Red fabric lay farther back at the road edge. One of the women from the gate ran toward it and dropped to her knees. The other gathered her black abaya in both hands and pulled it off her shoulders as she came. The first woman lifted an open hand toward the girls following her, and they stopped near the wall.

The two women lowered the abaya together. The black covering drew smaller behind the moving vehicles.

The girl had been bending over a blue cap. For one senseless instant the red recalled another child’s faded shirt beside the Tomb of Ezra. This child had not approached Sam. She had not touched him or spoken. She had been occupied with a thing she could make spin.

For what felt like a minute, the driver held the ordered speed past another wall and an alley. The school gate passed out of the rear opening. The lead vehicle was already clearing the next intersection. The third and fourth remained behind them at interval. The movement had not stopped for the girl.

Then the driver looked once more into the left mirror. His right foot came off the accelerator.

No radio order told him to stop.

He struck the brake before the intersection. Sam’s harness locked across his chest. Something loose hit the floor behind him, and one of the Marines swore.

The third driver braked hard and stopped behind Sam’s vehicle, leaving a gap between them. The fourth stopped farther back. The lead continued clear through the intersection.

“Two hard stop,” the Marine behind Sam said into the handset. He listened, looked back through the rear opening, and answered, “No contact. Civilian strike behind us.”

Doors opened behind them. Boots struck the road.

“Right side, wall and roofs.”

“Rear clear.”

The commands came over the radio and from outside at nearly the same time. Marines spread into the spaces the vehicles had left. Their weapons faced along the street, toward rooflines, cross streets, doorways, and the ground near the shoulders. The drivers of the other vehicles stayed behind their wheels.

The Marine with the handset leaned between the front seats. “Chaplain, stay in.”

Sam nodded. His hands had found the buckle after the stop. He took them away from it.

The driver still held the wheel. Sam kept imagining his own hand on it, turning them away from the girl. He made himself look up at the Marine’s face.

“Look at me,” Sam said.

The Marine’s eyes did not move.

Through the rear opening, the black covering was now a small shape beside the distant wall. More people gathered inside the school gate. The women motioned them back behind the wall. Sam no longer heard their words.

He knew the Arabic for chaplain. He knew the word for God. Neither was useful from where the driver had stopped them.

No shot came. No explosion followed. The Marines did not relax. One watched the roofline above the school. Another turned slowly between the road behind them and an alley opposite the gate. Neither pointed his weapon toward the women.

The radio sounded again.

“Relief driver to Two.”

A corporal came forward from the third vehicle after another Marine took his sector. He opened the rear door first, then the driver’s door.

“Come on,” he told the driver. “You’re riding back here.”

The driver’s eyes shifted to him, but his hands stayed on the wheel.

The corporal reached across the opening and released the driver’s belt. “I’ve got the wheel. Move to the back.”

Sam said, “Let him help you.”

The driver’s hands came away from the wheel. The skin beneath his fingers had gone pale. The corporal guided him out and into the rear seat. One of the Marines who had been riding there stayed outside with the security element while the replacement driver climbed in.

The convoy leader remained forward of the intersection. After a short radio exchange, he gave the signal to mount.

Marines returned to the vehicles. The Marine displaced from the second vehicle took the relief driver’s former place in the third. The replacement driver eased forward, and the trailing vehicles followed.

Sam looked back once. The black covering remained beside the distant road. The blue cap was no longer visible. He remembered where it had stopped.

They moved to a secured yard several minutes away. Over its wall Sam could see the tops of date palms and nothing else. While the convoy leader made his report on the radio, Sam sat in front of the original driver without turning around to demand his attention. The relief driver took them back to the support area before dark.

❦

The driver sat beside the second vehicle with both hands between his knees. He was shaking.

The wash rack had gone quiet. Dust dried in fans across the doors where Marines had thrown water and stopped before finishing. The replacement driver stood with the convoy leader at the far end of the lane, speaking over the movement card. No one asked the original driver for a tool or signature.

Sam brought him a bottle of water and sat on an overturned crate at a shallow angle. The Marine could see him without having to meet his eyes.

“You don’t have to talk,” Sam said. He put his hand on the Marine’s shoulder and left it there.

The driver’s shaking subsided. Sam placed the bottle on the ground between them.

For a while they watched Marines account for gear. A handset went back into its cradle. A corporal found the rag beneath the driver’s seat and laid it on the hood. When Len crossed the lane with two movement cards, he slowed. His eyes went from the driver to Sam. Sam neither spoke nor motioned him over. Len went on to operations.

A mess runner came down the lane carrying a pan covered in foil. He called that chow would close in twenty minutes. Nobody answered him, so he called it again with greater authority and moved on.

The driver reached for the water. He turned the bottle once between both hands but did not open it.

“I’ll stay,” Sam said.

The Marine nodded, head down.

Sam stayed after chow closed. Sometime after dark a sound came across the river, a hollow knock, then another. The Marines accounting for gear lifted their heads toward it. One said a word to the other, and they went back to their count. Four more came, spaced out. Under the last of them, a short crackle, and then nothing. The driver had not raised his head. Sam put his hand back on the Marine’s shoulder. The ground-level red lights along the vehicle lane were on when he finally stood.

❦

The next morning, Sam asked the executive officer for five minutes outside the command shelter. The sun was not high yet, and the hangar’s shadow still lay across the dirt where they stood.

“A girl died during yesterday’s movement,” Sam said. “I rode beside the driver when the vehicle struck her. Another Marine drove us back. I stayed with the driver last night. He didn’t say a word. Before we left, the Marines I asked could not explain what they were supposed to do at the added stop. The watchstander said the responsible officers had reviewed it.”

The executive officer heard him out.

“Our fourth vehicle carried a load under canvas,” Sam went on. “Nobody told me what it was or who it was for. I prayed over that movement at the hood and rode in with it. I go out on that road without a weapon, sir. I’m allowed to because I carry nothing but myself. That is the whole of my protection under the Conventions. If that load was somebody else’s, then I was the cover for it, and every chaplain who goes out after me is a target. I need to know whose it was, and who added the stop.”

“What rode on a Marine vehicle was ours, Chaplain, until somebody shows me otherwise,” the executive officer said. “I’ve seen the initial report. I’ll have the operations staff review why that stop was added. I’ll speak with the convoy leader about the driver and the other Marines before they go out again.”

Sam thanked him.

❦

Three nights after the movement, the driver found him at the edge of the vehicle lane. “Can we walk, sir?”

They walked along the parked vehicles, facing the same direction. The night had cooled the metal but not the ground. A generator covered the first stretch of silence.

The Marine spoke without looking at Sam. He stopped twice. Both times Sam waited, and both times he continued. At the last vehicle they turned and walked the lane again.

At the end, he stood with his arms close to his sides and watched dust lift beyond the red lights set low along the lane.

“Will you talk to your commander?” Sam asked. “Tell him you need help after what happened.”

The Marine shook his head.

“I’ll go with you,” Sam offered.

“No, sir.”

“You won’t have to go in alone.”

“I said no.” The words came without force. “Please.”

Sam let the silence stand before he answered. “May I repeat any part of what you told me?”

“No.”

“All right,” he said. “I won’t.”

The driver wiped both palms against his trousers.

“I’m walking back with you,” Sam told him.

The Marine nodded.

Sam walked him to his tent. He returned the next morning and found him outside with a cup of coffee cooling between his hands. The Marine said nothing about the night before. Sam sat with him until the first maintenance crews began work.

❦

On the fifth evening after the movement, Len came to him after the movement board had been posted.

He fell into step with Sam at the vehicle-lane edge. They passed two rows before he spoke. “If I bring you something as a matter of conscience, it stays with you?”

“It does.”

“Even if it concerns a movement?”

“I won’t repeat it or take it to command without your permission.”

Len looked over at the operations shelter. “I need you to hold it until I’m ready to put my name behind it.”

Sam nodded. “Then I’ll hold it.”

Len described what he had personally seen and heard in motor transport before the vehicles left, and on the second run through the town two days later. When he finished, they were at the far end of the lane.

“I’ll come back,” Len said.

“I’ll be here.”

Len left him beside the final vehicle and followed the ground-level red lights back to operations.

Sam remained where Len had left him. He remembered the prayer because he had meant every word of it. Clear judgment. Restraint. The people who lived along those roads.

He had asked God to go with them before knowing what the added stop was for.

At the operations shelter the watchstander told him the stop was reviewed, and he let the man go back to his radio. Then he stood at the hood with his cover off and prayed. The fourth vehicle went out under his prayer with its load. A girl died on that road, and he was in the vehicle that killed her.

The words came in the cadence of that prayer, though he had not prayed them.

You put God’s name on it.
