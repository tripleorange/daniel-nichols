Chapter Five

A Familiar Man

Sam had put the visitor sticker on the part of his shirt that folded when he sat. By the time the elevator doors opened, his name had disappeared into the crease.

The man who came up to him glanced at the sticker, then held out his hand.

“Sam Artell. Martin Dambrosky.”

His hand was warm. A pale band of skin showed where he had recently moved his watch. He wore a dark suit without a tie and carried a paper cup with its lid pressed down crookedly.

He led Sam away from the elevators through Delmere’s Reston office. The floor was open and carpeted. People sat at long tables in rows and did not look up. A line of framed maps ran along the inner wall, ports and coastlines, their names too small to read from the aisle. A woman coming the other way lifted a folder toward him. Dambrosky paused and read the page she indicated.

“They’ve changed the delivery date again,” the woman said.

“Can the crew stay another night?”

“Two of them can’t.”

“Then bring those two home. I’ll speak to the client.”

She closed the folder and thanked him. Dambrosky went on to an office at the end of the corridor.

Sam had expected someone older. The biography Jasleen sent with the invitation put Dambrosky in field work before Sam entered the Navy, but his stride remained quick. He was close shaven and gray above the ears. When he spoke to a person he looked at that person and at nothing else. The glance at the sticker was the only time his eyes had left Sam’s face. At the office door he turned his cup upright and found coffee on his thumb.

“Every time I take one of these,” he said.

He put the cup on a side table and reached for a tissue. Beyond the windows, the tops of other office buildings rose between trees. A man in the courtyard below was pulling a wheeled case whose handle seemed too short for him.

Dambrosky indicated the chair. “Please. Coffee? Water?”

“Water would be good.”

He took two bottles from a small refrigerator and set one in front of Sam. Instead of going behind his desk, he sat in the other chair. A framed photograph sat on the low cabinet between them: men around a crane, the boom extending beyond the picture. Sam could not make out what was on the truck door.

“Kuwait,” Dambrosky said, following his glance. “Before we took the equipment north. That crane broke down as soon as we reached the yard.”

“You kept the photograph.”

“I liked that crew.”

Sam twisted the cap from the water. He could not place the younger faces, and he had no reason to think he should. Jasleen had said Dambrosky spent years in the field. She had not said Sam had met him there.

On the wall behind the desk, Mission Support had a mark of its own on a gray panel: the wolf’s head from the truck doors, the cut closed, the two halves one piece now. Sam looked at it once and then at the water in his hand.

“I read your Civic Passage biography,” Dambrosky said. “You stayed with veterans after the Navy.”

“In a different kind of work.”

“A useful one. James tells me you’ve found something our people have been missing.”

“James understands what we’ve built.”

“He does. He also tells me when someone sends him a presentation in place of software. He sounded considerably happier after meeting you.”

Sam smiled before he meant to. He had sent James the explanation he owed him on Tuesday evening. James had sent back a picture of the revised profile. The training a veteran lacked sat next to a list of confirmed capabilities, where a recruiter would see it.

“We’ve started well,” he allowed.

Dambrosky leaned forward, resting his forearms on his knees. “I’d like to hear what you need from us.”

Sam had rehearsed the first question during the drive, saying it aloud to the windshield for most of an hour with the trucks passing him and the radio off. By the Reston exit there was no feeling left in his voice when he said it. He had two questions, and only one of them belonged to Civic Passage. Now the man was in front of him, with coffee drying on the cuff of his sleeve.

“What did you do at Wolmeck?”

Dambrosky did not look away. “I ran field programs. Local suppliers, equipment, people who could get crews where they were needed. Later I helped bring that work into Delmere.”

“Including the work alongside Marines.”

“Including that.”

Sam set the bottle on the table. “What were you asking them to do?”

“Different things in different places. Escort crews. Protect equipment. Help us reach people we couldn’t safely reach on our own. We had local teams trying to get services working again.”

“Who decided where the Marines went?”

“Their officers.”

“On information from your people?”

“Sometimes. We knew the people doing the work. An operations officer might know the road was open and not know if there’d be anybody at the other end.”

Outside the office, someone laughed. Dambrosky waited while the voices passed his door.

“Before one convoy, I asked the leader why a stop had been added,” Sam said. “He had the route. He couldn’t tell me what his men were meant to do there.”

“Did you ask the officer who sent them?”

“I asked the responsible staff. I was told the work had been reviewed.”

“It may have been.”

“That didn’t tell the men what they were doing.”

Dambrosky sat back. “No. It wouldn’t have.”

For a moment the agreement left him with nothing to push against.

“You can’t explain every part of an operation to everyone involved,” Dambrosky went on. “The local people had families. Some couldn’t afford to be seen working with us. We had to protect them too.”

“I’m not asking for their names. A man ought to know what you’re using him for.”

“Even when knowing could put someone else at risk?”

“Then the people sending him ought to be willing to answer for that.”

Dambrosky pressed one finger against the edge of his watch. “Are you asking about a particular movement?”

Sam looked over at the photograph. A sleeve hung from the open door of the crane’s cab. The men below it were looking at whoever had the camera, not at him.

Some of what he wanted to ask came from what he had seen that day. The rest came from men who had sat with him afterward and told him things on the promise that they went no further. He had spent years making sure a question of the second sort never left his mouth sounding like one of the first.

“I’m asking what your company expected from the Marines they worked with,” he pressed.

“And I’m trying to answer. But you remember a day and I remember programs that ran for years.” Dambrosky paused. “Did any of your men speak to you about our work?”

“I won’t discuss conversations I had as their chaplain.”

“Of course.”

Dambrosky said it readily. Sam could not tell if he had expected another answer.

A day was what Sam had. He had kept it nineteen years. Dambrosky had just put it away among his programs.

“I can discuss what I saw,” Sam said. “A load under canvas on our fourth vehicle, and an added stop where it came off. I had led the prayer for that movement. I rode out unarmed. That was my protection, and whatever was on that vehicle went out under it. I asked whose it was. I was told it was ours until somebody showed otherwise. Nobody came back to me.”

“I wasn’t in the room when you asked.”

“No.”

“If you want me to look into a particular job, I’ll try. I won’t promise we can reconstruct every decision from nineteen years ago.”

Sam picked up the bottle and held it between his knees. He would write down the place and the month, hand them over, and leave it to someone higher up to decide where to look.

He had done that before. He remembered being grateful that someone with authority would attend to the question. For a moment he felt the dry heat of the vehicle beside him, although the bottle was cold against his palm.

“I gave it to somebody once,” Sam said. “I was told it would be reviewed.”

He set the bottle down.

“The six assignments came through your side of Delmere,” Sam said. “The part that came over from Wolmeck.”

“Through people who work for me now. Some have been with us a long time.”

“And you want our veterans doing them.”

“If they want the work and can do it.” Dambrosky put a hand to his coffee, checked the lid, and left it on the table. “One of those clients needs to keep deliveries moving through a long power failure. They’d usually ask managers at other distribution companies. Why not ask someone who’s kept supplies moving for the military? James showed me how you find those people.”

“Those people aren’t in the military anymore.”

“I know.”

“They can refuse.”

“Jasleen has agreed to that.”

“I’m asking you.”

Dambrosky watched him. “Yes. They can refuse the work. They can leave it and be paid for what they’ve completed. You should tell them that.”

Sam took his notebook from his bag. He wrote down the words while Dambrosky waited. The pen went through the paper on the first stroke.

“Does that settle whether you’ll help James?” Dambrosky asked.

“Graham called this morning to tell me the board approved the trial. I’m continuing the technical work.”

“I know what Graham’s board decided. I’m asking if you’re willing.”

Before driving to Reston, Sam had told his team that Delmere wanted to discuss buying the technology. They wanted to know if they would keep their jobs at Civic Passage and be allowed to keep improving the model. All he could tell them was that Graham would consider an offer. None had arrived.

Their questions were the ones he would have asked at their age. He was asking them now, at his.

“There isn’t a purchase offer,” Sam said.

“Not yet. Jasleen wants to finish evaluating the model first.”

“Then I don’t want people preparing as though we’re selling it.”

“I’m talking about six assignments. Would you rather we went ahead without your help?”

Sam looked down at his notebook. He had written the promise about leaving the work directly under the question about Wolmeck.

The assignments were useful. He had read them, watched Janet question them, and seen James change his own design. Nothing Dambrosky had said removed that. A few weeks of paid advice could let someone catch up on bills and keep a job they needed. But he kept returning to the Marines who had been sent out not knowing what they were meant to do. If a client began asking an adviser to run a delivery operation instead of reviewing their emergency plan, Sam wanted to hear about it before the person agreed.

“I’ll work with James,” he said. “I’d like to speak with whoever writes the assignments if the work changes.”

“Tell James. He can bring the right person into the conversation.”

Dambrosky stood. Sam put the notebook away and rose with him.

At the door, Dambrosky stopped at the photograph. He straightened the frame, which Sam had not noticed was crooked.

“A good man can spend years thinking he should have known more,” he said. “That doesn’t mean there was something he could have done.”

Sam kept his hand on the strap of his bag. He had not told Dambrosky what he thought he should have done.

“Which man are we talking about?”

Dambrosky left his hand on the frame. “Any of them.”

He had asked Dambrosky about the men and about the work. Perhaps that was enough for the man to work it out.

“I’d still like to know,” Sam said.

Dambrosky nodded and opened the door.

❦

Sam sat in his parked car with the windows down. The car had sat in the sun while he was upstairs, and the wheel was too hot to hold. A delivery driver was arguing into a phone two spaces away. From time to time the driver paused to listen, shook his head, and began again.

Laura had sent a photograph of the ceremony lawn. Water shone in the tire ruts outside the service gate. Under it she had written that she would need the walkway panels in the morning. Sam replied that he would load them when he got home.

He put the phone on the empty passenger seat. He could drive back to Civic Passage, tell Janet what Dambrosky had said, and let the afternoon become work. He would have something to do with every hour of it.

Instead he took up the phone again and searched his personal email for Webb.

The latest exchange was nearly four years old. Webb helped organize a gathering of men from the deployment and asked Sam to come. Sam wrote that he hoped to make the next one. Below Webb’s reply was a cell number and an offer to collect him from the airport if he changed his mind.

He had not answered that part.

Sam remembered Webb at a movement board in Iraq, replacing a strip of paper while balancing a mug on the narrow frame. He was one of the younger Marines who worked the operations watches. Sam stood with him more than once to hear where he could go that day. Webb knew who came into those rooms. He might remember what they asked for.

The number was still in Sam’s contacts. He called it.

While the phone rang, he tried to decide how to begin so it would not sound as though he had been thinking of Webb for the last four years.

The voicemail greeting used the same surname in the same impatient voice. Sam waited for the tone.

“Webb, it’s Sam Artell. Padre. I’ve started working with people who came over from Wolmeck, and I’d like to talk to you about what you remember of them in Iraq. Call me when you have time. This is still my number.”

He ended the call before adding that it was important.
