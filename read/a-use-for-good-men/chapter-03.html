Chapter Three

The Message

On Monday morning, Aisha appeared on his monitor at eight-thirty. Sam had already opened Janet’s three pages. She had sent them late Friday afternoon, with the questions they could answer and the ones they would have to ask Delmere.

Aisha set a legal pad by her keyboard. “Before we talk, Sam, I’m giving you a verbal warning. I’ll send you a summary afterward. You can acknowledge receiving it without agreeing with everything I say.”

Sam leaned back. Sarah’s paper lantern hung above the monitor, outside the camera frame.

“Did Janet complain?”

“No. I spoke separately with everyone who was there. I didn’t need someone to complain to know we had to address what happened.”

“Then you saw how Graham spoke to her.”

“I did, and I’ve spoken to him about it. I can’t discuss that conversation with you.”

That was more than he had expected her to say. He looked at the edge of the legal pad on her screen.

“Janet said he humiliated her,” Aisha continued. “She said your first correction helped. Graham backed down. Then she tried to end the argument, and you wouldn’t stop.”

“She shouldn’t have had to say it was okay.”

“No, she shouldn’t. But she told you she could handle it, and you went on. When you got up and moved toward Graham, you frightened her.”

Sam shifted in his chair. “I didn’t touch him.”

“I know.”

“The way to the door was open.”

“I was in the room, Sam. You don’t have to explain where everyone stood. Janet was frightened by something you chose to do. It matters even if you never intended to hurt anyone.”

His own image filled a small square at the corner of the screen. The camera cut off the top of his head. He closed the preview.

“He threatened to take me off the project.”

“He told you to sit down or he would take you off it. You’re still the technical lead. It won’t stay that way if it happens again.”

Civic Passage owned the model. He had known that when he began, when the first version failed, when Priya Shah and Aaron Cole helped him find the mistake, and when it finally produced something worth showing. He had trusted Civic Passage to use it to help veterans find jobs they would otherwise miss. Until Friday, he had also trusted that he would be in the room when they decided.

Downstairs, Laura moved a pan across the stove. Metal scraped iron and went quiet.

“Did Janet use the word frightened?” he asked.

“Yes.”

He remembered waiting beside the table for her to look at him. Her eyes stayed on the screen.

“Should I call her?”

“Not to discuss her account of the meeting. I’ll ask if she wants to hear from you about what happened. For now, do the work you agreed to do with her.”

“I thought she owed me a look, at least,” Sam said.

“She didn’t owe you anything on Friday.”

“All right.”

“If you disagree with someone this week, stay seated and let them answer.”

There was no smile on Aisha’s face.

“I understand,” he said.

After the call, he acknowledged her written summary and opened a message to Janet. He typed an apology, read it, and saw that he had spent most of it explaining Graham. He deleted the message.

He returned to her three pages. She had left a space for him to explain how much of the trial his team could complete while keeping up their existing work. He filled it with what he had actually asked the team to do, then removed two promises he had not asked them about, and sent it to her. She had asked for his part by ten, and there was nothing more he could honestly put in it.

Sam ended the morning call with James and closed the page they had been marking together. He had promised to send the explanation of one result before the end of the day.

The work inbox was open behind the page he had closed. Near the top of the new messages, a subject line read Leonard Mercer—Iraq, 2003.

Sam took his hand off the mouse.

Outside, a lawn-service truck was backing a trailer into the neighbor’s driveway. Its warning beeps came through the closed window. When they stopped, he opened the message.

Chaplain Artell,

I saw your name on the announcement about Civic Passage and Delmere. Are you the same chaplain who was with us in Iraq in 2003?

Staff Sergeant Leonard Mercer. Motor transport, reserve. I kept the green notebook.

Denise says I should find out if you remember me before I write you a book.

Len

There was no attachment.

The staff sergeant Sam remembered carried the notebook inside his shirt when the dust got bad. He had a broad face and a way of listening with his chin lowered, as though the person speaking might yet get around to the part he needed. Sam remembered walking with him at the edge of a vehicle lane. Len kept his eyes ahead when he began to talk.

Sam could remember the way he asked for privacy. He could remember answering him. He put both palms on Sarah’s desk and left them there.

Communications had posted the partnership announcement that morning. Janet had sent him the link before breakfast: Civic Passage and Delmere were planning paid advisory work for veterans, and Sam would lead the technical evaluation. The page said the board had yet to approve the terms. No one could apply yet.

He had read the announcement as a modest description of something they hoped to do. Len had read his name in it.

Sam began a reply.

Len,

I remember you. I still have trouble finding a vehicle I fit in.

Please don’t email the notebook or anything else from that time. I’d like to hear how you are. Can we talk at four? This is my number.

Sam

He added his personal number, read the message again, and sent it.

At two, Laura found him at the desk, turning an old pen between his fingers. He had answered Len’s confirmation and reopened the page he and James had marked that morning. She had her drawing of the aisle folded in one hand.

“I’m going to the nursery for the aisle markers. Do you want to come?”

Sam set down the pen. “Someone I knew in Iraq got in touch.”

Laura stopped in the doorway. She did not look at his monitor.

“A Marine?”

“Len Mercer. Staff sergeant. We haven’t spoken since then.”

“Is he all right?”

“I think it’s about the advisory work. I don’t know how he is.”

“Do you want me to go on my own?”

He looked at the first page on the screen, where James had asked him to describe how ADICUS would receive one result from the model: the work it recommended for a person and the reason it gave. He could stay and look at it for another two hours.

“No. I’ll come.”

She was already on the stairs. He followed her down, remembering halfway to the kitchen that he had not eaten lunch.

The nursery lot was white gravel, and heat rose off it. Laura walked the rows of pots with her drawing folded in one hand and talked to the man who ran the place about depths and drainage. Sam carried what she pointed out to the cart. Twice she asked him a question and had to ask it again. The second time she looked at him for longer than the question needed, then turned back to the pots.

They were home from the nursery before four. Sam carried the pots through the side gate, washed his hands, and went upstairs. He closed Sarah’s door before the phone rang. His work notebook stayed on the shelf. He turned the chair away from the monitor and sat with his hands loose on his knees, the way he had sat in the tent in Iraq while he waited for a Marine to come in. The light from the one window had left the quilt and lay along the floorboards.

“Chaplain Artell?”

“Staff Sergeant Mercer.”

Len laughed, briefly and without much air. “You sound the same.”

“You don’t.”

“I’ve had nineteen years to improve.”

Sam smiled. “Is that what you’ve been doing?”

“Some days. Denise is here. She wants to know if you really do remember me.”

A woman’s voice came from farther away. “I wanted you to check before you sent him everything in that box.”

“I sent nothing,” Len protested.

“For once,” Denise put in.

“It’s good to meet you, Denise,” Sam said.

“You too, Chaplain. He’s been trying to work out what to call you.”

“Sam is fine.”

Len made a doubtful sound. “We’ll see.”

Sam had heard men give up ranks without giving up the names they called one another. He had done it himself. He settled back in the chair and asked where they were living.

“Outside Charleston,” Len said. “I run fleet operations for a private intermodal company in North Charleston. Trucks, chassis, drivers. Something breaks, somebody closes a road, and we work out how to get through the day.”

“Do you like it?”

“I like most of the people. I like knowing what I’m looking at.” A chair scraped near the phone. “I’m fifty-eight now. I don’t mind work. I’d like to stop being the person they call whenever nobody else picks up.”

“And you’re looking for another job that calls you?” Denise asked.

“I’m looking at a short assignment I could agree to before the phone rings.” Len paused. “We have some bills I’d like to finish paying.”

“We do,” Denise agreed. “But I don’t want another promise that the extra work is only for a little while.”

Something was set down on a table at their end.

“I haven’t promised anybody anything,” Len told her. “That’s why I’m calling.”

“All right,” Denise allowed. “Ask him.”

Len breathed out near the phone. “What is the work?”

“They’re proposing six paid advisory assignments. Helping clients plan for problems with transport and equipment, from what they’ve told us so far. We haven’t agreed to the terms. I can’t promise you a place.”

“I didn’t ask for one,” Len said.

“I know.”

“I saw your name, so I kept reading. I thought you’d have asked a few things before you put it there.”

Sam faced the window. The lawn-service trailer had gone. A ridge of clipped grass remained along the neighbor’s drive.

“We’re asking,” he said. “If this goes ahead, you’ll see the assignment before deciding whether you want to be considered. You choose what we send Delmere about you. Nobody here gets to volunteer you.”

“That’s a start.” Len sounded neither reassured nor offended. “Will your model read my service record?”

“If you apply and agree to it. A counselor reads it first, and another employee checks what the model adds. You get the result whether Delmere offers you work or not.”

“And you look at it?”

“Could you and I speak on our own for a minute?” Sam asked.

Len asked Denise to give them a little time. Sam heard her say she would be in the kitchen, then footsteps and a door closing.

“I can’t be part of reviewing your application,” Sam said. “I was your chaplain. Other people would have to do that work.”

There was a pause.

“You remember,” Len noted.

“I remember that you trusted me.”

When Len spoke again, his voice was closer.

“It’s still private.”

“Yes.”

“I haven’t decided to tell anybody else.”

“You haven’t asked me to.”

Len was quiet. Sam let the quiet remain. He wanted to ask what happened after they left Iraq, whether Len had found anyone else he could talk to, whether the ordinary voice on the phone meant the years had been kinder than he imagined.

“To keep me out of your review,” Sam said, “I’ll need to tell Janet Yoon, who is organizing it, and Aisha Grant in People Operations that I was your chaplain. May I use your name for that?”

“You can tell them we served together, you were my chaplain, and you can’t review me. Nothing from Iraq.”

“Nothing you told me.”

“All right.” Len moved away from the phone. Sam heard him ask Denise if she wanted to come back.

“I’m back,” Denise said a moment later.

“This is why I wanted to speak to you first,” Len said.

“I can still talk to you, Len. Staying out of your application doesn’t mean you can’t call.”

“I might take you up on that.”

“I hope you do.”

Denise asked when there would be something they could read. Sam said the public announcement would be updated after the directors made their decision; she could also contact the career-services office directly. He gave her the number. Len did not ask him to put in a word.

After they ended the call, Sam stood and opened Sarah’s door. He went to the kitchen for water and found Laura fitting one of the new pots over its wooden base.

“How was he?”

“Working. Married. Thinking about taking on more work.”

“Sounds familiar.”

Sam filled a glass. “I was glad to hear him.”

Laura pressed the pot down, tested it, and looked up. “You can tell him that.”

“I think I did.”

She went back to the base. Sam lingered at the sink for a while before returning upstairs. Through the window over the sink the new pots sat in a row along the fence where he had set them down, the paper tags on them turned every way. Behind him Laura worked the pot onto its base a second time and left it. He was glad. The gladness was more about Len than about the pots, and he did not try to say so.

He kept the call with Janet and Aisha short and told them only what Len had allowed. He and Len had served together. He had been Len’s chaplain. He could not review him. Janet would put someone else on any application Len made, and Aisha said he could go on with the evaluation and nothing more.

Afterward he wrote Len three lines saying whom he had told. Then, because it was true, he added a fourth. It had been good to hear his voice.

Len called again while Sam was closing the laptop.

“I got your message,” he said. “Thank you. For doing what you said.”

“You’re welcome.”

“I’ve got the notebook out. There’s something in here I thought you might remember.”

Sam rested his hand on the closed laptop. “If it’s about what you told me—”

“It isn’t. This is from the movement notes I kept in operations.” A page turned near the phone. “Religious support. Ezra. Under that I’ve got Padre A., rabbi, two vehicles. Then Civil Affairs, and one contractor vehicle joining at the river. I don’t know what that was about. I wasn’t on that trip. Did you get there?”

“Yes,” he said. “We got there.”

“Does it say who the contractor was?”

“No. One vehicle, joining at the river. That’s the whole line.”

He remembered the first steps down from the helicopter, how the ground had seemed to move after his feet were already on it. For a moment he heard rotors beating the air over a country nineteen years away.
