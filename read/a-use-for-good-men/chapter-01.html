A Use for Good MenChapter One

Chapter One

The Empty House

Sam’s harness was still locked across his chest. He had not tried to free it.

A generator ran somewhere beyond the wall. Heat had filled the vehicle and settled on his wrists. The trucks parked in the yard were black with soot, not dust, the greasy soot the oil fires had put on everything that spring. A sleeve had been dragged across the nearest door. In the clean stripe an overly masculine contractor logo showed through, a space splitting the animal-like symbol in two.

A man in a civilian shirt stood beside that truck with the convoy leader, who had a handheld radio to his ear. The man checked his watch. He kept his back half turned, and neither of them looked at the second Humvee, where Sam sat.

Beyond them, contractors in tan from boots to ball caps, plate carriers over their shirts, better kit than the Marines had, were handing wooden crates down from the truck’s bed. Two of them had weapons slung on their backs. A crane stood by the gate with its boom lowered to the ground.

The Humvees had stopped in a line inside the gate. Nobody from the yard came near them.

“Two, stand by,” the radio said.

Their original driver sat in the back now. A corporal had moved him there after the stop and taken the wheel. The driver had not spoken since, and Sam did not turn around.

Behind him, someone struck the roof twice with a flat hand.

Laura tapped twice more on the desk above his head. “Where did you go?”

He lay still until the thing above him was Sarah’s desk. He had not heard her bare feet cross the carpet. One shoulder was wedged against a drawer unit and both knees were bent to keep his shoes off the bedspread. The adhesive clip had given way again, leaving the monitor cable to dangle and sway above him.

From the floor the room was all underside. The pale wood of the desk still carried the outlines of a row of stickers Sarah had pressed on in the fourth grade and later tried to scrape away. Under the bed, past the hem of the quilt, a hair tie and a yellow pencil lay where they had fallen. Nobody had missed them.

“Under here,” he said.

Laura bent and looked from the cable hanging above him to the two coils on the floor. “That isn’t an answer to my question.”

“Just thinking. Lots of change right now,” Sam replied.

Laura straightened, a fan of paint cards sticking out of her linen overshirt. Soil had settled into the creases of her knuckles and stayed there through two washings. The sun of a working summer stopped at her sleeves. “For all of us.” She held out her hand. “Give me the slack.”

He passed the loose end up to her.

She held the slack from above while he pressed a fresh clip against the wood and guided the cable through it.

A pale ring from a glass and two scratches in the shadow of the keyboard shelf marked the old desk. While making room for his dock, Sam had found a small blue star inside the right-hand drawer and left it where it was.

The fresh clip held when Sam tested it with two fingers.

He slid out and stood slowly, avoiding the shelf. At six foot five, he discovered most low objects through impact, and Sarah’s room offered a lantern, a sloped ceiling, and a brass hook near the closet.

Sarah was at college now. She had turned nineteen the week before they drove her up. That made her the oldest in her year, as she had been in every class since kindergarten. The room remained hers. Only his monitor and dock occupied the cleared desk. The quilt Laura and Sarah had chosen together, small blue and white squares that Sarah outgrew at fourteen but would not give up, lay smooth across the bed. The afternoon light came through the one window in a long slant across the squares. Photographs stayed clipped above the dresser. The closet door was shut. He had closed it himself the first morning she was gone and had not opened it since.

Laura rested her hand on the back of the desk chair. “It fits.”

“Contained and reversible.”

On the short shelf, a stethoscope lay across the cracked spine of Gray’s Anatomy, the one book Laura had kept from her year of nursing school. A fired-clay fragment marked with shallow wedges propped three books upright. Sarah had used it that way since middle school, despite Sam’s proposals involving a shadow box, display stand, and archival foam.

Laura touched the edge of the shelf. “Did you dust the brick?”

“Its current surface may be historically significant.”

Laura ran one finger across the top. “The history says Sarah hasn’t dusted since May.”

Sam took a microfiber cloth from his pocket and wiped the fragment before she could. It came home from Iraq. When Laura asked again where it had come from, that first winter, he had told her he no longer trusted his memory of who gave it to him or what the man claimed: old, perhaps, or merely old-looking. The script might have been Sumerian or the careful work of someone who knew what Americans would buy, but Sarah called it her bookstop and treated it as the heavy thing that kept anatomy from falling over.

When Laura’s phone chimed, she checked the screen and typed with both thumbs.

“Rain plan?”

Laura slipped the phone into her pocket. “Mother of the bride.”

“Worse.”

“Much,” Laura said. “She has discovered that August is warm.”

Laura had an outdoor ceremony in nine days, and sample pots, sketches, and two aisle markers occupied one end of the dining room. After two lean years, her business returned all at once: three fall ceremonies and a hillside garden for a client devoted to plants that would not grow in Maryland. The whole downstairs had smelled of cut stems and wet soil since June. Nobody had eaten at the table since the Fourth.

Laura faced the window, where the maple had begun to dull at its edges, not yet autumn but no longer fully summer. She stood with her arms crossed over the paint cards, the way she stood at a site before she had decided where the first thing would go. Beyond the glass, the yard sloped down to a row of pines, with no car in the drive and no one due home after practice.

“We could take the extra leaf out of the dining table,” she said. “Put the small lamps back in the living room. Maybe eat somewhere besides in front of our laptops.”

“Your laptop is the one with flower petals stuck under the keys.”

Laura took the back of the chair in both hands. “I’m serious, Sam.”

“About the lamps?”

“About us having some room.”

He glanced around Sarah’s bedroom. “That’s what I’m doing.”

“Yes. It is.”

Before he could ask what she meant, his phone vibrated and Sarah’s face filled the screen above the word Video.

Laura got to it first and accepted the call.

The picture opened on a white ceiling, then swung past half a forehead and one brown eye.

Sarah’s voice came through the speaker. “Mom?”

Laura turned the screen facing her. “We’re in your room.”

Sarah’s face settled into view with her hair tied on top of her head and a strip of blue painter’s tape crossing the wall behind her.

“Show me,” Sarah said.

Laura turned the phone to face the room.

Sarah squinted at the screen. “That’s it?”

Two message banners crossed the top of the phone screen, both from the family thread. One of Sarah’s brothers asked if Dad had filed a permit. Another wanted proof the bed remained neutral territory.

“I already sent pictures,” Sarah said.

“Does it look all right?” Sam asked.

“I said you could use the desk,” Sarah said. “You didn’t even take down the lantern.”

“The lantern and I have reached an understanding.”

Sarah’s laugh came thin through the phone speaker, but it was hers. “Did you leave my gray notebook?”

Sam opened the right-hand drawer. “Top drawer, under the charger you said wasn’t yours.”

The camera steadied when Sarah sat on the edge of her bed. “It’s Michael’s.”

“Michael has not lived here in three years,” Sam said.

“That doesn’t make it mine.”

He held up the notebook. “Do you want us to mail it?”

“No. Leave it there. I’ll get it next time I’m home.”

Sam placed the notebook exactly where he had found it.

When Sarah angled the phone, he saw the narrow bed behind her, already buried under clothes, and the closet door standing open.

“Did they fix that?”

“Fix what?” Sarah tilted the phone at the open closet.

“The closet.”

Sarah closed her eyes. “Dad.”

“It wasn’t square. The upper hinge was pulling out.”

Sarah put a hand behind her and pushed the door. “It closes.”

The latch missed the plate. “If you lift it.”

She lifted the knob, and the latch caught. “Then I’ll lift it.”

Laura lowered the phone enough that Sarah could no longer see Sam’s face and gave him the same small, knowing smile she gave Sarah in the dorm room.

On move-in day he had tightened, shimmed, and tested that closet while Laura carried empty boxes to the car, and had kept at it long after the work ceased to be useful.

That afternoon Laura had looked at Sarah, then at him, and had gone to find the recycling bin. Sarah had put her hand over his on the screwdriver. “Thanks, Daddy.” She had hugged him, close and unembarrassed, the way she hugged him at nine and fourteen. “Why don’t you go get your office together.”

He heard the kindness in it, and he also heard go.

On the phone, Sarah pulled the loose end of her ponytail over one shoulder. “I have to meet my floor for dinner. They’re threatening team-building.”

“Don’t give them your Social Security number,” Sam said.

A dimple appeared at the corner of Sarah’s mouth. “That was one icebreaker.”

He closed the drawer. “I knew it.”

“Love you,” Sarah said.

Laura raised the phone again. “Love you too.”

“Love you, Sare,” Sam said.

“Send me a picture when you’re done,” Sarah added.

When the call ended, the dark screen stayed in Laura’s hand. “You know she’s fine.”

Their reflections replaced Sarah’s face on the dark screen. “I know she has a defective closet,” Sam said.

Laura turned the phone facedown. “Which is not the same thing.”

“No.”

Laura waited while the house held its new shape around them: no music through the wall, no shoes on the stairs, no request shouted from another floor, only the refrigerator’s hum coming up from the kitchen.

She handed him the phone and went downstairs, where the sample pots sat on the dining table. He listened to her go down: the fourth stair, which had reported every one of the children coming in late, then the hall, then the kitchen.

He opened the group text he and Laura shared with Don Rowan and his wife, Ellen. Don had known him since they were boys in South Carolina. He had asked when Sam and Laura could come down now that Sarah was away. Sam started an answer and found he did not know which weekend to offer. He closed the message with Don’s question on the screen.

After she left, Sam carried the spare cables to the bedroom and folded the table he had worked at for two years, between Laura’s side of the bed and the laundry hamper. It had left a rectangle in the carpet. The pile stood up pale where the legs had pressed it. Laura had slept two years with that table at her elbow, his lamp on past midnight and his chair rolling back each time a run finished. She had not once asked when the table would be gone.

He carried the computer and keyboard to Sarah’s room, connected them to the dock, and sat at the desk.

The monitor woke to the Civic Passage logo, then a locked screen and four notifications: two routine model reports, a message from a career-coaching director, and a calendar invitation marked On-site required.

He opened that one first.

The meeting was in Arlington on the twenty-sixth of August, 2022, in a training room Sam knew well. Beside Sam’s name, the agenda listed two tasks: explain how the model worked and say what records it could read.

He clicked Accept.

After the Navy, he had gone back to school for statistics, then spent years writing programs to translate military experience into something civilian employers could understand.

In her message, the career-coaching director described a blind test: a veteran whose name had been removed and whose case the model had never seen. They gave it only his service history.

The former Marine had coordinated people, vehicles, fuel, and hazardous cargo through ports and airfields. His civilian résumé described that work only as warehouse support. A conventional matching program returned warehouse supervisor, inventory clerk, and dispatcher.

Sam’s model read the assignments together and recommended intermodal operations planner, emergency logistics coordinator, and utility restoration coordinator. Next to the utility role, it listed two civilian safety courses the service record did not show.

Sam opened the explanation.

It said the veteran had managed port operations. His record showed him coordinating them. Sam corrected the wording and ran the case again. The recommendations held.

A little before six, Laura appeared with two glasses of iced tea.

She set one glass by the keyboard and kept the other. The glass had already begun to sweat. He moved it onto the mouse pad, off the wood, and saw her see him do it. “Is that the thing they want?”

“Part of it.” Sam angled the monitor her way.

“What am I looking at?”

“A test where we hid the answer before we ran it.” He opened the hidden civilian outcome. After leaving the Marines, the veteran completed both courses and spent four years coordinating utility crews and equipment after storms.

Laura leaned close enough to read, one hand braced on the desk. Her eyes moved from utility restoration coordinator to the four years recorded below it. “It found the job he ended up doing.”

“One of three possibilities.”

She read the hidden outcome again. “That matters.”

Laura did not look up from the screen. “How much of your model do they want?”

“I don’t know yet. We’re supposed to decide what Civic Passage can offer at the meeting.”

Laura straightened. “In person?”

“Arlington.”

“When?”

He told her.

Laura lifted her glass. “Dinner outside?”

Sam checked the time on the monitor. “Half an hour. I want to finish checking this.”

She left with her tea. Downstairs, a cabinet door closed. Sam reopened the explanation and compared its descriptions of the veteran’s other duties with the service record, looking for another overstatement.

The air-conditioning shut off.

The vent above the window gave a final metal tick, and the low rush of air withdrew from the room. In the quiet, the computer fan became audible.

Pressure stayed in Sam’s ears, the way it had on nights when a generator cut out and it took a while to believe the quiet.

He swallowed. The right one cleared. The left did not.

He sat still, listening for the system to restart. The room felt sealed, though the door was open behind him on the hall, the folded table, and a strip of sunlight across the floorboards.

For several seconds the room seemed to have changed altitude while the rest of the house stayed where it was.
