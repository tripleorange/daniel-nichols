Chapter Thirty-Six

Saturday with Sarah

Sam set the bag by Sarah’s chair. She stood and put her arms around him before he had taken off his coat. He bent to hold her, feeling the pressure of her forehead against his shoulder.

“Are you all right?”

“I thought they’d stopped watching us. He knew we were meeting for lunch.”

“Do you think somebody followed you?”

“I don’t know. I didn’t notice anyone on the way here. He said it was your mother they stopped watching. Not me.”

She moved back and looked at his face. Tessa hovered at the neighboring chair with her phone in her hand. She was a head shorter than Sarah, in a hooded sweatshirt with the hood down, and she kept the phone turned so that the screen faced Sarah. Sam remembered her at the corner while he tried to persuade Sarah to go home with him.

“It’s good to meet you properly, Tessa,” he said. “I didn’t give you much of a chance last time.”

“I wasn’t sure you saw me.”

“I did. I’m sorry.” He looked at the two young women. “Thank you for staying with her. The other day too.”

“You’re welcome.” Tessa lowered her phone. “We were together when you texted. She’s told the desk we’re here.”

Sam watched the student center’s entrance. The woman at the desk was speaking into a telephone. The room was glass on two sides, with a stair going up at the back and a corridor off to the left that he could not see down. Beyond the glass doors, people crossed the walk in both directions. He wanted Sarah somewhere he could watch every way in.

He sat beside her.

The campus officer who came to meet them was a woman with a notebook in one hand. She was young for the job, in a dark uniform jacket with a radio at the shoulder, and she pulled a chair over before she asked anything. Sarah gave her the name of the officer she had spoken to before. She said her father had just been approached by the executive whose company paid to have her photographed.

Sam described where Dambrosky had been standing and what he wore. When the officer asked what he had said, Sam repeated the words about meeting Sarah, then the last remark about their lunch.

“Did he tell you how he knew you’d be here?”

“He told me I’ve been followed. He didn’t say by whom.”

Sam did not need the name. He had read five names once, under an added stop, in Webb’s downhill hand.

“And you saw him leave?”

“I saw him walk toward the far end of the lot. Then he went behind a van and I lost sight of him. I don’t know if he got into a car.”

She wrote that down. He spelled Dambrosky’s name for her and found the executive page on Delmere’s website.

She gave them a number to use if Dambrosky approached either of them again on campus.

“What do you have planned now?” the officer asked.

“We were going out to lunch,” Sarah answered. “I’d rather stay here. There’s a cafe by the library.”

Sam looked at her. He could have offered the car, the house, the relief of putting miles between her and the place where Dambrosky had found him. Sarah was explaining which cafe she meant. When she had finished, she looked at him and waited.

“Will you show me where it is?”

“Yes.” She reached across and put her hand on his sleeve. “We can walk.”

The officer nodded and asked her to keep her phone with her.

“I will,” Sarah said.

There was no promise that the man would be found, or that he could not return. The officer went back to the desk to speak to someone there. Sarah put the number in her phone, then turned to Sam.

“Have you told Mom?”

“I called her. She wants to hear from us once we’ve spoken to the officer.”

They called from the chairs at the window. Sarah told Laura what they had reported and that they were staying on campus for lunch. Sam could hear his wife’s questions through the speaker. He answered when she asked what Dambrosky wanted.

“He asked me to work for him. I said no.”

“Will you tell Evelyn?”

“He doesn’t answer to Evelyn. He never did. They can vote him off the board. It won’t touch him. I’m going to write down for Janet and the counselors what he said about his analysts, that they’re working out who a veteran asks before he takes a job. That much I can still do.”

“He went to her school,” Laura said. She was quiet after that. Then she asked Sarah what she wanted to do.

“Have lunch with Dad. Tessa’s going to the library. I’m meeting her afterward.”

“Call if that changes.”

“I will.”

Sarah asked how the consultation had gone. Laura had just come out of it. The client had said yes to the whole garden, not only the beds. Laura had not priced a single plant yet. She had wanted it, and she would tell them the rest tonight. Sam listened to the two of them and did not interrupt. The last thing Laura said was to him. Call before you drive.

Tessa walked with them as far as the cafe. At the entrance she touched Sarah’s arm and said she would see her inside the library later.

“Thank you again, Tessa,” Sam said.

She smiled at him. “You’re welcome.”

She went up the library steps. Sarah watched her go, then opened the cafe door.

“I’m still hungry.”

“So am I.”

Inside, the cafe smelled of toast. At a table by the window, two students bent over a laptop, their lunches pushed to either side.

A man carrying menus offered them a booth. Sam looked under the table and asked whether they could have chairs instead. Sarah led the way to the table the man indicated, with room at one end for her father’s knees.

Sam put the bag at her feet. She took out the notebook, checked the bent corner of the cover and put it in her backpack.

“I kept thinking I’d packed it.”

“You left it under Michael’s charger.”

“Which is still there?”

“Still there.”

Sarah smiled a little, folded the coat over the back of her chair and sat down. Sam put a hand in his pocket and found the keys, and held them a few seconds, pressing their edges against his palm. Then he let them go and opened the menu.

Sarah knew what she wanted but kept reading the description of another dish, as though it might become more persuasive if she gave it time. Sam remembered her doing that with books in a shop, taking two to the counter and trying to make him choose which one she should leave.

“You can get that next time.”

She looked up.

“The other lunch,” he said. “You’re going to live here for a while.”

“I know. I’m trying to have a varied diet.”

“What did you eat yesterday?”

“Something from a machine while I was waiting for Tessa.”

“Something?”

“It had a picture of food on the wrapper.”

He laughed. The server came to take their order. Sarah changed her mind when he came up to her, then returned to her first choice while Sam pretended to study the window.

“You can look at me,” she told him after the man had gone. “I know what I did.”

“I wasn’t going to say anything.”

“You don’t have to. You’ve got a whole face for it.”

He laughed again, more freely this time. Sarah took a drink of water. There was a little color in her cheeks from the walk.

She told him about a lab partner who kept taking her pen. He did it and never noticed, she said. By the end of a class he could have three of them lined up beside his notebook while she wrote with one that had no cap and had leaked into her bag.

“Have you asked for them back?”

“Yes. He gives them back. Then he picks one up again.”

Sam pictured arranging the pens on the far side of the bench, beyond the other student’s reach. He began to suggest it, then Sarah took a pen from her backpack.

She had attached a length of bright ribbon to its end. The ribbon hung nearly to the table.

“That’s difficult to overlook,” he said.

“He borrowed it too.”

“Of course he did.”

“But he knew whose it was. He handed it back before we left.” She put the pen away. “I think I made him feel bad. He brought me a whole packet yesterday.”

“That was decent of him.”

“It was. They’re terrible pens.”

Sam smiled. She had not finished when the food came. He moved the paper bag off the next chair so the server could get between the tables, then listened while she explained why she would have to keep using at least one of the new pens.

They ate slowly. Sarah asked about her mother’s work, and he told her about the plants they had kept moving away from the kitchen door. Laura had objected when he called them temporary because she had heard him use that word about the office.

“Is my room still an office?”

“My laptop’s on the kitchen table now. The papers are down. I’ve made a mess of the paint.”

“How bad?”

“Bad enough that I took photographs before I offered to fix it.”

“Show me.”

He found the pictures and passed over his phone. She enlarged one with two fingers.

“That bit was already there.”

“Which bit?”

She pointed. “By the window. I pulled a poster down. Mom said we’d paint when we could agree on a color.”

“We can still do that.”

“Can we? I don’t want the same color.”

“Tell your mother what you’re thinking. She’ll want to look at the light and bring home every paint card in Maryland.”

Sarah smiled at the screen. “I used to like that.”

“You can still like it.”

“I do.” She handed the phone back. “I’ll ask her when I’m home.”

They finished eating. Sarah pushed a piece of bread around her plate, picking up the last of the sauce. Sam had been hoping they could order coffee and go on talking about her classes until it was time to leave. She set the bread down.

“I need to tell you something,” she said.

He took his hand away from his glass.

“When Mom said you were at the hospital, I kept thinking about what I said outside the library.”

He opened his mouth, then waited.

“I told you to go home. And then you went away for days, and Mom was trying to find you. I didn’t know whether you were still upset with me.”

“I wasn’t upset with you.”

“You didn’t tell me that.”

Sam looked at the table between them. He had sent an apology and thought he was giving her time.

“No,” he admitted. “I didn’t.”

“I wanted you to leave that day. I still wish you’d just gone when I asked. But I didn’t mean I never wanted to see you.”

“I know.” He stopped. “I don’t think I knew it very well then.”

Her eyes filled. She looked past him toward the window, pressing her lips together. He wanted to move his chair beside hers. He remained where he was while she took a napkin from the table.

“You didn’t put me in the hospital, Sare.”

“I didn’t say that.”

“I know. But I don’t want you trying to find the thing you could have said differently that would have kept me out of it.”

She pressed a knuckle under one eye.

“Were you having trouble before I left for college?”

“Yes.”

“How long?”

He drew a breath. He had answered versions of the question all week, and he wanted to give a date that would make the answer smaller.

“Years. It hasn’t always been this bad. I could work. I could do things with you. Those weren’t pretend.” He looked at her. “But I haven’t been sleeping well for a long time. Some mornings I’d wake up already waiting for something to go wrong. I didn’t want anybody to know how much of the day I spent doing that.”

“Even Mom?”

“She knew more than I told her. I made it hard for her to ask.”

Sarah folded the napkin into a small square.

“I don’t know what I’m supposed to do with that.”

“You don’t have to do anything with it today.”

“I mean when I call you. If you tell me you’re okay, am I supposed to believe you?”

He turned his glass a little. He could promise to tell her everything and make another promise he had no right to make. There would be things she did not need to carry, things he would need to discuss with Laura or the woman at the clinic.

“I can tell you I’ve had a bad night,” he offered. “Or that I’m having a hard day. I can tell you what I’m doing to get help, instead of saying I’m fine because I want you to stop worrying.”

“And if you don’t?”

“You can ask me again. You can call Mom. I won’t ask you to keep something from her.”

Sarah watched him, then nodded once. She did not put down the napkin.

“I’m still angry about you coming,” she said. “I had people helping me. You kept talking as if none of them counted.”

Outside the library that morning, Tessa had stood at the corner through all of it. He had scarcely looked at her except to wonder what she thought of him.

“I kept imagining getting a call that something had happened to you,” he said. “I couldn’t bear the thought of being at home when it came. I thought if I could get you into the car, I’d be able to think again.”

“I had an exam to study for. I told you about it that morning.”

“I know.”

“Do you? Because I went back to class and couldn’t remember what we’d been doing. Tessa had to go over it with me that night. And you were driving off to talk to that man, and I didn’t know what you’d do when you got there.”

He looked at her face. She had been frightened for him while he told himself she did not understand enough to be frightened for herself.

Look what you have made her do.

The words came as readily as if he had been waiting to say them. He pressed his feet against the floor. Sarah was in front of him, holding the napkin. He wanted her to reach across the table and tell him he had been a good father most of the time. He wanted it so much that he could feel the question beginning in his mouth.

“I’m sorry,” he said instead.

She nodded, looking down at her plate. He stayed quiet. The server passed them and went on to another table.

After a while Sarah said, “I thought if I could explain it properly, you’d listen. But every time I answered you, you asked me something else.”

Sam had done that to men who wanted to go back to work before he thought they should. Sometimes another question helped. Sometimes he had wanted the person to remain where he could see him. He could remember the effort of making his voice gentle while he tried again.

“I was looking for a way to get the answer I wanted,” he said. “When you didn’t give it to me, I kept asking.”

“I can’t text you every time I get somewhere so you can breathe again.”

“No.”

She took a breath and let it out. Sam moved his hand away from the phone. He had not meant to reach for it.

“If he comes back, I’m calling that number,” she said. “I’m not going to wait until you can get here.”

“You shouldn’t wait.”

“You say that now.”

He could feel the keys in his pocket. A few hours earlier he had wanted to put her in the car before he had even seen her. He wanted to know whether Dambrosky had left, whether he would be there when Sarah came out of the library another day.

“I was glad you’d called them today,” he said. “You knew what to do before I’d thought past keeping you inside.”

Sarah looked down at her phone. “Tessa helped.”

He drank some water. “If I learn something you need to know, I’ll call you and Mom together, and your brothers. You should hear it from me before I come up with a plan for what you ought to do.”

“You can ask what I think.”

“I will.”

She raised her eyes. The cuff of her sleeve was picked loose at the seam, and she had her mother’s way of setting her jaw before a hard thing. Sam looked at her until she looked away.

“What would you like to do this afternoon?” he asked.

Sarah looked out the window. “We could walk. I don’t want to sit here anymore.”

He asked for the bill.

They took the bag back to his car. Sarah put on the blue coat, then found the ribboned pen catching in the zipper of her backpack. While she worked it free, Sam wrote to Webb. He asked nothing. He wrote that he had thought of him this week, and that if there was another gathering he would like to come. He put the phone away before he could reread it.

Sarah tucked the ribbon inside and closed the zipper. Sam put the empty paper bag in the car. When he turned, Sarah was at the end of the row with her hands in her coat pockets.

“There’s a way back along here,” she said. “It’s a little longer.”

“Do we have time?”

“Yes.”

He locked the car and went with her. The path rose behind the library before turning between two of the older college buildings. Leaves collected along the fence. Sarah took the turns without looking where they went, and raised two fingers to somebody at a window he had not noticed. Sarah walked through them without avoiding the deeper patches, and he could hear the dry scrape beside his own steps.

For a while they said nothing. He was tired from the drive and from sitting across the table trying not to defend himself. Sarah stopped to pull a leaf from where it had caught between her shoe and her sock.

He stood while she bent to her shoe, and prayed with his eyes open, the way he had once prayed in a lane with the doors open: for her, for the two of them, and for something to do with his hands. He kept it short.

Study. Do. Teach. The rabbi had read it to him in a room by the river, and the middle word had stayed with Sam nineteen years.

“I’ve been thinking about coming home for a weekend,” she said when she straightened. “Not this one. I want to see Mom. And I want to sort through some things in my room.”

“They’re there.”

“I know.” She looked at him. “I might bring Tessa, if that’s okay. She’s been asking if you really used to carry all our bags at once when we went away.”

Sam considered the allegation. “Usually.”

“We couldn’t see you from the back. You were just bags.”

He laughed. “It saved a trip.”

“It saved you a trip. We were all walking in with nothing.”

“You could have offered.”

“We did. You told us to open doors.”

He could remember saying it, with a strap cutting into his shoulder and something sliding toward his elbow. They had stood aside while he angled himself through doorways, calling for someone to get the light.

“Tessa’s welcome.”

“She’s a little nervous.”

He looked at Sarah.

“Because of the library?”

“Some of it. She knows you’re not always like that.”

He nodded. Whatever Sarah had told her would not change what Tessa had seen.

“Tell her I’d be glad to have her.”

“I will.”

They came through a gate onto a narrower walk. Sarah pointed out the room where she had her early class. One of the windows was open a few inches. Sam imagined her hurrying along this path while he was at home, deciding whether to call. There were mornings of hers he would never see, people whose names would mean nothing to him until she chose to tell him about them.

“Do you like it?” he asked. “Being here?”

“Mostly.”

“What’s the part you don’t like?”

“Sometimes everybody sounds as if they already know what they’re going to do. Not just their major. Where they’ll go afterward, what they’ll specialize in. I still change my mind about lunch.”

He smiled, then looked at her more carefully. She was watching the open window.

“You don’t have to know all of it yet,” he said.

“I know people say that.”

“Do you want me to stop saying it?”

“No. Just don’t sound so sure.”

He laughed quietly. “I can manage that.”

Sarah smiled at him. They walked on. He asked which class she looked forward to, and she took some time to answer.

“Astronomy, I think.”

“The evening class you didn’t want?”

“I didn’t want anything after dinner. That’s different.” She pushed her hands farther into her coat pockets. “We went outside Tuesday. They set up telescopes behind the science building. I thought we’d all take a look and go back in, but Devon kept asking about every single thing he could see.”

“Who’s Devon?”

“He sits next to me. He takes more notes than anyone I’ve ever met. He was trying to write in the dark.”

“What were you looking at?”

“Jupiter. There were these little points beside it, and our professor told us they were moons. I knew it had moons. I didn’t know I’d be able to see them.”

“Could you see the planet clearly?”

“It was smaller than I expected. I kept waiting for it to get bigger.” She laughed. “Like somebody was going to turn something and give us the proper view. But that was it. I went around to the back of the line so I could look again.”

He pictured her out in the cold while other students bent to the eyepiece, without the coat he had brought her today. She was wearing it now, talking about something she had liked before he knew it had happened.

“Did Devon get his notes?”

“He sent me a picture of the page. Half of it was written over the other half. He wanted to know if I could remember what he’d been trying to write.”

“Could you?”

“Some. We compared them yesterday.” She glanced at him. “You don’t have to look like that.”

“Like what?”

“Like I’ve introduced you to someone.”

Sam laughed. “You told me his name.”

“That’s all I’ve told you.”

She smiled and went on telling him about the class.

Near the library, Sarah checked the time. Tessa sat with two other students on the low wall by the entrance. She looked up and raised a hand. Sarah waved back.

“I’ve got to go.”

He stopped at the edge of the path. She adjusted her backpack and looked at him. He wanted another hour, preferably one in which he had done nothing she needed to forgive.

“Thank you for lunch,” she said.

“Thank you for asking me.”

She stepped forward and put her arms around him. He held her. For a moment her forehead rested against his shoulder, as though she was tired too.

“Love you, Dad.”

“Love you, Sare.”

She moved back, keeping one hand on his sleeve.

“Text me when you’re home?”

“I will.”

“And tell Mom I’ll call tomorrow.”

“You can tell her yourself.”

Sarah glanced at her phone. “I will. After this.”

He nodded. She took a step toward the building, then turned back.

“You never asked about the film.”

“The three-hour one?”

“Yes. I had things to say.”

“You can call me and say them.”

“You’ll have to watch it again first.”

“It’s been years. I don’t remember most of it.”

“Then you definitely have to.”

He remembered stopping himself before he could give away the ending. She had called because she wanted to speak to him, even then.

“Send me the name again.”

Sarah took a few steps backward, smiling now. “No reading a summary.”

“I wouldn’t.”

She gave him a look he knew very well.

“I’ll watch it,” he promised.

“Good. Then we can argue.”
