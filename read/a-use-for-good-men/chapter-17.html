Chapter Seventeen

The Driver

The lance corporal had the hood up when Sam came down the vehicle lane.

He stood on the front bumper with a rag in one hand and his head inside the engine compartment. Staff Sergeant Keene waited at the fender with the clipboard under his arm. Sam had not been back to the supply yard since the day the lance corporal could not walk into the container. In the days between, the staff sergeant had gone to the lieutenant.

“You told me to ask him what work he could do,” the staff sergeant said.

“I did.”

“He said he could drive.” The staff sergeant glanced at the boots on the bumper, then at Sam. “He’s detailed to you, sir. The lieutenant signed it. He drives, he fuels, he waits with the vehicle. He doesn’t go in the containers.”

“Does he know you’ve told me that?”

“He knows he’s driving you.”

The lance corporal came down off the bumper and let the hood down. He did not look at either of them. “Belts and fluids are good, sir.”

“Thank you.”

“Thirteen hundred,” the staff sergeant told him. “Fuel it before chow.” He tipped the clipboard at Sam and went back toward the supply yard.

The Marine went to the driver’s mirror with the rag. He wiped it the way he had on the morning of the run. Some of the gray came off on the rag. Most of it stayed on the glass.

“Have you eaten?” Sam asked.

“I’ll eat, sir.”

“That isn’t what I asked.”

The Marine put the rag under the seat. “No, sir.”

“Then eat before you fuel it. I’ll find you here.”

“Yes, sir.”

A detachment an hour down the river had asked for a service. Two vehicles went. The lance corporal drove the first, with Sam beside him in the right-hand seat and nobody behind them. The Marines who rode as Sam’s security were in the second vehicle, holding their interval.

The road ran out along the river with fields on both sides and palms in clumps where the water was. The lance corporal drove as he had on the run, his eyes going from the road to the mirror and back. He gave the second vehicle its room. For the first mile Sam watched the Marine’s hands on the wheel. Then he made himself watch the road.

“The corporal can read the board without me,” the lance corporal said, before Sam had asked him anything. “I wrote it so he could.”

“I saw the board.”

“Yes, sir.”

That was all he said on the way out that was not about the vehicle.

The detachment was a walled compound at a bend in the river, sandbags along the top of the wall, an antenna, a vehicle with a Marine asleep under it in the only shade. The service was in the lee of the wall: a dozen Marines, a corpsman, a folding table with a cloth over it. The lance corporal did not come to it. He stayed with the vehicle, where Sam could see him from the table, sitting sideways in the driver’s seat with the door open, his weapon across his knees and his eyes on the ground between his boots. Sam read the psalm. He said the prayer with his cover off, looking at the Marines in front of him and not past them at the vehicle.

Afterward three Marines wanted a minute each, and Sam stood where each of them chose. The corpsman carried bottles of water out to the vehicles and held one up to the lance corporal. The lance corporal shook his head. The sun was low when the vehicles started back.

The light was going when the road turned away from the river and ran straight between two fields. A canal lay along the right side, and its bank was built up higher than the road.

The driver eased the wheel to the right.

Sam looked ahead for whatever he was making room for. There was nothing in the road. The right wheels left it and climbed the bank. Loose dirt rattled under the wheel wells. The vehicle tilted. Sam’s side came up, and the belt took his weight toward the driver. The bank was steeper above them. The driver kept the wheel turned toward it.

“One,” the radio said. “One, what are you doing?”

“Bring it down,” Sam said.

The driver looked straight ahead at the top of the bank. His mouth was closed. The hands on the wheel had not moved.

“Look at me.”

The Marine’s eyes came around to him.

“Bring it down. Then we’ll talk.”

The driver turned the wheel left. The vehicle came off the bank and struck the road with all four wheels. A canteen went across the floor. The driver held the road and the speed.

“One, what was that?” the radio said.

The driver took the handset without looking at it. “Shoulder went soft. We’re up.”

Neither of them said anything else for the rest of the road. At the gate the driver stopped where the sentry wanted him and waited to be waved through.

❦

The second vehicle pulled in behind them in the lane. Its Marines came past on their way to turn in their gear, and the last of them, a corporal, stopped at the driver’s door.

“What was that back there?”

“Shoulder went soft, Corporal.”

The corporal looked past him at Sam. Sam met his eyes and did not answer. The corporal went on up the lane. The lance corporal had the hood up again before he was out of sight.

Sam stayed at the fender. The low red lights were on along the lane. Above it the sky was black, with more stars in it than Sam ever saw at home.

“Have you heard of Abraham?” Sam asked. “From the Bible.”

The Marine took his head out of the engine. “Yes, sir.”

“Look up.”

The lance corporal looked at him, then up.

“That’s the sky he looked at,” Sam said. “God took him outside one night and told him to count the stars, if he could count them, and said that was how many his children would be. He was an old man with no children. Those stories are more than stories. They connect us to a God who is real and who cares about us. About you.”

The Marine kept his face turned up.

“Did he count them?”

“Nobody can. He believed it anyway. That’s the part that got written down.”

“And another thing,” Sam said. “My wife is due in August. If you make another plan like that one, leave me out of it. She would hold it against you.”

The sound the Marine made was not a laugh, but it was near one. He put a hand on the hood and lowered it until it latched.

“I wasn’t going to bring it down, sir.”

“I know.”

“What I said just now. That stays with you.”

Sam did not answer at once.

“You’re the chaplain,” the Marine said. “You can’t tell anybody.”

“What you’ve told me is private,” Sam said. “It stays with me. What you did out there isn’t. The second vehicle saw it, and the corporal asked you about it in front of me. That has consequences. One of them is that I have to ask the executive officer for a consult, and I have to ask him now. I fly out at first light.”

“He’ll ask what I said.”

“He can ask. I won’t tell him.”

The lance corporal nodded once.

Sam stayed at the vehicle until the Marine had put the rag under the seat and gone up the lane toward the tents. Then he went to the command shelter and asked for the executive officer.

“The driver from the run, sir. He drove me today. On the way back he put the vehicle up the bank of a canal, and the second vehicle saw it. I’d like him put in for a consult.”

The executive officer looked at him. “Did he say why?”

“I’m asking for the consult, sir.”

“I’ll put him in for the morning.”

“Thank you.”

Sam lay down on his cot with his boots on. He was still lying there when the helicopter came in.

From the air he could see the strip, the hangars and the lane with its vehicles in a row. Then there was only dust.

❦

At the other base he held a service in the morning and another at dusk. Between them he sat with the men who asked for him, and with one who had not asked but had stood near the door until Sam looked up. He lay down late.

A Marine woke him with a hand on his boot.

“Chaplain. Sir.”

Sam sat up.

“Your unit’s on the net. They want you back.”

“Who is it?”

“A lance corporal, sir. He’s alive. That’s all they gave me.” The Marine stood back from the cot. “There’s a helo going that way before light. I’ll take you out to it.”

Sam had the helmet bag on his shoulder before the Marine had the flap open.

He came down into the rotor wash with the sun not yet up. The staff sergeant was at the edge of the strip with no clipboard.

They walked toward the tents. The staff sergeant told it without being asked.

“He had his consult in the morning, sir. They sent him back to me by noon, and I kept him in the yard with me. I’ve had eyes on him since the day at the container. I went to receiving for ten minutes. When I came back around the containers he had the section’s generator pulled out with the cover off, and he was wiring it up.”

Nobody ever told Sam what the Marine had wired it to, and he did not ask.

“Where is he?”

“Aid station. There’s a corpsman on him. Doc says a psychiatrist is coming this afternoon. Air Force. I don’t know from where.”

The staff sergeant stopped short of the tent.

“You rode with him.”

“Yes.”

The staff sergeant waited. Sam said nothing.

He looked at Sam a moment longer, then went back toward the yard.

A corpsman sat at the entrance to the aid station on a folding stool. He stood when he saw who it was.

The lance corporal sat on a cot inside with his boots on and his hands open on his thighs. He looked up when Sam came in, then at the tent wall.

Sam sat on the next cot, turned toward the wall rather than the man.

“You came back, sir.”

“Yes.”

The Marine said nothing else for a long time, and Sam did not ask him to. The sun came onto the tent wall and moved across it. The corpsman brought two bottles of water and set them on the floor between the cots. The Marine opened his and drank half of it.

“Did he ask you, sir?”

“He asked. I told him what the second vehicle saw. Nothing you said.”

The Marine looked at the tent wall again.

Somewhere beyond the tents a generator was running.

An Air Force psychiatrist came in the afternoon. He asked Sam to step out, and Sam stepped out. The corpsman stood beside him in the sun and did not try to talk.

The psychiatrist came out after an hour and stood with his back to the tent.

“He goes home,” he said. “Tonight, if there’s a flight. He doesn’t go back to that section.”

“Yes,” Sam said.

“Did he say anything to you?”

“He said I came back.”

The psychiatrist looked at him, then went to arrange the flight.

Sam went back inside and sat down.

The helicopter came in after dark. The lance corporal walked out to it between Sam and the corpsman. His bag was on the corpsman’s shoulder, and his own hands were empty. At the ramp he stopped and looked up. Then the crew chief put out an arm, and he went in.

Sam stood at the edge of the strip until the aircraft had lifted and the dust had come down again.

In the morning a Marine Sam did not know was on the bumper of his vehicle with his head inside the engine compartment.
