Chapter Thirty-Five

Another Offer

On Thursday, Sarah sent the meeting place and asked Sam to bring her gray notebook. The one in her desk drawer. She needed her warmer coat too. He wrote both things on the directions so he would not arrive with one of them left on the bed.

❦

On Saturday morning, the gray notebook had not moved from under Michael’s charger. Sam lifted the charger by its cord, freed the notebook and put the charger back. One corner of the cover had bent beneath the plug. He straightened it with his thumb before closing the drawer.

Sarah had sent three messages about the coat. Not the raincoat, the other one. In the closet, unless Mom moved it. The dark blue one.

There was only one blue coat in the closet. Sam took a photograph of it anyway.

That one, she answered. Thank you.

He put the notebook inside a paper bag and folded the coat over it. The desk was where he had moved it to make room for the papers. He had finished taking them down on Wednesday, carrying the box into his own bedroom afterward. Pale patches remained on the wall. He had not bought paint.

Laura came to the doorway holding an earring.

“Have you seen the other one?”

Sam glanced at the bare desk. “In here?”

“Anywhere. I thought I put them together last night.”

He followed her to their room. They looked on the dresser, under a scarf, around the lamp. Sam found it caught in the open fold of her handbag and held it up.

“Thank you,” she said. “I was about to change everything I was wearing.”

She put on the earring, checked it in the mirror and turned to him.

“How are you feeling about today?”

He looked at the bag of Sarah’s things by the door.

“I’ve been awake since five.”

“I wondered. You were very quiet.”

“I didn’t want to wake you just because I was thinking about lunch.”

Laura took his hand. “Were you only thinking about lunch?”

“No.”

She stayed beside him. He had rehearsed so much of the conversation during the night that he could barely remember the words Sarah had actually used when she invited him. In his head she had already forgiven him, already refused to, already asked him to go home. He wanted to arrive without making her compete with all those answers.

“I want to see her,” he said. “I’m afraid I’ll spend the whole time trying to find out whether she’s still angry.”

“She probably is.”

Laura ran her thumb over his knuckles. He looked at her, then down at their hands.

“Thank you for looking for the earring,” she said.

He smiled a little. “That I can do.”

“Yes.” She released his hand and picked up her coat. “Tell her I love her. And don’t leave that bag here.”

He brought it downstairs with him.

The road west climbed out of the valley in long grades. Most of the color was off the ridges, the oaks brown and the rest bare. He kept to the right lane behind a flatbed for twenty miles because it saved him deciding anything. At each exit he checked the mirror and saw nothing he could have described afterward. The bag rode on the passenger seat with the coat folded over it. Once, when a truck came by close, he put his hand on it, then took the hand back.

❦

He was in the visitors’ lot fifteen minutes early and parked where he could open his door clear of the neighboring car. He left the bag on the seat while he put on his coat. The lot was nearly empty, a flat square of asphalt with the rows marked in faded paint, and the wind came down off the hills with nothing to slow it. The light was gray and even. Nothing in the lot cast a shadow.

He could see the corner of the library from the lot. He sent Sarah a message saying he was there and would wait by the doors. She did not need to hurry.

He had just lifted the bag from the seat when somebody said his name.

Dambrosky stood at the end of the row. He wore a dark coat and carried a paper cup. He was bareheaded in the cold, his hair short and going gray. He kept the cup in front of him with one hand under it, and he did not shift his feet while Sam looked at him. For a moment Sam expected someone else to appear at his shoulder, a student or a colleague who could explain why he was here.

The man came toward him alone.

“Good morning, Sam.”

Sam shut the car door. “What are you doing here?”

“I was hoping we could talk.”

“You came to her college to talk to me?”

“I understand you’re meeting Sarah for lunch. I won’t keep you long.”

Sam looked past him toward the library. Two students were coming down the steps. Neither was his daughter.

“How do you know I’m meeting her?”

“I’ve come to speak to you, Sam.”

He set the bag on the roof of the car and took out his phone. His fingers were cold enough that he mistyped the name before finding Sarah’s message. He wrote that Dambrosky was in the visitors’ lot and had approached him. He asked her to wait inside and said he would call.

Dambrosky stood a few feet away, holding the cup.

“I haven’t approached your daughter,” he said.

“Don’t. If you want to speak to me, you have my number.”

Dambrosky let the silence last. A car moved slowly down the row behind him. Sam watched it pass, trying to remember if he had noticed it on the drive. The driver stopped at an empty space and began backing in. There was no reason to connect him to the man in front of him. Sam made himself stop looking, and hated that it took an effort.

His phone sounded.

I’m with Tessa. We’re inside. I’m calling the campus office.

He read it twice, then wrote that he was all right and would stay where he was. He did not put the phone away.

“I received Aaron’s warning,” Dambrosky said. “Thank you for asking him to send it.”

“Has your manager checked the problem?”

“His engineers are checking the cases Aaron identified. I’ve asked him to send their findings to Civic Passage.”

“Before your recruiters use the recommendations?”

“They’ve been told which ones need checking. I’m not the engineer, Sam.”

“Aaron can speak to him.” Sam moved so he could see the library and not turn his back. “What do you want from me?”

“I’d like you to consider leading a research group at Delmere.”

Sam looked at him. “I’ve just been fired by an organization you help run.”

“I know. I’m still asking.”

The blue sleeve of Sarah’s coat had slipped over the edge of the bag. Sam tucked it back in.

“You have the model,” he said. “You’ve paid for it. You don’t need me to help you run it.”

“I’ll tell you what you’re for, since you’ll want to know,” Dambrosky said. “You keep what you’re given and you finish what you accept. I read that in your file before I met you, and I’ve watched you do it since. I don’t need you to approve of us. We have the work you did at Civic Passage. I don’t think it’s the last useful thing you’re capable of doing.”

Sam wanted someone else to have said that. Janet, Graham, a stranger replying to an application. He had wanted it badly enough that part of him went on listening, with his daughter’s coat between them and her waiting inside because of this man.

“What would you want me to do?”

“Spend a day with the people working on ADICUS. Let them show you what they’re trying to do. If there’s something you’d like to take further, we can talk about putting a group together.”

A group at Delmere would come with a badge, a desk, and a page signed on the first morning about what stayed inside the building.

“What are they trying to do, Martin?”

Dambrosky glanced at the phone in Sam’s hand. Sam had not lowered it since reading Sarah’s message.

“She knows you’re here,” Sam warned. “She’s called the campus office.”

“Then if someone comes over, I’ll explain why I asked to speak to you.”

Sam looked at Dambrosky’s cup, the coat buttoned against the cold. A man offering a former colleague a job. He could hear how Dambrosky would describe it.

“You could have sent an email.”

“Would you have answered it?”

Sam did not answer.

“My analysts have been going back over what happened before the meeting at Alderwick.”

“Then they know your guards kept Nadia and Amir inside the compound until Vale could hand them to the Guard you arm.”

“Celia stopped that. They left with their lawyer.”

Sam thought of the man Nadia had told him about, the one the Guard had kept. Leila had put him at the end of her message about the driver. His wife no longer went to the gate.

“How long did Jasleen have to keep asking Celia before she let them out?” he demanded. “She hung up on the whole board first.”

A student came between them with a bicycle. Sam stepped closer to his car to let her pass. Dambrosky waited until she had gone.

“That’s what interests me,” Dambrosky said. “You and Jasleen had already asked. By Friday, you had people willing to act together.”

“So next time you’d like to stop us talking.”

“I’d like to know whose advice people want before they agree to work for us.”

“You put James and Jasleen in that assessment. You’d already decided they could persuade me.”

“We thought James might. We didn’t ask who he would want to talk to. My analysts are starting with the people an applicant asks for advice, then looking at who those people turn to.”

Sam had seen that done once, from the back of a vehicle. You stand at a gate with a watch and wait to see who comes. Dambrosky had the cup in one hand and the other in the pocket of his coat. He looked at Sam the way the man with the watch had looked into the second vehicle at the yard.

“And you want me to help you get past the person who tells him not to take the job.”

“Let me show you something else we could do with ADICUS.” Dambrosky did not look away. “A health ministry needs help during an outbreak. Half the staff at one clinic are ill. Before they send a relief team, somebody has to work out which other clinics will be left short if those people go. I’d like ADICUS to help them do that, and keep doing it as the outbreak moves.”

Sam had begun to wonder what information the ministry could give them. He caught himself before asking. Dambrosky bent a little to look at him.

“What?” Sam said.

“You used to go quiet like that in meetings. Then you’d ask to see what the engineers had done.”

“What goes with the relief team?”

“Whatever the ministry needs to keep the clinic open.”

“That’s what the shrine was. My visit to the tomb, and the stop you added on the river. A real mission with your load riding under it. Nobody carrying it was told.”

Dambrosky did not look away.

Sam looked down at Sarah’s bag.

“And you’d want me to help you recruit for the Guard too.”

“We take on work governments ask us to do, Sam. It isn’t all for the Guard.”

“Government approval hasn’t made what they’re doing to those families any better.”

“No. And refusing every other assignment wouldn’t help those families either.”

“The trucks went out again,” Sam said. “The Saturday after Len told a room what was in them.”

“The program didn’t stop,” Dambrosky said.

“On the Sunday one of them took fire south of the port. The driver’s in the hospital.”

“I know his name,” Dambrosky said.

Sam looked over at the library. He could not see Sarah from here.

“If I told you an assignment was going to hurt people, could I stop us taking it?”

“You could bring it to me.”

“And if you disagreed?”

Dambrosky was quiet.

“I would still be responsible for the division,” he allowed. “If we couldn’t agree, I would have to make the decision.”

Sam lowered the phone to his side.

“The day our vehicles came to your yard,” he said, “a girl was killed on the way. Did anyone tell you?”

Dambrosky looked at the cup in his hand. “Your convoy leader reported it when you arrived.”

“And the next load?”

“The following week,” Dambrosky said. “The same road.”

“Past where she died.”

“Yes.”

It was the first answer anyone who had sent that load had given him in nineteen years. There was nowhere to put it.

The phone moved in Sam’s hand. Sarah was calling. He answered at once.

“I’m still in the lot,” he said.

“The officer I spoke to isn’t here today. Someone else is coming to meet us at the student center. Will you come there?”

“Yes. I’ll come now.”

“Are you okay?”

He looked at Dambrosky.

“I am. I’ll be there in a few minutes.”

After ending the call, Sam picked up the bag.

“Will you come and meet the ADICUS team?” Dambrosky asked.

Sam pulled the keys from his pocket and locked the car.

“I’m not taking the job, Martin.”

“I haven’t asked you to decide today.”

“Sarah’s waiting for me. Don’t come here to find me again. If you want to ask me anything, write to me.”

“I’ll write,” Dambrosky agreed. “And since you asked how I knew. I suspended the observation of your wife when I said I did. I didn’t say anything about you.”

Sam put the keys away. Sarah had asked him to come to the student center. He started toward the end of the row.

“Enjoy your lunch with Sarah,” Dambrosky called after him.

Sam looked back. Dambrosky was walking toward the far entrance to the lot. He passed behind a parked van. Sam did not see which car, if any, he approached.

Sam wanted to follow him far enough to find out.

His legs were shaking. He set the bag down and rested a hand on the roof of the car. He waited through a few breaths before picking up the bag.
