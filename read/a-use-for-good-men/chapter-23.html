Chapter Twenty-Three

The Departure

Laura sat on the bottom stair to pull on her boots. Sam was at the front door with her rolled plans. He could see the gray thread where she repaired the hem of her coat, a small crooked line she complained about and never changed.

“I can put those in the car,” he said.

“Thank you. Behind the passenger seat, if there’s room.”

There was room. He moved a tote of ribbon out of the way and laid the plans where they would not unroll under her feet. The passenger seat was damp at one corner from the water bottle she left on it. Sam tightened the cap and put it upright in the holder.

Laura came down the front path, testing the fit of her left boot as she walked.

“You’ve got time to change them,” he said.

“Then I’d have to admit they don’t fit.”

“You admitted that in the shop.”

“I thought they’d stretch.” She opened the driver’s door. “I’ll be back around four. Later if they want to measure the route again.”

He nodded. The day felt nearly over, though the sun hung above the roofs opposite. He had left home that morning expecting to return with his computer. He put a hand to his shirt, where he usually clipped his badge. He had left it on Graham’s desk.

Laura followed his glance. “Do you want me to stay?”

“No. Go do your work.”

“I can make another time if you need me.”

He looked at her. This was when he could tell her he was thinking of going to Don’s that afternoon. He could ask whether she wanted to come, or whether they should speak to Sarah before he left. But he wanted to be alone for the drive, and he did not want to see her face when he said so.

“You should go,” he said again.

She closed the rear door. Before she sat, she put a hand against his chest and kissed him.

“Answer Janet,” she said. “And answer me when I call.”

“I will.”

Sam stepped back so she could leave. She raised two fingers from the steering wheel as she pulled away. He kept to the path until her car passed the corner.

Sarah’s room was warmer than the hall. Sam raised the blind and opened the window an inch. A lawn mower started somewhere beyond the neighboring houses.

His desk lamp was on. He switched it off and set the pen he was carrying in the mug Sarah once used for paintbrushes. Her books leaned against the inscribed brick on the shelf. He touched the top of the anatomy book, then took his hand away.

The wall above the desk had filled since Saturday: the embassy page, Len’s two messages, the sentence from the assessment with his name in it, his own questions down the margins in pencil. He had moved the desk to make room for more of them. He did not look at it long.

He called Sarah.

The call rang long enough for him to picture her looking at the name. He knew she might be in class. When the recorded greeting began, he ended the call before the tone.

He could send an apology. He began one, wrote that he had been afraid after seeing the photograph, and got no further. She had heard all that yesterday, on the bench.

Sam deleted the explanation and wrote that he was sorry he broke that promise. He sent it and asked for no answer.

The computer cables lay loose around the monitor. Sam unplugged the dock and put its cable with it for whoever Aisha sent to collect it. There was nothing else he needed to do before leaving the room.

He did not get up.

From here he could see part of the street through the window. A delivery driver stopped two houses down. The man checked a package against his phone, went to the door and returned to the van. Sam watched until the van left.

Suspended, Dambrosky had said. The photograph of Sarah was older than that word. It made no difference to his eyes. Every unfamiliar car had to be watched to the corner before he could let it go.

If he stayed at home, Laura would return and find him doing this. They would eat, or she would eat while he moved food around his plate. She would ask whether he had answered Janet. He would say he was thinking about it. At some point he would come back upstairs.

He opened the thread with Don and Ellen. Don’s last invitation was still there. A photograph below it showed a lopsided cake with a single candle near the edge. Ellen said Don had begun eating before she found the matches.

Sam smiled, then lowered the phone.

Don was several hundred miles away. He would be at school. Sam could be at the house late that evening if he left soon, and there would be hours before he had to tell anyone what he did to Aaron.

He could also keep driving if he decided not to go there.

Sam stood. He put his black notebook in the work bag, carried it into the hall, and left Sarah’s door open.

In his own room, he put clothes in the small overnight bag. Laura had made the bed before she left and put his pillow back on his side. A plant catalog lay open on her night table with a pencil in the fold. He paused over the drawer Laura shared with him for things they misplaced in the laundry. A pair of her socks lay folded on top of his. He set them on her side of the bed so she would find them.

His phone sounded. Janet had emailed the details of Friday’s examination at Alderwick.

Sam did not open the email. He put the phone in his pocket, zipped the bag and carried it downstairs.

At the kitchen counter, he filled a bottle with water. He took an apple and the crackers Laura bought for the car. He had eaten lunch, but it would be late before he was in South Carolina. He put the food in a grocery sack and set it next to his keys.

He could call Laura. She would probably be standing on the grass with the venue manager, measuring a route she had already measured once. She might sound impatient until she understood what he was saying. Then she would ask him to wait.

He found a sheet of paper in the drawer beside the refrigerator and wrote to Laura that he was going to Don’s:

I have gone to see him. I am safe. I need time without questions. Please do not worry.

Sam read the words again. His hand began to tremble at without questions. Laura had asked him to answer when she called. He had promised her he would.

He set down the pen.

For a moment he heard the same mower through the kitchen window as if it were running beneath the floor. A low, steady note deepened until he could no longer distinguish it from the pressure in his ears.

They will be safer when you go.

He waited without moving. There was no voice in the room. He could see sunlight on the refrigerator handle and a wet circle near the bottle. The mower died. Somewhere outside, a man called for somebody to bring a rake.

Sam wiped the circle from the counter and put the cloth over the tap. He left the note in the center of the table.

Sam returned once to check the back door. He checked the front window too, although he could see the latch was down.

At the car, he opened the route to Calder Gap on his phone. He recognized the names of towns from trips they took years ago, when the children slept across the back seat and Laura made him stop before he wanted to. He chose a rest area well before he would need fuel.

Then he started the engine.

The road south carried him between hills patched with the first color of autumn. The sun was behind him and to the right, low enough to lie along the fields. The long trucks went by in the left lane and rocked the car as they passed. He had the heater on his feet and the window down an inch. The cold air came in across his hands. He watched the mirrors more than he wanted to. A silver car hung behind him through two exits, then passed and went ahead until he could no longer see it.

He could remember the same ordinary passing movement from hundreds of drives. Knowing that did not stop him checking for the car again.

Laura called before the rest area. He looked at her name on the screen, then at the narrow shoulder beside the traffic. He could not talk properly while driving. That was true. He used it until the call ended.

When the rest-area sign appeared, he nearly stayed on the highway. He was making good time. The car was quiet, and if he stopped he would have to look at the phone again.

Sam took the exit.

He parked near a tree and sat until his hands loosened against the wheel. The lot was half full, the light going yellow in the trees, and a man walked a dog along the grass strip with his collar up. His shoulders hurt. He lowered them deliberately, turned off the engine and took the food to a picnic table beyond the vending machines.

Walking helped. He ate the apple in small bites and drank half the water. A family at the next table was trying to divide three sandwiches among four children. The oldest boy announced that he wasn’t hungry, then watched his brother unwrap the largest half.

Sam put the apple core in the sack. He took out his phone.

Laura had sent two messages.

You should have let me say goodbye.

He read that one twice before opening the next.

I spoke to Don. He and Ellen are home tonight. Don will wait up. Please tell him when you expect to arrive, and tell me where you are when you stop.

Sam looked past the screen to the parked cars. The family was moving away from the table, the oldest boy carrying the baby while his mother gathered the wrappers. His brother came back and pressed part of a sandwich into his free hand.

Laura was arranging a welcome for him after he left her a note. He wanted to think that she preferred him gone, that calling Don confirmed something he was right to fear. Her first message remained above the second. He could not make goodbye mean that she wanted to find the house empty.

He opened Don’s number. His thumb stayed above the call button until the screen dimmed. Then he pressed it.

“Sam?” Don said.

“Yes.”

“Where have you got to?”

Sam told him the rest area and the road. His voice sounded strained in his own ears. Don’s had not changed. It was the low, slow voice he had known longer than any other, and there was no hurry in it.

“That’s still a long drive,” Don said. “Do you want to stop overnight?”

“I can come. If tonight’s all right.”

“Tonight’s all right. Ellen has already put sheets on the bed. She says that obliges you to sleep in it eventually.”

Sam pressed his free hand against the table. He looked at the remaining miles on the phone and read Don the arrival time.

“We’ll be here,” Don said. “If you get tired, stop. Call me and I’ll still be here when you start again.”

“Laura told you?”

“She told me you left to come see us. You can tell me the rest when you get here.”

Sam let his head drop back. Don said nothing while he waited.

“I’ll come,” Sam said.

“Good. Now tell Laura where you stopped.”

After the call, Sam sent her the name of the rest area and the arrival time he had given Don. He wrote that Don had answered and he was going there.

He could call her from the table.

Instead he put the phone in his pocket. He gathered the sack, walked back to the car and continued south. The light went while he was still in Virginia. After that there was the road in the headlights and the red lights ahead of him. The hours alone that he had wanted had nothing in them but the sound of the tires.
