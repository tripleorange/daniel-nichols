Chapter Twenty-Five

The Old Friend

Don was standing on the porch when Sam turned into the driveway. He came down the steps while Sam shut off the engine and tried to straighten his left leg under the wheel. The clock on the dashboard read 11:08.

For a moment Sam could do nothing but sit. His neck and shoulders ached after the drive. Beyond the windshield, Don waited with his hands in the pockets of a jacket he had not fastened.

The house was one story of white boards with a porch the width of the front and a single light over the steps. Gravel ran from the road to the porch and gave out in grass. He had come to it through the town a mile back, a crossroads with a church sign lit and a gas station dark. After that there had been nothing on either side but trees until this light showed through them. The road had been climbing since the state line, and the hills were somewhere above the roof in the dark.

Sam opened the door.

“You don’t have to unfold all at once,” Don said.

“I used to fit in this car.”

“You used to get out of it more often, I bet.”

Don offered his hand, and when Sam stood he closed the distance and drew him into a brief embrace. His hair had gone almost entirely gray, and he carried more weight through his shoulders and middle than Sam remembered. Don’s hand lay flat on his back for the length of it, and Sam, a head taller, bent to be held.

A woman was in the open doorway behind him. Sam recognized Ellen from photographs and visits he had given up years ago. Her hair was shorter now and silver at the temples. She was small, in a cardigan buttoned over a flannel shirt, with reading glasses hung from the neck of the cardigan, and she looked him over from the doorway before she said anything, the stiff leg, the stoop, the bag.

“Sam,” she said. “You found us.”

“My phone did most of it.”

“Don’t tell Don. He still gives directions by things that burned down.”

“The store is still there,” Don objected.

“The foundation is there.” She stepped aside. “Come in. There’s soup.”

Sam opened the trunk. Don carried the overnight bag while Sam collected the work bag and the charger from the car. The road beyond the driveway was empty. Sam looked along it before locking the door.

Warm air and the smell of onions met him inside, where the entrance opened onto a kitchen with a saucepan on the stove and school keys lying on a narrow table among the mail. Ellen went back to the stove while Don set the bag at the foot of the stairs.

“Laura called,” Don said.

“I know,” Sam said.

“She said you might change your mind,” Don went on.

“I nearly did.”

“About coming here?”

Sam rubbed the mark the seat belt had left near his collar. He could see the other exits, their green signs appearing beyond the headlights. “I wasn’t sure I should bring this to your house.”

“Laura told us about the photographs,” Don answered. “Ellen and I talked. We asked you to come.”

Ellen lowered the heat under the pan. “Let her know you got here before you lose another hour deciding what to say.”

Sam took out his phone.

I’m at Don and Ellen’s. I’m staying tonight.

He sent it with the location. Don looked into the kitchen while he did it.

“Sent,” Sam said.

“All right,” Don said. “Let’s put your things down.”

In the guest room were a made bed, a folded luggage rack and two framed family photographs on the dresser. In one photograph a much younger Don posed beside a girl in a graduation gown. Sam remembered sending a card. He could not remember what he wrote in it.

“Bathroom across the hall,” Don said. “Clean towels on the chair. The window sticks if you open it too far.”

His phone sounded before he could plug it in.

I’m glad you are safe.

He read it again. Glad he had made it to the house. Glad he was off the road.

Safely away from her.

The last meaning arrived with the calm of a correction.

He saw her in the kitchen doorway with her hand flat on the frame, and the door upstairs she had asked him to leave open.

“Did she answer?” Don asked.

Sam looked up. Don stood in the doorway with one hand against the frame.

“She said, ‘I’m glad you are safe.’”

“Good.”

Sam kept looking at the message.

“What did you tell her before you left?”

“I left a note.”

Don waited.

“I said I was safe. That I needed time without questions. I asked her not to worry.”

“You asked for no questions.”

“Yes.”

“I had a boy last year who left a note on the kitchen table and walked to his grandmother’s. Four miles. His mother read it to me over the phone. She’d read it about forty times by then.”

“What did it say?”

“That he was fine and not to come after him.” Don dropped his hand from the frame and went ahead of him into the kitchen. “She said it was the not coming after him she couldn’t get past.”

Sam put the phone in his pocket. He heard relief in Laura’s words. He could not stop hearing the other thing as well.

In the kitchen, Ellen placed a bowl at the table and pushed a glass of water over to his chair. Don cut bread over a board, trying to catch the crumbs with his other hand.

“Have you eaten?” Sam asked them.

“Hours ago,” Ellen said. “Sit.”

“You should have gone to bed,” Sam noted.

“And left you to find the bowls?” Don brought the bread to the table. “We’d never sleep.”

Sam sat. The soup was thick with potatoes, tomatoes, beans and soft pieces of carrot. He took the first spoonful too quickly and let it cool in his mouth. Ellen turned off the burner, and Don sat opposite him with a piece of bread.

“Were the roads all right?” Ellen asked.

“Mostly. My back made the second half longer.”

Ellen took the chair at the end of the table. Don was spreading butter with more attention than the bread required.

Sam ate several spoonfuls before he could put the spoon down. The water glass was nearly empty. Ellen filled it without asking and left the pitcher at his elbow.

“How much did Laura tell you?”

“That someone working for your company’s partner followed her,” Don told him. “That they photographed Sarah too. That you haven’t been sleeping. She said you needed somewhere to stop for a while.”

“She didn’t say I was difficult?”

“She knows we met you before she did,” Ellen said.

Sam smiled, then lowered his eyes to the bowl.

Don pushed the butter toward Ellen. “You remember the hearse.”

Sam looked up.

“My father’s,” Don said to Ellen, though she had heard it. “He needed a truck and couldn’t afford one, and the funeral home in town was selling theirs. He drove it home with the back full of fence posts and Sam and me on the front seat, forty miles an hour on the highway because he was afraid of what it would do over fifty. There were cars behind us to the county line. Nobody would pass a hearse.”

“They thought there was somebody in it,” Sam said.

“There were fence posts in it. One man pulled off at the gas station and took his hat off.” Don shook his head. “My father waved to him.”

Ellen had heard it. She laughed anyway, and Sam found that he had too.

Don put down the bread. “Is the man still having them watched?”

“He told me he’d suspended it. He wouldn’t say permanently.”

“And Laura knows that?” Don asked.

“Yes.” Sam turned the glass between his palms. “She and Sarah have been talking to people who can help them. I was supposed to talk with them.”

“Today?” Don asked.

Sam nodded.

Neither Don nor Ellen spoke. From somewhere down the hall came the hum and click of an appliance switching off.

“I thought if I went away, they might stop paying attention to her,” Sam said.

“Did you ask Laura what she thought of that?” Don asked.

“No.”

Don looked at him. “She still wanted it to be you who called her.”

Sam reached for the spoon again, though the bowl was empty. He set it down carefully enough to hear the metal touch the rim.

“They fired me this morning,” he said.

“Laura told me,” Don said.

“Aaron is the best engineer I’ve worked with. He had the model ready to test with Delmere’s people this morning, and I put his old program in place of the new one so it would fail. I wanted a day. He asked me if I’d changed something, and I told him. By ten my computer was on Graham’s desk.”

Don’s chair creaked as he leaned forward.

“I’d already told the Delmere man which of his engineers was helping me,” Sam continued. “I promised I wouldn’t identify him. They’ve taken him off the project.”

“Is that the same man whose program you changed?” Don asked.

“No. Two different men.” Sam looked at the bread between them. “They both tried to help.”

Ellen picked up the butter dish and put it nearer Sam. He did not reach for it.

“They still want me at Alderwick on Friday,” he went on. “That’s where the model is. The directors watch Delmere use it, then vote on it.”

“Will you go?” Don asked.

“I haven’t answered.” Sam picked up his phone. The invitation was there, below the messages he had read on the road. “They asked me to answer today.”

“Tell them you’ve seen it,” Don suggested.

Sam opened Janet’s message and began a reply.

I should have answered today. I’m too tired to decide. I’ll call tomorrow.

He sent it and put the phone down by his plate.

“You can sleep now,” Don said.

“It doesn’t stop because I’m asleep.”

“You’ve driven all day,” Don insisted. “Whatever it is will keep until morning. It’s kept this long.”

Sam let go of the glass and put both hands flat on the table.

“In Iraq a contractor put mortar rounds on the back of our convoy for the men he’d armed on the river,” Sam said. “I rode two vehicles in front of them, and I prayed over the hood before we left. That night it came down on the streets we’d driven through. Four men had walked to the gate that afternoon to ask after a child. They shot them behind the yard, with the old man from the shrine, and told the town they were insurgents. The old man had given me a brick from the tomb. It holds up Sarah’s books.” He stopped. “The man who ran that is at Delmere now, and he’s doing it again on a coast in Meredan. The ammunition rides in the relief trucks, and the drivers find out what they’ve been carrying when it’s too late to put it down. He used my shrine to get his truck across the river. He used my prayer. Now he wants my model to find him the next men, and he said so the way a man reads out a lease. I want him stopped, Don. I don’t know what I’d do with the rest of my life if I let it go on with my name on it.”

Ellen’s hand went still on the butter dish.

Don nodded once. He did not ask whether either force had a good reason.

Sam looked toward the dark kitchen window. “There was a girl killed on a movement in Iraq. The vehicle I was in struck her. Before we left, I asked what the Marines were supposed to do at an added stop. Somebody told me the right officers had looked at it.”

Don said nothing.

“I believed him.”

The refrigerator began to run. Sam could see the driver’s hands against the wheel, and the bottle he had set on the ground between them afterward, still capped. He moved his own hands into his lap.

“Have you told Laura about the girl?” Don asked.

“Some of it. Last week.”

“After all this time?”

Sam nodded.

Don sat with him while Ellen carried the saucepan to the sink. Water ran against the metal, then shut off. She kept her back to them, both hands on the edge of the sink.

“I can’t let it happen again,” Sam said.

“Which part?” Don asked.

Sam opened his mouth. He could have named the model or the five men by the river, the child, the people he had failed to protect this week. For a moment he could not choose one.

“I don’t know.”

Don pushed back his chair. “Come on. You can tell me more tomorrow.”

Ellen lifted Sam’s bowl before he could take it and carried it to the sink.

Nobody asked him to finish the argument he had started.

On the short wall between the kitchen and the guest-room hall, family photographs surrounded a wooden plaque. Don and Ellen appeared at graduations and picnic tables while the same younger faces grew older around them. A household calendar hung below the photographs. The plaque read:

Act justly. Love mercy. Walk humbly with your God.—Micah 6:8

Sam paused long enough to read it. A dental appointment was written on the calendar below. Someone had crossed out a dinner and moved it to the following square. He had seen the words on much larger walls.

Don opened the linen cupboard and handed him another towel.

“I have bus loop in the morning,” he said. “The assistant principal and I trade arrival duty. Tomorrow is mine.”

“What time?”

“I leave at five forty-five.”

Sam checked the kitchen clock.

“You can sleep,” Don offered. “Ellen will be here. Or you can come with me and sit somewhere with bad coffee while I work. You won’t be helping with students.”

“I didn’t drive this far to sit in your house.”

“At five forty-five, you may discover a deep respect for my house.”

Sam pressed the towel against his chest. “I’ve been absent too long.”

Don rested a hand on his shoulder, lightly enough that it did not hurt. “You’re here tonight.”

The shower loosened Sam’s neck, and he stayed under the water until he could turn his head without moving his shoulders. When he came out, the kitchen light was off and a small lamp lit the hall.

He plugged in the phone. There was no second message from Laura.

He set the alarm for 4:45, then changed it to five. Don had offered him a bed. He lay down in it with the phone beside his hand and the lamp on. The sheets Ellen had put on smelled of the line. Through the wall Don’s voice came low, then Ellen’s, then neither.
