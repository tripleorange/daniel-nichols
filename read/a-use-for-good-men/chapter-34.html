Chapter Thirty-Four

Someone to Stay

Sam wiped his face on a corner of his shirt. His breathing had eased, but the shaking had moved into his legs. He told Don that, partly because he wanted to hear himself explain something solid that wouldn’t push him over the edge.

He looked at the floor between the bed and the door. The box would be in the way. He pushed it with one foot until there was room to walk past.

“I didn’t mean to frighten you,” he said.

“You called me because you needed somebody. I’m glad you did.”

He picked at a loose thread on the blanket.

“I don’t know what to say now.”

“You don’t need to keep talking.”

For a while they listened to each other. A vehicle passed outside.

“Tell me about school,” Sam said.

“Today?”

“Anything.”

Don took a breath.

“They found a sweater in the ceiling above the boys’ bathroom. No one’s claiming it.”

Sam looked up.

“How did it get there?”

“We have several accounts. One involves a very strong draft.”

Sam made a sound that might have become a laugh if his throat had not hurt. Don went on about the ceiling tile, which would have to be replaced, and the custodian who wanted the sweater’s owner to see how long that took. Sam listened. There were boys at a school in South Carolina who had managed to lose a sweater overhead. Don would be there tomorrow, and the sweater would probably be on his desk.

He heard a car door outside, then Laura’s key. She called his name from the hall.

“Up here,” he called back.

She came into the room carrying her phone. Her coat was open, and there was a damp mark on one sleeve where she had brushed against something.

“They’re on their way,” she said. “Don?”

“I’m here.”

She sat beside Sam and put one hand between his shoulders. He had expected her to ask what he had done. Instead she asked whether he was hurting anywhere.

“Just tight. Here.” He touched the base of his throat. “It’s better than it was.”

“All right.”

He looked at her phone, then at her coat.

“Your client—”

“I told her I had to go.”

“I’m sorry.”

“We’ll deal with that later.” She moved her hand slowly over his back. “I’m here now.”

Don said he would let them go. Sam reached for the phone too quickly and almost knocked it off the bed.

“Can you stay until they come?”

“Of course.”

Laura left her hand where it was. Sam sat between the pressure of it and Don’s voice until someone knocked downstairs.

❦

At the hospital, a nurse put his folded coat under the chair while he answered questions. Laura stood near the foot of the bed with her coat on and her bag against her chest. The bay was a bed, a chair and a curtain on a rail, with a gap at the end where people went past. They had taken his shirt and given him a gown that tied behind. His feet in their socks came past the end of the mattress. They had asked Sam whether he wanted her there, and he said yes before she could offer to wait outside.

By the time the paramedic had talked him into coming, he could speak in full sentences and had begun to feel foolish, which was easier for a few minutes than feeling afraid.

He turned the plastic band on his wrist until he could read his name.

The nurse asked when the breathing trouble started. Sam tried to work backward from the paper falling off the wall. Laura checked the time of Don’s call and gave it to him.

“Before that,” Sam corrected. “I sat down for a while before I called. I don’t know how long.”

He described the heat, the shaking and his trouble getting air. The nurse asked about medication, the last time he had eaten, and who his doctor was.

Laura supplied the name of the practice when he could remember the doctor’s name but not the office.

After the nurse left, Sam leaned back. Laura pulled the chair nearer.

“Don’s going home,” she said. “He’ll call later.”

“He should have left an hour ago.”

“He’s leaving now.”

Sam watched her remove a leaf from the cuff of her coat. She folded it into a tissue and put it in her pocket.

“What did he tell you?”

“That you couldn’t breathe properly and that you were alone. That he was worried.”

“Nothing else?”

“He said you’d been talking. He didn’t tell me what about.”

Sam looked toward the curtain. Beyond it, a man was asking where his shoes had gone. Another voice assured him they were in the bag.

“I started telling him something I haven’t known how to tell you.”

Laura rested her hands in her lap.

“You can tell me now. Or when we’re somewhere else.”

He wanted the second choice, and for once he knew why. At home there would be dishes, calls and whatever work she had left unfinished to come for him. He could let the evening fill up until it seemed considerate not to begin.

“I keep coming back to the girl,” he said. “I told you about her, and I think I made it sound as if that was what I’d been keeping from you all these years.”

“Was it?”

“Part of it.”

His throat tightened. He willed the sensation to pass before going on.

“There were so many other people who needed a chaplain. People who came looking for me, and people somebody asked me to go and see. Sometimes I could help. Sometimes all I could do was go back the next day.”

Laura stayed still. He looked down at the blanket, trying to find a way to say more that did not begin one of those stories.

“I remember how tired I was. How hard I worked not to let anybody see it when they came over. They already thought they were bothering me. I didn’t want them looking at my face and deciding they ought to come back another time.”

“You came home tired,” Laura said. “Even after you’d been home for months.”

He nodded.

“I thought it would wear off.”

“So did I.”

“There are things I know that I can’t tell you. I can’t tell Don either. I don’t want you thinking I called him because he gets to hear what you don’t.”

“I understand that.”

“I used to think I could explain enough if I was careful. Leave out the names. Change some detail.” He shook his head. “But I would still be giving you something somebody asked me to keep. And once I started trying to decide which part I could say, I couldn’t talk at all.”

Laura looked at him, then down at her hands.

“I wasn’t waiting for you to give me their stories. Sometimes I was waiting for you to tell me you didn’t want to go out. Or that you hadn’t slept. I could see you standing in the kitchen, and you’d tell me you were thinking about work. Then you’d go upstairs, and I wouldn’t know whether to follow you.”

Sam remembered the light beneath the office door. He had heard her on the stairs more often than she knew. Sometimes he had kept typing until she went back down.

“I didn’t want to start talking and find I couldn’t stop,” he said. “You’d ask how I was, and I’d spoil the whole evening. Then the next one.” He looked at her. “I kept waiting until I could come downstairs and be good company.”

Laura moved the chair closer until her knee touched the bed.

“I’m frightened too,” she said. “I was at somebody’s house, talking about a border along her wall, and Don called because you couldn’t breathe. I didn’t know what I’d find when I got home.”

“I’m sorry.”

“I know.”

She was holding the edge of her sleeve tightly enough to bunch it in her fist.

“I want you with me,” Laura said. “I wanted you with me when you were just tired, when I thought that was all it was. But I can’t keep guessing whether I should leave you alone or come through the door. I need someone besides me to help you with this.”

He nodded, then looked away. Agreeing was easy in a hospital bed. Tomorrow there would be a number to call and a stranger who would ask him to begin.

“I’m afraid they’ll need the stories before they can help,” he said.

“Then tell them what you’ve just told me. Ask them.”

A doctor drew back the curtain and introduced herself. Laura moved her chair up against the bed.

❦

They stayed for several hours. Late in the evening, the doctor sat on a stool next to him. The results had not shown an urgent physical cause for the episode, she said. She asked how he had been sleeping. He told her he had lost his job, and she asked if the trouble had begun then.

“No,” he said. “It’s been longer than that.” He told her he had served as a Navy chaplain with Marines in Iraq, and that what people had said to him there was not his to repeat. He wanted her help. He did not know how to answer if she asked what he was remembering. She asked instead what happened to him when he tried to sleep.

“I keep thinking I haven’t finished something,” he said. “I know I’m in bed. I know where I am. But I feel as if I’ve gone home and left somebody waiting.”

“Does it help to have Laura with you?”

He looked at her.

“Yes. I haven’t made it easy for her to stay.”

The doctor asked for a few minutes alone with him, and Laura went out past the curtain. The doctor asked Sam about his safety. When she asked if he wanted Laura back, he said yes.

Before they left, the doctor gave him the number of a mental health clinic to call in the morning, and his regular practice’s.

“What if it happens again tonight?”

“Then you come back. Don’t wait for those appointments.”

He held out the page. “Would you circle the one I should call first?”

She circled the number for his regular practice.

Outside, the air was cold. He stood beside the car while Laura unlocked it, his coat loose at the neck because he disliked anything close to his throat.

“Can we get something to eat?” Laura asked.

“Yes.”

“You must be hungry,” he said.

“I am.”

She drove to a place still serving sandwiches. It was almost empty, and a boy behind the counter had started turning the chairs up onto the tables at the far end. He sat with her while she ate, then found he could eat most of his own. The polish on her right thumb had chipped away near the bed of the nail. She ate the way she ate on a site, fast and looking at nothing. When she saw him watching she slowed down.

When she finished, he gathered their wrappers while she looked for her keys.

❦

Don called while Sam was sitting on the side of the bed at home. Laura had gone downstairs to get water. Sam answered and told him what the doctor had said, keeping the paper open on his knee.

“Will you make the calls?”

“Yes.”

“Do you want me to call you afterward?”

“I’d like that.”

They settled on the afternoon, when the students had left.

“I thought coming to see you had helped,” Sam said.

“I thought it had too.”

“Then I come home and do this.”

Don was quiet.

“You called me, Sam. Last Tuesday I heard from Laura before I heard from you.”

Sam glanced at the doorway. He could hear Laura setting a glass down in the hall.

“I liked having you there,” Don went on. “Ellen did too. I’d rather you hadn’t had such a hard time tonight, but I don’t regret the visit. Do you?”

“No.”

He was exhausted, but the thought of ending the call made him sit straighter. There would be hours in which nobody was speaking, and he would still know everything he knew now.

“I don’t want to lie awake beside her again,” he admitted. “Once she’s asleep, I’ll start thinking about all of it. I know I need to make the calls tomorrow, but there’s still tonight.”

“You can call me again,” Don said. “You don’t have to wait until tomorrow afternoon.”

Don had a school to open in the morning.

“Did you ever get somebody to look at that tree?”

Don laughed softly.

“Saturday. Ellen made the appointment.”

“Good. I thought you were going to ask me to help.”

“No,” Don said. “I want you available for my calls.”

Sam laughed. He could hear Don laughing too.

After they said good night, Sam set the phone with the medical papers. Laura came in with the water and handed him a glass.

“I texted the children,” she said. “I told them you’d had a difficult evening and had been checked at the hospital. That we were home together.”

He looked up.

“They’re going to worry.”

“I know. They’d worry more if they heard it later and thought we’d hidden it from them.”

Sam picked up his phone. Michael had written first: Say the word and I’ll drive down tonight. Peter had sent a heart and then, a minute later, Dad? Sam looked at the two of them for a while before he answered that he was all right and that they should sleep. There was a message from Sarah asking if he was home now.

He wrote that he was, that her mother was there and he would be getting more help. He started to add that she did not need to worry, then deleted it.

I’m glad you’re with Mom, she wrote back. I’ve got class early. Can I call tomorrow?

Yes. Whenever you’re free.

He put the phone down. There were things Sarah had left to say about his visit to her college. He did not want this night to become the reason she never said them.

Laura took his empty glass.

“I’ll leave my phone on,” he said.

“Good.”

She got into bed. He lay beside her with the lamp lit, waiting for his body to believe there was nothing else he had to get up and do.

❦

In the morning, Sam sat at the kitchen table while Laura spoke to her client outside. She had taken her notebook with her. Through the window, he watched her write something on the page and cross it out, then laugh briefly at something the woman said.

His usual doctor’s office offered him an appointment on Monday. The clinic’s line went to a recorded message. He left his name and set the phone on the table where he could see the screen.

Laura came back in and laid the notebook on the table.

“We’re moving the plants on Monday,” she said. “She was fine about it.”

“I have an appointment Monday.”

“What time?”

“Ten.”

She opened her notebook again. “I’ll need to move the collection to the afternoon.”

He looked at the page where she had just written the delivery time. “I want you there. I don’t want you to have to keep putting things off because of me.”

“I’m moving the collection to the afternoon, Sam. The client doesn’t mind.” She turned back to the notebook. “I’d like to come with you.”

He nodded. She wrote the appointment in with the other things she needed to do.

Sam’s phone began to ring. The number was local and unfamiliar. He looked at the medical papers, then answered while Laura sat across from him.

Sam turned over the hospital page as the woman from the clinic gave him Tuesday afternoon. Laura slid the notebook across the table.

“I can come then,” he said.

He wrote the appointment under Tuesday in Laura’s notebook. Now there were two places expecting him.

“Would you like me to take you to that one too?”

“Can you?”

“I can make Tuesday afternoon work.”

“Yes,” he said. “I’d like that.”

She left the notebook open between them.

❦

The email from Aaron arrived just before lunch. Aaron had warned Delmere’s technical manager that the model could mix up two jobs a person had worked at the same time, and attached the reply so Sam could see the warning had arrived.

Sam read the message twice. It did not tell him what the engineers had found or if anyone had changed the software. He began a reply asking Aaron to find out, then broke off with both hands over the keyboard.

Sam deleted the unfinished reply and began again.

Thank you for making sure Delmere heard about it. How are you getting on with the correction at Civic Passage?

He read it once before pressing send.

Don called that afternoon, as he had promised. Sam took the folder of hospital papers into the living room and sat with it on his knees.

Sam gave him the dates of the appointments, then admitted that he had done very little else.

A message from Len arrived after the call. His lawyer had nothing in writing from Delmere. He had told Rusk he was done. The trucks had gone on Saturday without him. The cargo supervisor had let him know. He would be back on the fleet desk Monday. Denise said to eat something.

Sam wrote back that he had, and that he was glad about Monday. He read Len’s message again.

James had written as well, that morning. He was at Delmere, on other work, and he had given the lawyer his account. The last line was for Laura. Sam carried the phone downstairs and read it to her, and she said James could tell her the rest himself.

Janet forwarded a message from Leila that evening. On Sunday a program truck had been fired on south of the port, on the road the relief took. The driver had been hit through the door and was in the hospital in the capital. He was a man Amir had worked alongside. Nobody had fired on a relief truck on that road before. The man the Guard had kept when Amir went to ask about the families had not come home. His wife had given up going to the gate.

Sam read it on the stairs, halfway up, with one hand on the rail. He read it again, and then he went down to the kitchen and told Laura what it said, all of it, before she asked.

❦

Sarah called after dinner, while Sam was helping Laura clear the table. He dried his hands before answering.

“Did I wake you?”

“No. We’ve just eaten.”

“Is Mom there?”

“Beside me. Would you like—”

“Can I talk to you for a minute?”

He turned to Laura and mouthed Sarah’s name, then carried the phone into the living room.

“Of course. Go ahead.”

Sarah was quiet. He could hear people near her, then a door closing.

“Mom said you were having trouble breathing. I didn’t know what that meant. I kept reading it and it didn’t—” She stopped. “What happened?”

“I couldn’t take a full breath. Sitting down didn’t help. That’s when I called Don.” He sat down and put his free hand on his knee. “It was easier by the time we got to the hospital.”

“What about now?”

“I’m breathing all right now. I don’t want to go through that again, Sare.”

“Are you going back?”

“My doctor on Monday, and a clinic the hospital gave me on Tuesday.”

Sarah was quiet again. From the kitchen came the sound of the radio. Laura was running water into the sink.

“I was going to ask Tessa to drive me home,” Sarah said.

“Last night?”

“Yes. Then Mom said you were coming home together. I didn’t know if you wanted me there.”

“I would have liked to see you.”

“I wanted to see you too.”

“Your mother stayed with me at the hospital,” he said. “I was glad she was there. But I didn’t want you having to come home to look after me.”

“I wasn’t going to look after you, Dad. I just wanted to know what was happening.”

Sam looked at the folder next to him.

“I should have called you.”

“You texted. I was glad you did.”

A voice called to her from farther away. Sarah answered, too far from the phone for him to make out the words.

“We’re going to get dinner,” she told him. “Somewhere with plates. Tessa says we need to remember how.”

He smiled. “You’d better go, then.”

“Wait. I was going to ask if you could come up next Saturday. We could have lunch.”

He looked at the two appointment dates on his folder. He would have gone in the morning if she had asked.

“The twenty-ninth?”

“Yes. I’ve got things tomorrow. Is that okay?”

“Yes. I’d like to come.”

“Could Mom come too?”

“You can ask her. Hold on.”

He took the phone back to the kitchen. Laura dried her hands and pulled her notebook closer.

“I’d like that,” she said. “But I’ve got a consultation that morning. Go without me. We’ll find another day.”

She handed the phone back, and Sam took it into the hall.

“Can we still do lunch?” Sarah asked. “Just us?”

“Of course we can. Where would you like to go?”

“There’s a place off campus. Meet me outside the library at twelve fifteen and we can walk over. I’ve got to meet some friends at three, though.”

“We’ll have lunch. Then I’ll get out of your way.”

“Dad.”

He leaned against the wall.

“I’m not trying to get rid of you,” she said. “I’ve already made plans with them.”

“I know. I meant—” He stopped. From the kitchen he could hear Laura putting away the plates. “Lunch sounds good.”

“Good. I’ll send you a map. I’ve really got to go now.”

“Go and eat. I’ll see you next Saturday.”

“Bye, Dad.”

“Bye, Sare.”

He stayed in the hall after the call ended. Laura called to offer him tea.

“Yes,” he called. “I’ll come and make it.”

❦

On Monday, Laura drove him to the doctor’s office. In the car afterward, she asked if he wanted to go straight home. He said he did. A few minutes later, when she passed a bakery, he asked if they could stop.

She pulled into the lot, and they went inside together. Sam picked up the bread she liked, then joined her by the cakes.

“Which one would you like?”

“The one you never buy because you think it’s too much for two people,” she said.

“It is quite large.”

“We’ll do our best.”

That afternoon, Laura came downstairs with her keys to go and collect the plants.

“Will you be back for dinner?”

“Yes. Unless something goes wrong with the collection.”

“I’ll make something.”

Laura stopped with her keys in her hand. “Something we can eat with rice?”

He looked at the cupboard and laughed.

“For the foreseeable future.”

She kissed him before she left.

❦

At the clinic on Tuesday, afternoon sunlight fell across a faded sofa under the window. The woman who came out for him was about Laura’s age, in a gray sweater with the sleeves pushed up. She took the chair and left the sofa to him. When the woman sat down, he began explaining why he could not tell her what the men in Iraq had said to him.

He told her about calling Don, about how close he had come to hanging up before his friend answered. She asked what it had been like to hear him.

“Once he’d answered, I didn’t want to let him go,” he said. “I kept thinking about him getting on with his evening, and me still sitting there.”

There were other questions he had trouble answering. When she asked if he would try again, he said he would rather stop for today.

Before he stood, she told him what the work would be. He would write an account of the worst day, not what happened but what he had told himself about it since, and they would read it together and see which parts were true. It would take weeks. He could keep every confidence he had ever been given and still do it, she said, because the account was his own.

“I know which parts,” Sam said.

“Then you’re ahead of most people.”

He did not say them. They were in the room anyway.

You did nothing. You put God’s name on it.

He had lived under those two sentences for nineteen years without hearing them in another person’s voice, and now a woman in a clinic was proposing to read them off a page.

He came home with a headache and another date written on a card.

That evening Laura asked how it had been. He said he had spent the first part explaining to the woman what he could not tell her, and she had said that was fine, and then he had run out of things he could say.

“She wants me to write down what I’ve told myself about that day. Then we’ll see which parts are true.”

“Do you know which parts?”

“Some.” He turned his cup on the table. “One of them is about you. After I called your client. That you were afraid of me.”

Laura put down the cloth she was holding.

“I was never afraid of you,” she said. “I was angry. I’m still angry about that call. It was never fear.”

He faced her. He had carried the other version since that lunch. It had not once occurred to him to ask.

“Write that one down first,” Laura said.
