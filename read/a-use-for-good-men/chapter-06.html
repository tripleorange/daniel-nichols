Chapter Six

The Rain Plan

The first walkway panel was halfway out of the pickup when Laura changed where it was going.

“Can you set it on the pavement?” Laura glanced back at her notes.

Sam eased the panel down at the edge of the parking lane. The other three were left strapped in the truck bed. “Yesterday you said the arbor.”

“Yesterday I had a lawn.”

The storm had left the grass dark and polished. The route the guests were supposed to take ran from the parking lane down a gentle slope to a white arbor near the barn. Water filled the tire ruts by the service gate. Each time Laura pressed the heel of her boot into the planned aisle, mud rose around the leather.

To their right, a level gravel apron began beneath a stand of cedars and continued as far as the reception tent. A strip of low ground separated it from the pavement.

The restored farm’s barn rose above them, its doors open to the morning. Three planters Laura had set by the arbor the previous afternoon were in place: two pots of tall grass and a Japanese maple. The air smelled of wet soil and diesel.

Laura pushed a narrow soil probe into the aisle. It sank nearly to the handle.

“That seems conclusive,” Sam said.

“It might dry by Saturday,” Laura allowed. “I’m not counting on it.”

The venue manager crossed from the barn, a small woman in a tan raincoat carrying a clipboard sealed inside a clear plastic sleeve. Her shoes were clean. She stopped where the pavement ended.

“The sun’s out,” the manager said. “By Saturday, this will be fine.”

“Saturday is in two days,” Laura said.

Laura pulled the probe free and pointed to the lawn between them. “The bride’s mother uses a cane. She would have to cross this.”

“The family can help her,” the manager said.

“I’m not asking them to carry her over ground we already know is unstable.” Laura wiped the probe on the grass and put it away.

She pointed across the low strip toward the cedars. “Start the first panel here, Sam. Square to the pavement.”

Sam lifted the panel he had set down and placed it where she pointed. Laura knelt at the outer edge and pressed a rubber leveling pad under one corner until it no longer rocked.

Together they joined the four panels end to end. The line ran across the worst of the low ground but ended several yards short of the gravel.

Laura stood at the last panel and faced the trees. Beneath them, the branches made a dark ceiling over a level space.

“The arbor goes there,” she said.

“The couple paid for the lawn,” the manager objected. “My crew would have to move every chair.”

“They paid for an outdoor ceremony,” Laura said. If the lawn won’t carry them, I need to show them what will. They can decide whether they want it here.”

The manager looked at Sam. His hands rested on the last panel, and he said nothing.

Laura walked to the arbor and pointed to the planters. “Bring me the maple first.”

Sam carried it to the cedars, then went for the grasses. Laura arranged the three pots in the shade of the trees and took photographs.

Laura measured the gap between the walkway and the gravel and priced the extra sections with the rental company. She wrote the three o’clock delivery time on the back of her hand, then sent the price, photographs and revised sketch to the couple. She did all of it standing in the mud in her boots, the phone at her ear, the pen in her teeth between numbers. She never looked up to see what the manager made of it.

“You might lose the job,” Sam said.

“I might,” Laura agreed.

“Or the venue’s next one,” Sam said.

Laura looked over the cedar space. “Then I lose it. I’m not sending her mother across that lawn.”

She called the client. The manager was close enough to hear.

“Hi,” Laura said. “I’ve sent you pictures from the venue. The lawn won’t hold the traffic we discussed. I’m recommending we move the ceremony under the cedars and extend a temporary walkway from the parking lane.”

Laura listened, then smiled once.

“Good,” she said. “I’ll confirm the delivery.”

She ended the call and confirmed the rental. The manager wrote something on her clipboard and went to speak to the kitchen staff.

“That sounded like relief,” Sam said.

Laura put the phone in her pocket. “Mine too.”

The four panels and the plants stayed where they were. Sam loaded her tool tote into the pickup against a bundle of unused sign stands lashed in the bed. The mud on his boots had dried into a pale crust.

They drove home with the folded stands rattling behind them.

Laura drove. She had the window down and her elbow on the sill. She talked about the cedars, how the light came through them at ten and where it would be at four. Sam watched the road. A hawk sat on a fence post and did not move for the truck. He nodded when she paused. He was still in the office in Reston, with the bottle of water and Dambrosky straightening the frame. Each time she turned to him he had to come back from it. A few miles from home she stopped talking and drove the rest of the way with both hands on the wheel.

At home, he carried the stands into the garage. The garage had been hers for years: pots in stacks by size, stakes in a barrel, bags of soil on a pallet, and his own tools in one drawer at the end of her bench. The door from it led to the kitchen. The kitchen opened to the dining room and the table where she worked at night. Laura set the muddy tool tote by the door.

“Not in the house,” she said.

“I had not planned to bring mud into the kitchen.”

“You have brought stranger things into the kitchen.”

“Name one.”

“The brick.”

“The brick was useful.”

“So is a doormat.”

He left his boots beside the stands and looked at his phone. James had sent two questions about the software that joined his model to ADICUS, and none about Dambrosky.

By six that evening Sam had answered both. When he came downstairs, Laura was at the dining table with a box of ribbon and the small yellow measuring tape. She had washed her hands, but a blue smudge remained where she had written the delivery time.

“Did the walkway arrive?”

“It did. The manager sent pictures, and I went back to check. Her crew had already moved the chairs.” Laura drew a length of green ribbon across the table. “She asked if she could use my photographs on their website.”

“Will you let her?”

“Once she’s spoken to the couple. With my business name.”

Sam held the ribbon where it wanted to curl. Laura cut it and laid it next to the others.

“Have you eaten?” Laura asked.

“Not since breakfast.”

Laura pointed at the kitchen counter, where a dish towel covered a plate. One full sandwich and one half lay under it. “The other half was mine. Bring it over.”

He brought the plate to the table.

“I can help with these,” he offered.

“I’d appreciate that.” She settled beside him and pulled the box closer. “The ends need to match. One of these is supposed to be the example.”

“They all look the same.”

“Thank you. That took forty minutes.”

He lifted the shortest piece and set it by his plate. Laura handed him the tape, then pulled another spool from the box. He liked sitting close enough to feel her chair move when she reached for something.

“I could bring the computer down when we finish,” he said. “If you’re going to be working all evening.”

“I’m trying not to be.”

“I can work here. You don’t have to wait for me.”

Laura put the spool on the table. “Do you want to work, or do you want me to sit with you?”

“There are some things I need to go through after yesterday.”

“Then go through them.” She picked the spool up again. “There’s a box of these to finish by Friday, and the ends have to match. If you’re down here, you can hold. If you’re upstairs, you’re upstairs.”

He laid the tape across the ribbon. The end had slipped. He measured it again before answering.

“Dambrosky, the man I met at Delmere yesterday, talked about the Marines as if it were perfectly reasonable to use them without telling them what the work was for. I keep thinking of things I should have asked him.”

Laura waited with her hands on the unopened spool.

“I should have stayed until he answered,” he went on.

“Would he have answered?”

Sam looked at the ribbon beneath his hand. “I don’t know.”

She took a drink of water. He expected another question about the meeting. She set the glass down and waited instead.

“I don’t know how to leave it alone,” he admitted. “I can finish what’s in front of me, but then I go back over what he said. I was doing it in the truck this morning. You were talking to me about the arbor, and I was still in that room with him.”

“I wondered where you’d gone.” Laura rested her forearms on the table. “I know there are things from Iraq you can’t tell me. Tell me when it’s this hard to be here. I spent the drive guessing whether you were unhappy with me or tired or waiting for me to stop talking. I’m not doing that again.”

“I wasn’t waiting for you to stop.”

“I’m glad.” She looked at him. “I still don’t know what you’re asking me to do tonight.”

“I want company,” he said. “I don’t want to go upstairs by myself.”

Laura reached for his hand. There was a small cut along her thumbnail, and he turned her hand to look at it.

“The grasses,” she said. “I caught it on the pot.”

“Does it hurt?”

“Only when you do that.”

He loosened his grip, and she left her hand in his.

“I’d like company too,” she said. “I’ve been looking forward to finishing this and sitting down with you. We can have that much of an evening.”

“I need to speak to James tomorrow,” he said. “About who decides what the advisers will actually do.”

Laura let go of his hand, and he sent James a message asking for a call at nine.

Laura slid the plate back to him. “Finish that before you start cutting. I only have so much ribbon.”

He ate the rest of the sandwich while she showed him how she wanted the ends folded. When James replied that nine was fine, Sam left the phone on the kitchen counter.

He returned to the chair at her side. She passed him the scissors, and he held the first length against the piece they were copying. One end projected half an inch beyond it.

Laura looked at the two ends and then at him.

“I’m allowing for growth,” he said.

She laughed and pulled another length from the spool. He cut his piece again and set it beside hers.
